Dimensionality reduction & PCA

Real data often has many features, and many of them repeat the same information. Dimensionality reduction squeezes data into fewer features while losing as little as possible. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is the standard method. It finds the directions in which the data varies most, using the covariance matrix and its eigenvalues. GATE Data Science and AI asks for PCA steps and small numerical eigenvalue calculations.

The idea in plain words

Imagine a photograph of a tilted coin. The picture has millions of pixels, but the coin itself is just a circle. Its shape can be described with a few numbers: where its centre is, how big it is, and how much it is tilted. A few numbers capture almost everything that matters. That is the spirit of dimensionality reduction: describe each data point with fewer numbers, while keeping the information you care about.

Suppose each person in a survey is described by height in centimetres and height in inches. These two features are almost the same information written twice. We can drop one of them and lose almost nothing. Real datasets are full of such repeats. Dimensionality reduction finds them.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is the standard method. It looks for the direction in which the data is most spread out, then the next direction at right angles to it that is most spread out, and so on. These directions are called principal components. If the data is spread out mostly along one direction, we can keep just that one.

Why does this matter for GATE? The Data Science and AI paper asks you to state the steps of PCA, compute eigenvalues of small covariance matrices, and say how much variance a component keeps. Each term is defined below, and each calculation is checked with Python.

Key terms

Feature (dimension)
One measurement in each data point. Precise: a coordinate, so d features give points in d-dimensional space.
Dimensionality reduction
Representing data with fewer features. Precise: mapping d-dimensional points to k dimensions, with k less than d.
Curse of dimensionality
Problems that grow as the number of features grows. Precise: in high dimensions, most of the volume lies far from the centre, and data becomes sparse.
Mean
The average of each feature. Precise: the sum of the values divided by the number of points.
Centring
Subtracting the mean from each feature. Precise: each feature of the centred data has mean zero.
Variance
How spread out one feature is. Precise: the average squared distance of its values from their mean.
Covariance
How two features vary together. Precise: positive if they rise together, negative if one rises as the other falls, zero if unrelated.
Covariance matrix
A table of variances and covariances. Precise: a square matrix whose entry (i, j) is the covariance of feature i and feature j.
Eigenvector
A direction that a matrix only stretches, not turns. Precise: a non-zero vector v with Cv = λv.
Eigenvalue λ (lambda)
How much an eigenvector is stretched. Precise: the number λ in Cv = λv.
Principal component
A direction of maximum remaining variance. Precise: a unit eigenvector of the covariance matrix, usually ordered by eigenvalue.
Variance explained
The share of total variance kept by a component. Precise: its eigenvalue divided by the sum of all eigenvalues.
Standardising
Rescaling each feature to a common scale. Precise: subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation.
SVD (singular value decomposition)
A way to split a matrix into rotations and stretches. Precise: any matrix X can be written as U Σ VT, and PCA can be computed from it.

Concept 1: Why fewer dimensions help, and the curse of dimensionality

Redundancy is the first reason

If two features carry the same information, keeping both wastes effort and adds noise. Removing redundant features makes models faster and often more accurate, because there are fewer chances to fit noise.

The curse of dimensionality

The second reason is harder to see. As the number of features grows, the space becomes enormous, and a fixed amount of data covers it more and more thinly. Distances between points also start to look alike, which makes "nearest" less meaningful. A useful fact to picture: take a cube, and the ball inscribed in it that touches each face. In low dimensions the ball fills much of the cube. In high dimensions the ball fills almost none of it.

Let us measure this. The code uses the formula for the volume of a ball of radius 1 in d dimensions, and compares it with the volume of the cube from −1 to 1 in each direction, which is 2d.

import math

for d in [2, 3, 10]:
    vol_ball = math.pi ** (d / 2) / math.gamma(d / 2 + 1)
    vol_cube = 2 ** d
    print("d =", d, "fraction =", round(vol_ball / vol_cube, 4))
d = 2 fraction = 0.7854
d = 3 fraction = 0.5236
d = 10 fraction = 0.0025

Read the output. In 2 dimensions, about 79 percent of the square lies inside the circle. In 3 dimensions, about 52 percent of the cube lies inside the ball. In 10 dimensions, only about 0.25 percent lies inside. Most of the volume sits near the corners, far from the centre. This is why data in high dimensions is so sparse, and why many methods need far more examples as dimensions grow.

Key ideaMore features make data sparse. Reducing dimensions keeps the data in the region where the examples actually are.

Why does the curse of dimensionality make nearest-neighbour methods harder?

In high dimensions, points are spread thinly, so the nearest neighbour of a point is often not very near. Distances become less informative, and more data is needed to find genuinely similar examples.

Does dimensionality reduction always lose information?

Usually a little, yes. The aim is to lose only the directions that carry little variance or are redundant, and to keep the rest.

Concept 2: Centring the data and the covariance matrix

Step 1: centre the data

PCA cares about spread around the middle, not about where the middle sits. So we first subtract the mean of each feature from every point. After this, each feature has mean zero. This is called centring. If the data is already centred, nothing changes.

Step 2: the covariance matrix

For centred data, the covariance of two features is the average product of their values:

cov(x, y) = (1/n) × Σ xi yi
  • n is the number of points.
  • xi and yi are the two feature values for point i, after centring.
  • The variance of x is the same formula with y replaced by x, so var(x) = cov(x, x).

Some textbooks divide by n − 1 instead of n. That scales every entry by the same factor. The eigenvectors stay the same, and the eigenvalues are multiplied by the same constant, so the choices of components do not change. This page uses 1/n.

A worked example

Take four points: (1, 0), (−1, 0), (0, 2) and (0, −2). Their mean is (0, 0), so they are already centred. Now compute each entry of the covariance matrix by hand.

  1. Variance of x: values 1, −1, 0, 0. Squares 1, 1, 0, 0. Sum 2. Divide by 4: 2/4 = 1/2.
  2. Variance of y: values 0, 0, 2, −2. Squares 0, 0, 4, 4. Sum 8. Divide by 4: 8/4 = 2.
  3. Covariance of x and y: products 1 × 0, (−1) × 0, 0 × 2, 0 × (−2) are all 0. Sum 0. Divide by 4: 0.

So the covariance matrix is [[1/2, 0], [0, 2]]. The y feature varies four times as much as the x feature, and the two are unrelated. The Python run in the next concept confirms these numbers.

TipAlways centre first. Forgetting to subtract the mean is one of the most common PCA mistakes, and it changes the covariance matrix.

What is the mean of the four points (3, 1), (5, 1), (4, 0) and (4, 2)?

x: (3 + 5 + 4 + 4)/4 = 16/4 = 4. y: (1 + 1 + 0 + 2)/4 = 4/4 = 1. The mean is (4, 1), so the centred points are (−1, 0), (1, 0), (0, −1), (0, 1).

What does a covariance of zero mean?

The two features are uncorrelated in a linear sense: knowing one tells you nothing linear about the other. A covariance of zero does not always mean full independence, but it is the case we use here.

Concept 3: Eigenvalues and eigenvectors as principal components

What an eigenvector is

Multiply a matrix C by a vector v. Usually the result points in a new direction. For special vectors, the result points in the same direction, only stretched. Those are the eigenvectors. Written out:

C v = λ v
  • C is the covariance matrix.
  • v is an eigenvector, a non-zero direction.
  • λ is the eigenvalue, the stretch factor along v.

For a covariance matrix, the eigenvectors are the principal directions and the eigenvalues are the variances along them.

Checking eigenvectors by hand

For the covariance matrix C = [[1/2, 0], [0, 2]] from Concept 2, try the vector (0, 1):

  1. C times (0, 1) = (1/2 × 0 + 0 × 1, 0 × 0 + 2 × 1) = (0, 2).
  2. (0, 2) is 2 times (0, 1), so (0, 1) is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 2.
  3. Try (1, 0): C times (1, 0) = (1/2, 0), which is 1/2 times (1, 0). So (1, 0) has eigenvalue 1/2.

The largest eigenvalue, 2, belongs to the direction (0, 1), which is the y axis. That is the direction of greatest spread, so it is the first principal component.

The formula for a 2 by 2 matrix

For a symmetric 2 by 2 matrix [[a, b], [b, d]], the eigenvalues are:

λ = (a + d)/2 ± √( ((a − d)/2)² + b² )

The sum (a + d) is the trace, the total of the diagonal. Check it on the matrix above, with a = 1/2, b = 0 and d = 2: the middle is (1/2 + 2)/2 = 5/4, and the square root is √((−3/4)² + 0) = 3/4. So λ = 5/4 ± 3/4, which gives 2 and 1/2. These match the hand check.

Now a fuller example with exact integers. Take C = [[3, 1], [1, 3]]. The Python code tests two candidate directions and computes the trace and determinant.

C = [[3, 1], [1, 3]]

def matvec(M, v):
    return [M[0][0] * v[0] + M[0][1] * v[1], M[1][0] * v[0] + M[1][1] * v[1]]

for v in ([1, 1], [1, -1]):
    Cv = matvec(C, v)
    print("C times", v, "=", Cv, "so eigenvalue", Cv[0] // v[0])
print("trace:", C[0][0] + C[1][1], "det:", C[0][0] * C[1][1] - C[0][1] * C[1][0])
C times [1, 1] = [4, 4] so eigenvalue 4
C times [1, -1] = [2, -2] so eigenvalue 2
trace: 6 det: 8

The eigenvalues are 4 and 2. The trace is 4 + 2 = 6, and the determinant is 4 × 2 = 8, which the output confirms. The eigenvector for 4 is (1, 1), and the eigenvector for 2 is (1, −1). These two directions are at right angles, which is always true for eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix such as a covariance matrix. For PCA we usually scale each eigenvector to length 1 by dividing by √2.

Key ideaThe principal components are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, ordered from the largest eigenvalue to the smallest. The eigenvalue tells you how much variance lies along that direction.

TrapDo not mix up the eigenvalue with the eigenvector. The eigenvalue is a number, the stretch factor. The eigenvector is a direction, and the first principal component is the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue.

For C = [[2, 0], [0, 5]], which direction is the first principal component?

The y axis, direction (0, 1). Its eigenvalue is 5, which is larger than the eigenvalue 2 of the x axis.

What are the eigenvalues of [[1, 0], [0, 1]]?

Both are 1. Every direction is stretched by 1, so every direction is an eigenvector, and no single direction is the principal one.

Concept 4: Variance explained and choosing the number of components

Total variance is the sum of eigenvalues

The total variance of the data is the sum of the variances of the features, which is the trace of the covariance matrix. This equals the sum of all the eigenvalues. So the share of variance kept by one component is:

Variance explained by component i = λi / (λ1 + λ2 + … + λd)
  • λi is the eigenvalue of component i.
  • Denominator is the total variance, the sum of all the eigenvalues.

Applying it to the four-point example

The covariance matrix of the four points was [[1/2, 0], [0, 2]]. Its eigenvalues are 2 and 1/2, and the total is 5/2. The first component keeps 2 / (5/2) = 4/5, which is 80 percent of the variance. The second keeps 20 percent. The Python code below computes this, then projects each point onto the first component.

The projection is the score: the dot product of the point with the unit eigenvector. Here the first component is (0, 1), the y axis.

from fractions import Fraction as F
import math

data = [(1, 0), (-1, 0), (0, 2), (0, -2)]
n = len(data)
mx = F(sum(p[0] for p in data), n)
my = F(sum(p[1] for p in data), n)
print("mean:", mx, my)
centred = [(p[0] - mx, p[1] - my) for p in data]
a = sum(p[0] * p[0] for p in centred) / n
d = sum(p[1] * p[1] for p in centred) / n
b = sum(p[0] * p[1] for p in centred) / n
print(f"covariance matrix: [[{a}, {b}], [{b}, {d}]]")
print("trace:", a + d, "determinant:", a * d - b * b)
mid = (a + d) / 2
spread = math.sqrt(((a - d) / 2) ** 2 + b * b)
lam1 = mid + spread
lam2 = mid - spread
print("eigenvalues:", lam1, lam2)
print("variance explained by PC1:", lam1 / (lam1 + lam2))
u = (0, 1)   # the eigenvector for the largest eigenvalue, found by hand
for p in data:
    score = p[0] * u[0] + p[1] * u[1]
    print("score on PC1 for", p, "=", score)
mean: 0 0
covariance matrix: [[1/2, 0], [0, 2]]
trace: 5/2 determinant: 1
eigenvalues: 2.0 0.5
variance explained by PC1: 0.8
score on PC1 for (1, 0) = 0
score on PC1 for (-1, 0) = 0
score on PC1 for (0, 2) = 2
score on PC1 for (0, -2) = -2

Read the output. The covariance matrix matches the hand calculation. The trace 5/2 equals 2 + 1/2, which is the sum of the eigenvalues. PC1 keeps 0.8, or 80 percent. The scores show that the points on the x axis all land at 0, while the points on the y axis land at 2 and −2. The one-dimensional summary keeps the information that matters most, the y spread.

How many components to keep

A common rule is to keep the smallest number of components whose cumulative variance explained reaches a target such as 90 percent. Another is to plot the eigenvalues in order, a scree plot, and look for an elbow, as in K-means. Both are rules of thumb, and the right choice depends on the task.

GATE loves thisExpect questions that ask for the variance explained by the first component. Compute the eigenvalues, add them to get the total, then divide. Always check that the eigenvalues add up to the trace.

If the eigenvalues of a 3-feature dataset are 6, 3 and 1, what share does the first component explain?

The total is 6 + 3 + 1 = 10, so the first component explains 6/10 = 60 percent.

Why does the sum of eigenvalues equal the total variance?

The eigenvalues are the variances along the principal directions, and those directions together cover all the features. Adding them recovers the sum of the feature variances, which is the trace of the covariance matrix.

Concept 5: Standardising features and the link to SVD

Why units matter

PCA maximises variance, so a feature measured in large units, such as centimetres of rainfall, can dominate one measured in small units, such as kilograms, just because its numbers are bigger. Before PCA, we often standardise each feature: subtract its mean and divide by its standard deviation. After this, every feature has variance 1, and the covariance matrix becomes a correlation matrix. Standardise whenever the features are on different scales.

The link to SVD

Stack the centred data points as the rows of a matrix X, with n rows and d columns. Then the covariance matrix is (1/n) times XᵀX. Any matrix can be split by the singular value decomposition:

X = U Σ VT
  • U and V have orthonormal columns, meaning they are rotations.
  • Σ is a diagonal matrix of singular values σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ …
  • The columns of V are the principal directions, and the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix are σi² / n.

In practice, PCA is often computed with SVD, because it is numerically stable. For GATE, the key link is that the principal components are the right singular vectors of the centred data, and the eigenvalues are the squared singular values divided by n.

TrapPCA is unsupervised. It never looks at class labels. A high-variance direction may have nothing to do with the class you want to predict, so PCA can throw away a useful direction.

Why should you standardise features with different units?

Otherwise the feature with the largest numbers dominates the variance, and PCA chooses its directions mostly because of units rather than because of real structure.

Is PCA a supervised method?

No. It uses only the inputs and never the labels. That is why it can miss directions that separate classes.

Variance explained by the first component

A covariance matrix is [[5, 2], [2, 2]]. What share of the total variance does the first principal component explain? Give a decimal rounded to two places. (NAT)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. λ1 / (λ1 + λ2), rounded.
  2. Which idea applies. Find the eigenvalues, then divide.
  3. Work it out. Trace 5 + 2 = 7, determinant 5 × 2 − 2 × 2 = 6. So λ² − 7λ + 6 = 0, which factors as (λ − 6)(λ − 1) = 0. The eigenvalues are 6 and 1. Share: 6/7, about 0.86.
  4. Check. The Python run below finds the same eigenvalues by the formula and gives the rounded share.
import math
C = [[5, 2], [2, 2]]
a, b, d = C[0][0], C[0][1], C[1][1]
mid = (a + d) / 2
spread = math.sqrt(((a - d) / 2) ** 2 + b * b)
print("eigenvalues:", mid + spread, mid - spread)
print("variance explained by PC1:", round((mid + spread) / (2 * mid), 2))
eigenvalues: 6.0 1.0
variance explained by PC1: 0.86

Answer: 0.86

Aptitude: a mixed quant and reasoning set

Aptitude rounds are timed multiple-choice or short-answer sets. They do not test deep theory. They test whether you can read a problem quickly, pick the right formula, calculate without errors, and move on. The speed comes from knowing a few formulas cold and from a habit: convert every percentage into a fraction or a multiplier before you calculate.

This unit covers the topics that appear most often: percentages, profit and loss, ratio and proportion, time and work, speed and distance, simple and compound interest, number series, direction sense, blood relations, seating arrangements and syllogisms. Work each problem on paper before you read the answer.

Percentages

A percentage is a number out of 100. "p percent of N" means p / 100 multiplied by N. So 15% of 240 is 0.15 times 240, which is 36.

The percentage change measures how far a value moved relative to where it started: (new value minus old value) divided by old value, times 100. The denominator is always the starting value, and that is the step people forget.

Successive changes are the trap of this topic. If a value rises by a% and then falls by b%, the overall effect is not a minus b. The overall multiplier is (1 + a/100) multiplied by (1 - b/100). Multiply the multipliers, then read the result as a percentage change.

TrapA 20% rise followed by a 20% fall does not return you to the start. The multiplier is 1.2 times 0.8 = 0.96, so the net result is a 4% fall.

Profit, loss, markup and discount

The cost price (CP) is what the seller paid. The selling price (SP) is what the buyer pays. If SP is greater than CP, there is a profit of SP minus CP. The profit percentage is profit divided by CP, times 100. Note that the base is CP, not SP.

The marked price (MP) is the list price. A discount is a percentage taken off MP, so SP = MP times (1 - discount/100). A markup is a percentage added to CP to get MP.

Ratio, proportion and averages

A ratio such as A : B = 3 : 5 means that for every 3 units of A there are 5 units of B. To combine two ratios that share a term, scale them so the shared term matches. If A : B = 3 : 5 and B : C = 2 : 3, rewrite the first as 6 : 10 (multiply by 2) and the second as 10 : 15 (multiply by 5). Then A : B : C = 6 : 10 : 15.

Once a ratio is in its final form, the total number of parts is the sum of the terms. Each part has the same value, so one part equals the total divided by the number of parts.

Time and work

If a person finishes a job in D days, their work rate is 1/D of the job per day. Rates add. Two workers with rates 1/12 and 1/18 together do 1/12 + 1/18 = 5/36 of the job per day, so they take 36/5 = 7.2 days.

Pipes use the same idea. A filling pipe has a positive rate, an emptying pipe has a negative rate, and the net rate decides whether the tank fills or empties.

TrapDo not average 12 and 18 to get 15 days. Rates add, times do not.

Speed, distance and time

Distance = speed times time. To convert metres per second to kilometres per hour, multiply by 18/5. So 10 m/s equals 36 km/h.

A train crossing a pole covers only its own length. A train crossing a platform covers its own length plus the platform's length. That single idea solves most train problems.

For two equal distances at different speeds a and b, the average speed is the harmonic mean, 2ab / (a + b), not the simple average (a + b) / 2. Going up at 60 km/h and back down at 40 km/h gives 2 times 60 times 40 divided by 100, which is 48 km/h.

Simple and compound interest

For principal P, rate R per year and time T years, simple interest is SI = P times R times T / 100. The interest is always calculated on the original principal.

Compound interest adds the interest to the principal each period, so the next period earns interest on the interest. With annual compounding, the amount is A = P times (1 + R/100) to the power T, and CI = A minus P.

Series, direction, blood relations, seating and syllogisms

A number series question gives a sequence and asks for the next term. Write the first differences (the gaps between neighbours). If the differences form a pattern, extend that pattern. If they do not, look at ratios, squares, cubes or two interleaved sequences.

Direction sense problems: draw a quick sketch with north up. A right turn from north faces east, from east faces south, from south faces west, and from west faces north.

Blood relations problems: draw the family tree with lines for marriage and parent-child links. Read each sentence and place the person on the tree before you answer.

Seating arrangements: number the positions from left to right (1 to 5 for five people in a row), place the fixed clues first, and then fill the remaining positions.

Syllogisms give two statements and possible conclusions. A conclusion is valid only if it must be true whenever both statements are true. To reject a conclusion, build one small example where the statements hold and the conclusion fails. That single counterexample is enough.

Six or more solved problems

Problem 1 (percentage change). A price rises by 20% and then falls by 20%. What is the net change? Start with 500. After the rise, 500 times 1.2 = 600. After the fall, 600 times 0.8 = 480. The net change is 480 minus 500 = minus 20, which is 20 divided by 500 = 4% of the original. Answer: a 4% decrease.

Problem 2 (profit percentage). A marked price is 1200. The shopkeeper gives a 15% discount. The cost price is 850. What is the profit percentage? SP = 1200 times 0.85 = 1020. Profit = 1020 minus 850 = 170. Profit percentage = 170 divided by 850 = 20%. Answer: 20% profit. Note that the base is CP.

Problem 3 (ratio). A : B = 3 : 5 and B : C = 2 : 3. Find A when A + B + C = 93. Combined ratio A : B : C = 6 : 10 : 15, which has 31 parts. Each part is 93 divided by 31 = 3. So A = 6 times 3 = 18. Check: B = 30, C = 45, and 18 + 30 + 45 = 93. Answer: A = 18.

Problem 4 (work). A finishes a job in 12 days and B in 18 days. How long together? Rates are 1/12 and 1/18. Together they do 3/36 + 2/36 = 5/36 per day. Time = 36/5 = 7.2 days, which is 7 days and 4.8 of a day. Answer: 7 1/5 days.

Problem 5 (pipes). Pipe A fills a tank in 6 hours. Pipe B empties it in 12 hours. Both are open. Net rate = 1/6 - 1/12 = 1/12 of the tank per hour, so the tank fills in 12 hours. Answer: 12 hours.

Problem 6 (trains). A 150 m train crosses a pole in 15 seconds. Its speed is 150 / 15 = 10 m/s = 36 km/h. The same train crosses a 250 m platform. Distance = 150 + 250 = 400 m, time = 400 / 10 = 40 seconds. Answer: 40 seconds.

Problem 7 (simple and compound interest). P = 5000, R = 8% per year, T = 3 years. SI = 5000 times 8 times 3 / 100 = 1200. CI with annual compounding: A = 5000 times 1.08 cubed = 5000 times 1.259712 = 6298.56, so CI = 1298.56. The difference between CI and SI is 98.56. Answer: SI 1200, CI 1298.56.

Problem 8 (number series). Find the next term of 2, 6, 12, 20, 30, ?. The differences are 4, 6, 8, 10, so the next difference is 12. The next term is 30 + 12 = 42. Another view: the terms are 1 times 2, 2 times 3, 3 times 4, 4 times 5, 5 times 6, so the next is 6 times 7 = 42. Answer: 42.

Problem 9 (direction). Ravi walks 3 km north, turns right and walks 4 km, turns right again and walks 3 km. How far is he from his start, and in which direction? After the first leg he is at (0, 3) with north as up. Turning right means east, so he moves 4 km east to (4, 3). Turning right again means south, so he moves 3 km south to (4, 0). He is 4 km east of the start. Answer: 4 km east.

Problem 10 (blood relations). A woman says, "His mother is the only daughter of my father." How is the woman related to the man? The only daughter of the woman's father is the woman herself. So the man's mother is the woman. Answer: she is his mother.

Problem 11 (seating). Five people, P, Q, R, S and T, sit in a row. P is at the left end. Q sits immediately to the right of P. R sits second to the right of Q. S sits at the right end. Positions run 1 to 5 from the left. P is 1, Q is 2, R is 4, S is 5, so T must be 3. Answer: T sits in the middle.

Problem 12 (syllogism). Statements: All A are B. Some B are C. Conclusion: Some A are C. Build a counterexample: A = {1}, B = {1, 2}, C = {2}. All A are in B, and some B (the number 2) is in C. But no A is in C. The conclusion does not follow. Answer: cannot be concluded.

Problem 13 (coding-decoding). In a code, each letter is replaced by the next letter of the alphabet. CAT becomes DBU. What does DOG become? D goes to E, O goes to P, G goes to H. Answer: EPH.

A price goes up 10% and then down 10%. Is the final price the same as the start?

No. The multiplier is 1.1 times 0.9 = 0.99, so the final price is 1% lower than the start. Successive percentage changes never cancel exactly unless the percentages are zero.

Two workers take 10 and 15 days alone. How many days together?

Rates are 1/10 and 1/15. Their sum is 3/30 + 2/30 = 5/30 = 1/6, so together they take 6 days. Averaging 10 and 15 would give the wrong answer of 12.5.

Why is the average speed for equal distances not the simple average of the two speeds?

Because the time spent at each speed is different. The slower leg takes longer, so more time is spent at the lower speed. The correct formula for equal distances is the harmonic mean, 2ab / (a + b). For 60 and 40 that is 48 km/h, not 50.

A train of length 200 m crosses a 300 m bridge at 20 m/s. How long does it take?

The train must cover its own length plus the bridge length: 200 + 300 = 500 m. At 20 m/s the time is 500 / 20 = 25 seconds.

Revision checklist: week 4

Week 4 covered backend engineering, Git, software testing, AWS and the fundamentals of system design. Before you sit the mock interview, answer each item below out loud in two or three sentences. If an answer feels vague, reread that section of the week's notes and try again. The goal is to say the idea clearly, not to recite a definition.

Each item below is a self-check. Open the answer only after you have spoken your own version.

Walk through what happens to a web request in a typical backend, from the router to the database and back.

The request reaches a load balancer or web server, which hands it to the application. The router matches the URL and method to a handler. Middleware runs first (authentication, logging, rate limits). The handler validates input, calls the business logic, and reads or writes through the data layer. The response is serialized (for example to JSON) and returned with a status code. Name the layers out loud, because interviewers listen for them.

What is the N+1 query problem, and how do select_related and prefetch_related fix it?

N+1 happens when code runs one query to fetch N parent rows and then one more query per row to fetch a related object. Fifty posts with one author query each cost 51 queries. In Django, select_related joins a ForeignKey or OneToOne relation in the same SQL query. prefetch_related runs a second query for many-to-many or reverse foreign key relations and joins the results in Python. Both reduce the count to a fixed small number.

Compare sessions and JWTs for authentication. When would you pick each?

A session stores state on the server, and the client holds only an ID in a cookie. Logout and revocation are immediate because the server deletes the session. A JWT is a signed token that carries claims, and the server checks only the signature. It needs no lookup, which suits many services, but revoking a token before it expires is hard. Pick sessions for a single web app that needs instant revocation. Pick JWTs for stateless APIs across several services, with short expiry and a refresh token.

What is the difference between git reset and git revert, and when do you use each?

git reset moves the current branch pointer to an earlier commit, which rewrites history. Use it only on commits that exist only on your machine. git revert creates a new commit that undoes an earlier commit, and it does not rewrite history. Use revert for anything already pushed to a shared branch.

Explain the difference between merge and rebase, and why you should not rebase shared branches.

Merge joins two histories with a merge commit that records both parents. Rebase replays your commits on top of a new base, which creates new commit IDs and a straight line of history. Rebasing a branch that others have already based work on forces them to reconcile two different histories of the same commits. Rebase private branches freely and avoid rewriting public history.

Describe the test pyramid, and say what belongs at each level.

Unit tests are many, fast and cover single functions or classes with dependencies replaced by doubles. Integration tests are fewer and check that components work together, for example a repository talking to a real test database. End-to-end tests are the fewest and slowest, and they drive the whole system through its user interface or public API. The shape should be a wide base of unit tests and a narrow top.

What are security groups and network ACLs in AWS, and how do they differ?

A security group is a stateful firewall attached to an instance or network interface. It allows traffic by rule, and return traffic is allowed automatically. A network ACL is a stateless firewall attached to a subnet. It has numbered allow and deny rules evaluated in order, and you must allow return traffic explicitly. Security groups are the usual first choice for controlling access between application tiers.

What does 99.9% availability mean in downtime per year, and what is 99.99%?

A year has 8,766 hours on average (365.25 days times 24). 0.1% of that is about 8.77 hours of allowed downtime per year. 0.01% of 8,766 hours is about 0.88 hours, or roughly 53 minutes. Each extra nine divides the allowed downtime by ten.

Name three rate-limiting algorithms and describe how the token bucket works.

The three common ones are fixed window, sliding window and token bucket (leaky bucket is a related variant). In a token bucket, the bucket holds up to a fixed number of tokens and refills at a steady rate. Each request takes one token. If the bucket is empty, the request is rejected or delayed. The design allows short bursts up to the bucket size while keeping the long-run rate fixed.

Resume and HR: stories, templates and frameworks

HR and behavioural rounds test whether you can describe your work clearly, own your mistakes, and explain why you want the job. The answers must come from your real experience. This unit gives you frameworks and blank templates. Fill the blanks with facts you can defend in a follow-up question. Never memorise a story you cannot back up.

The STAR method

STAR is a four-part structure for answering "tell me about a time when" questions:

  • Situation: one or two sentences of context. Who, what, and where.
  • Task: your specific responsibility. What did you have to achieve?
  • Action: what you personally did. Use "I" for your actions and "we" only for the team's shared work. This part should be the longest.
  • Result: the outcome, with a number if you have one. Add what you learned.

Aim for about two minutes per story. Interviewers stop you if you ramble, so practise saying the Result in one clear sentence.

TipKeep three or four stories ready and map each one to several questions. A story about a production incident can answer "a failure", "a conflict under pressure" and "how you handle stress".

TrapDo not say "we" for everything. If the interviewer asks what you did, you need a clear "I" answer. Also, do not claim a metric you cannot explain.

Illustrative example (a made-up story, not your own): Situation: a team's test suite took 40 minutes and blocked merges. Task: I was asked to cut the time. Action: I found that the slowest tests started a real database for each case. I moved them to a shared test database fixture and mocked the external API in unit tests. Result: the suite dropped to 12 minutes, and merges no longer waited on the queue.

Self-introduction: two minutes

A good self-introduction has three parts: present (what you do now or study), past (the experience that matters most for this role), and future (what you want next). Practise it until it fits in about two minutes, and end on the reason you are applying here.

Common HR questions and frameworks

  • Strengths. Name one strength, give a real example, and show how it helped a team or a project. Two or three strengths are enough.
  • Weaknesses. Choose a real weakness that you are actively improving. Say what you did about it and how you measure progress. Do not pick a fake weakness like "I work too hard".
  • Conflict. Describe the disagreement, your effort to understand the other view, and the agreed result. Show respect for the other person.
  • Failure. Describe what went wrong, your share of the cause, what you changed, and how you prevented a repeat.
  • I don't know. Say so directly, then explain how you would find the answer. Interviewers value honesty over a guess.
  • Where do you see yourself in five years? Describe the growth you want, linked to the role, not a title you want to hold.
An interviewer asks about a time you failed. What structure should you use?

Use STAR. Describe the situation and your task briefly. Spend most of the time on the specific mistake you made and what you did, and be honest about your share of the cause. End with the result, what you changed afterwards, and how you prevented a repeat. Avoid blaming the team.

Why does the Result part of a STAR answer matter so much?

The result proves the action worked. Without it, the story sounds like activity with no outcome. Give a number if you have one, and a clear qualitative outcome if you do not. Add what you learned, because it shows growth.

Interview questions: a timed mock

Treat this unit as a mock interview. Read each question, answer aloud in about two minutes, and only then open the model answer. Score yourself on three things: did you answer the question asked, did you give a concrete example or number, and did you name a trade-off. A model answer is a strong sample, not the only correct one.

Explain sessions and JWTs. Which would you choose for a mobile app talking to five microservices?

A session needs a lookup in shared storage on every request, which the microservices would all have to reach. A JWT is signed by the auth service and each service can verify it locally with the public key, without a central lookup. For a mobile app with five services, I would choose short-lived JWT access tokens plus a longer-lived refresh token stored securely on the device. Trade-off: revoking access immediately is harder, so keep access tokens short, and keep a denylist or token version check for critical actions like password changes.

A list page loads 100 orders and then shows each customer's name. The page makes 101 queries. What is wrong and how do you fix it?

This is the N+1 problem. The first query loads the orders, then the code runs one customer query per order. Fix it by fetching the customers in the same query with a join (select_related in Django, or an explicit JOIN in SQL), or by one extra query with IN (prefetch_related). Verify with query logging or a profiler that the count is now two or one, not 101.

How does the cache-aside pattern work, and how do you keep the cache correct after an update?

On a read, the application checks the cache first. On a miss, it reads the database, stores the result in the cache with a time-to-live, and returns it. On a write, the application updates the database and then deletes the cache key, so the next read repopulates it with fresh data. Deleting rather than updating avoids some race conditions. Trade-off: a short window of staleness is possible, which the TTL bounds.

You pushed a commit to the shared main branch that broke the build. What do you do?

Use git revert on that commit. It creates a new commit that undoes the change, so the history stays intact for everyone who already pulled. Do not use git reset or force-push to shared history. Then fix the problem properly in a new branch and re-merge. If the commit leaked a secret, revert it too, but also rotate the secret immediately, because a reverted secret is still in the history.

You have three messy local commits on a feature branch that is not yet pushed. How do you clean them up?

Run git rebase -i HEAD~3 in the editor. Mark the commits with squash or fixup to merge them, and reword the message if needed. Because the branch was never pushed, rewriting these commits is safe. Check the result with git log before you push.

Design a testing strategy for a payment endpoint.

Use the test pyramid. Unit tests cover the pricing and validation logic with no database. Integration tests run the endpoint against a real test database and check that an order row and a payment record are written correctly. Replace the external payment provider with a test double in most tests, and keep one contract test per provider to check the real API shape. Add a few end-to-end tests for the happy path and one failure path. Track coverage, but treat it as a signal, not a goal.

Users upload videos of up to 500 MB. Design the upload path on AWS.

Do not stream the file through the application servers. The server creates a presigned S3 URL (or a multipart upload plan for large files) that lets the browser upload directly to S3 for a limited time. When the upload finishes, S3 emits an event that puts a job on a queue such as SQS. Workers pick up the job, transcode the video, and store the result. This keeps application servers small and scales the upload bandwidth with S3.

Your application runs on EC2 in private subnets and must reach an RDS database. How do you set up the network?

Put the database in private subnets with no public access. Create a security group for the database that allows the PostgreSQL port only from the application's security group, not from a fixed IP. Give the application instances an IAM role for AWS API access rather than stored keys. If the instances need to reach the internet for package updates or external APIs, route their private subnet to a NAT gateway in a public subnet.

A read-heavy API is slow under load. How do you scale it?

First measure where the time goes. Then add a load balancer over several stateless application instances, so the app scales horizontally. Put a cache (for example Redis) in front of the hot reads with cache-aside and a sensible TTL. Add read replicas for the database and send read queries to them, accepting replication lag. If the responses are public, use a CDN. Estimate the numbers first: requests per second, cache hit rate, and replica capacity, so each step has a reason.

Explain the token bucket algorithm to a teammate and say when you would use it.

Each client has a bucket with a maximum capacity, say 100 tokens, that refills at a steady rate, say 10 tokens per second. Each request removes one token. If there is a token, the request passes. If not, it is rejected with HTTP 429. The long-run rate is capped at the refill rate, while short bursts up to the capacity are allowed. Use it for public APIs where you want to allow bursts but limit sustained abuse.

Three patterns appear today. Each one solves a whole family of problems, so name the pattern when you finish a problem.

  • Greedy by a sorted key. Sorting by the right key (here, the end time) lets a simple rule make the best choice at every step.
  • Constant-space marking. You can store information inside the data you already have, using a few flags, instead of allocating new arrays.

Non-overlapping Intervals (LeetCode 435)

You get a list of intervals, each written as [start, end]. Remove the fewest intervals so that the rest do not overlap. Intervals that only touch at an endpoint, such as [1, 2] and [2, 3], do not overlap. Example: intervals = [[1, 2], [2, 3], [3, 4], [1, 3]]. The answer is 1, because removing [1, 3] is enough.

How to think about it

  1. Understand. We want the number of removals, not the remaining intervals. Touching is allowed. The list can be empty.
  2. Brute force. Try every subset of intervals, keep the largest subset with no overlaps, and report the rest. With n intervals there are 2 to the power n subsets, so this is far too slow.
  3. Find the waste. The brute force keeps re-checking choices that a simple rule could decide. Ask which interval is always safe to keep.
  4. Optimise. Sort by end time. Keep the interval that ends earliest, because it leaves the most room for the rest. Whenever the next interval starts before the last kept end, it overlaps, so remove it. Its end is at least as late, so removing it is never worse. This is the greedy choice that finishes the problem.
  5. Dry run. Walk the example through the table below.

Code

def erase_overlap_intervals(intervals):
    if not intervals:
        return 0
    intervals.sort(key=lambda iv: iv[1])   # sort by end time
    removed = 0
    last_end = intervals[0][1]              # the first interval is always kept
    for start, end in intervals[1:]:
        if start < last_end:               # overlaps the kept interval
            removed += 1                    # drop it; its end is not earlier
        else:
            last_end = end                  # no overlap, keep it
    return removed

Set Matrix Zeroes (LeetCode 73)

You get an m by n matrix. If an element is 0, set its entire row and its entire column to 0. Do it in place. Example: [[1, 1, 1], [1, 0, 1], [1, 1, 1]] becomes [[1, 0, 1], [0, 0, 0], [1, 0, 1]]. Follow-up: can you use constant extra space?

How to think about it

  1. Understand. The rule applies to the original zeros only. A row that becomes zero because of this rule must not spread the zeros further, or the whole matrix would turn to zero. Remember which cells started as zero.
  2. Brute force. Copy the matrix. For each zero in the copy, zero its row and column in the real matrix. This takes O(m n) extra space and works.
  3. Find the waste. The copy is only used to record which rows and columns must be zeroed. Two boolean arrays of length m and n can record that, which is O(m + n) space.
  4. Optimise. Use the first row and first column as the marker arrays. First note whether the first row and first column contain a zero on their own, because the markers will overwrite them. Then mark: a zero at (i, j) with i and j at least 1 sets matrix[i][0] and matrix[0][j] to 0. Next, fill the inner cells from the markers. Finally, apply the first row and column using the two saved flags.
  5. Dry run. The zero at (1, 1) sets matrix[1][0] and matrix[0][1] to 0. The fill pass then zeroes row 1 and column 1. Neither the first row nor the first column held a zero at the start, so no extra work is needed there.

Code

def set_zeroes(matrix):
    m, n = len(matrix), len(matrix[0])
    first_row_zero = any(matrix[0][j] == 0 for j in range(n))
    first_col_zero = any(matrix[i][0] == 0 for i in range(m))

    # Pass 1: use the first row and column as markers for the inner cells.
    for i in range(1, m):
        for j in range(1, n):
            if matrix[i][j] == 0:
                matrix[i][0] = 0
                matrix[0][j] = 0

    # Pass 2: zero the inner cells whose row or column is marked.
    for i in range(1, m):
        for j in range(1, n):
            if matrix[i][0] == 0 or matrix[0][j] == 0:
                matrix[i][j] = 0

    # Pass 3: the first row and column, using the saved flags.
    if first_row_zero:
        for j in range(n):
            matrix[0][j] = 0
    if first_col_zero:
        for i in range(m):
            matrix[i][0] = 0

Time O(m n) · Space O(1) extra (the matrix itself holds the markers)

TrapIf you mark the cells in place without saving the first row's and first column's zero flags first, the markers you write there will look like real zeros and wipe out rows and columns that should keep their values.

Pattern to rememberWhen a problem asks for constant extra space and you have a grid, look for a row and column you can reuse as flags, and save their original state before you overwrite it.

Sudoku Solver (LeetCode 37)

You get a 9 by 9 board. Digits 1 to 9 are given, and "." marks an empty cell. Fill the empty cells so that every row, every column and every 3 by 3 box contains each digit from 1 to 9 exactly once. The puzzle has exactly one solution. Example input: the first row is 5 3 . . 7 . . . . and the rest of the board is in the code below.

How to think about it

  1. Understand. Fill every "." so that the rules hold. The board is changed in place. Each empty cell has at most nine candidate digits, and many are ruled out by its row, column and box.
  2. Brute force. Try digits in every empty cell, check the whole board after each fill, and back off when a rule breaks. With about 50 empty cells, the number of raw combinations is 9 to the power 50, which is hopeless without pruning.
  3. Find the waste. Checking the whole board every time repeats work. Each placement only affects one row, one column and one box. If we keep three sets of used digits, the check for one candidate is three set lookups.
  4. Optimise. Use backtracking. Collect the empty cells into a list. Place the first digit that is allowed, record it in the three sets, and recurse to the next empty cell. If no digit works, remove the most recent placement and try the next digit. Early failures prune huge parts of the search tree.
  5. Dry run. Start at the first empty cell (row 0, column 2). Its row has 5, 3 and 7, its column has 8 (from row 2), and its box has 5, 3, 6, 9 and 8. The digits 1, 2 and 4 are still allowed. The search tries 1 first, places it, and moves on to the next empty cell. Deeper cells may fail, and the search then undoes placements until it finds a path that completes the board.

Code

def solve_sudoku(board):
    rows = [set() for _ in range(9)]
    cols = [set() for _ in range(9)]
    boxes = [set() for _ in range(9)]
    empty = []

    for r in range(9):
        for c in range(9):
            ch = board[r][c]
            if ch == ".":
                empty.append((r, c))
            else:
                b = (r // 3) * 3 + c // 3
                rows[r].add(ch)
                cols[c].add(ch)
                boxes[b].add(ch)

    def place(k):
        if k == len(empty):
            return True                     # every empty cell is filled
        r, c = empty[k]
        b = (r // 3) * 3 + c // 3
        for d in "123456789":
            if d in rows[r] or d in cols[c] or d in boxes[b]:
                continue                    # this digit breaks a rule
            board[r][c] = d                 # choose
            rows[r].add(d)
            cols[c].add(d)
            boxes[b].add(d)
            if place(k + 1):                # explore
                return True
            board[r][c] = "."               # undo
            rows[r].remove(d)
            cols[c].remove(d)
            boxes[b].remove(d)
        return False                        # no digit works here

    place(0)
    return board

Why the naive version is too slow

A naive search fills cells in order and re-checks the whole board after every choice. Each check scans 27 units (9 rows, 9 columns, 9 boxes). The sets make one check a constant number of lookups, and they let the recursion stop as soon as a cell has no legal digit.

Pattern to rememberBacktracking is choose, explore, undo. Keep constraint sets so that testing a choice is cheap, and always undo the change before you try the next option.

Interviewers askHow do you know the search terminates? Each recursion level fills one more empty cell, so the depth is at most the number of empty cells, and the search space is finite.

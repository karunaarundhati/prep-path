Ideal voltage/current sources & dependent sources

An ideal voltage source keeps its voltage fixed and an ideal current source keeps its current fixed, whatever the rest of the circuit does. A dependent source has a value set by another quantity elsewhere in the circuit. These sources appear in almost every network problem in GATE RA and DA.

The idea in plain words

Think of a circuit as a network of pipes. Current is the water flowing through the pipes. Voltage is the pressure that pushes the water. A source is the pump or tank that supplies the push or the flow. Without a source, nothing moves.

  • An ideal voltage source is like a water tower that keeps the pressure exactly the same. You can open a big tap or a tiny tap, and the pressure stays put. Only the flow changes.
  • An ideal current source is like a pump that always pushes the same number of litres per second. If the pipe is narrow, the pressure at the pump rises to whatever is needed. The flow never changes.

A dependent source is a third kind. It is like a pump whose speed is set by a valve in a different pipe. Its output depends on something else happening in the circuit. Dependent sources are the mathematical model for amplifiers and transistors, which is why GATE asks about them so often.

Key ideaA voltage source fixes the voltage and lets the current change. A current source fixes the current and lets the voltage change. Everything on this page follows from that contrast.

Key terms

Each term has a plain meaning first, then a precise definition.

Source
Something that supplies energy to a circuit. Precisely: an element that forces a voltage or a current into the circuit.
Ideal source
A source described by a perfect rule with no internal imperfections. Precisely: the quantity it controls (voltage or current) is fixed, whatever is connected to it.
Ideal voltage source
Keeps a fixed voltage Vs across its terminals. Precisely: v = Vs for every current, and its internal resistance is zero.
Ideal current source
Pushes a fixed current Is through itself. Precisely: i = Is for every voltage, and its internal resistance is infinite.
Internal resistance
The resistance inside a source. Precisely: zero for an ideal voltage source and infinite for an ideal current source.
Short circuit
A zero-resistance path, such as a plain wire. Precisely: zero voltage across it, any current through it.
Open circuit
A break with no path for current. Precisely: zero current through it, any voltage across it.
Dependent source
A source whose value depends on another voltage or current in the circuit. Also called a controlled source.
Controlling variable
The voltage or current that sets the value of a dependent source, such as vx or ix.
VCVS, VCCS, CCVS, CCCS
The four types of dependent source. Each name says the type of the output (V or C for voltage or current) followed by the type of the controlling variable.
Gain
The number that multiplies the controlling variable: μ, gm, r or β.
Transconductance gm
The gain of a VCCS, measured in siemens (S).
Siemens (S)
The unit of conductance, the reciprocal of resistance. 1 S = 1 A/V.
Source transformation
Swapping a voltage source in series with a resistor for a current source in parallel with the same resistor.
Node voltage
The voltage of a node measured against a reference node. This is the unknown in node analysis.
KCL
Kirchhoff's Current Law. The sum of all currents at a node is zero, with leaving currents counted as positive.
KVL
Kirchhoff's Voltage Law. The voltage rises and drops around any closed loop add up to zero.

Ideal voltage sources

What it is

An ideal voltage source is a two-terminal element. It keeps a fixed voltage Vs across its terminals. The symbol is a circle with a plus and a minus sign inside. A battery is the everyday example, although a real battery is not perfectly ideal.

v = Vs for any current i

Here v is the voltage across the source and Vs is the fixed value we are given, such as 12 V. The voltage stays at 12 V whatever current flows. The current is decided by the rest of the circuit.

Why the internal resistance is zero

Internal resistance causes a voltage drop inside the source whenever current flows. That drop would make the terminal voltage move. To keep the terminal voltage fixed for any current, the internal resistance must be zero.

Worked example 1: current through a resistor

A 12 V ideal voltage source has a 4 ohm resistor connected across its terminals. How much current flows?

  1. Write what we know. Vs = 12 V and R = 4 ohm.
  2. The resistor sits directly across the source. So its voltage is 12 V.
  3. Use Ohm's law. i = v/R.
  4. Substitute. i = 12/4 = 3 A.

Change the resistor to 6 ohm. The voltage is still 12 V, because the source does not change. The current is now 12/6 = 2 A. The current went down, and the voltage did not move at all.

V = 12
for R in [4, 6]:
    i = V / R
    print("R =", R, "ohm, current =", i, "A, voltage =", V, "V")
R = 4 ohm, current = 3.0 A, voltage = 12 V
R = 6 ohm, current = 2.0 A, voltage = 12 V

Open and short circuits

Open circuit: no current flows, but the terminals still read Vs. Short circuit: a plain wire gives i = Vs/0, which is infinite in the ideal model.

TrapNever connect two ideal voltage sources of different values directly in parallel. A 5 V source and a 9 V source across the same two points force two different voltages on the same pair of points. KVL is broken and the ideal circuit has no solution.

Self-check

An ideal 9 V source feeds a 3 ohm resistor. What is the current? What happens if the resistor is changed to 1.5 ohm?

The current is 9/3 = 3 A. With 1.5 ohm, the current is 9/1.5 = 6 A. The voltage stays at 9 V in both cases.

Why must an ideal voltage source have zero internal resistance?

Any internal resistance causes a voltage drop inside the source when current flows. That would change the terminal voltage. Zero internal resistance keeps the terminal voltage fixed.

Ideal current sources

What it is

An ideal current source pushes a fixed current Is through itself. The symbol is a circle with an arrow inside. The arrow shows the direction of the push.

i = Is for any voltage v

Here i is the current through the source and Is is the fixed value, such as 2 A. The current stays at 2 A whatever voltage appears across the source. The voltage is decided by the rest of the circuit.

Worked example 2: voltage across a resistor

An ideal 2 A current source feeds a 5 ohm resistor in a simple loop. What is the voltage across the resistor?

  1. Write what we know. Is = 2 A and R = 5 ohm.
  2. The resistor carries the source current. So the current through it is 2 A.
  3. Use v = iR.
  4. Substitute. v = 2 × 5 = 10 V.

Change the resistor to 20 ohm. The current is still 2 A, but the voltage is now 2 × 20 = 40 V.

I = 2
for R in [5, 20]:
    V = I * R
    print("R =", R, "ohm, voltage =", V, "V")
R = 5 ohm, voltage = 10 V
R = 20 ohm, voltage = 40 V

Open and short circuits

Short circuit: the voltage across the source is zero and all the current goes through the wire. Open circuit: the source still insists on 2 A, so the voltage across the open terminals would grow without limit. Never leave an ideal current source open.

TrapTwo ideal current sources of different values in series are impossible for the same reason. One source wants 2 A through a wire and the other wants 3 A through the same wire.

Self-check

An ideal 4 mA current source feeds a 2.5 kilo-ohm resistor. What is the voltage?

v = iR = 0.004 × 2500 = 10 V. Milliamps times kilo-ohms gives volts directly.

Why must an ideal current source never be left open?

It forces a fixed current. With no path for that current, the voltage across the open terminals would have to be infinite. The ideal model then has no solution.

Dependent (controlled) sources

What it is

A dependent source has a value that is a multiple of another voltage or current somewhere else. That other quantity is the controlling variable. The source is also called a controlled source.

The symbol is a diamond, not a circle. A circle means independent, so its value is given. A diamond means dependent, so its value depends on the rest of the circuit. The diamond often shows the rule, such as 2ix.

The four types

There are two choices. The output can be a voltage or a current. The controlling variable can be a voltage or a current. Two choices with two options each give four types.

Worked example 3: a VCCS

A VCCS has gm = 0.5 S and controlling voltage vx = 4 V. Find the output current.

  1. Write the rule. iout = gm vx.
  2. Substitute. iout = 0.5 × 4 = 2 A.
  3. Try a negative control. If vx = −2 V, then iout = 0.5 × (−2) = −1 A. The minus sign means the current flows opposite to the arrow.
gm = 0.5
for vx in [4, -2]:
    i = gm * vx
    print("vx =", vx, "V, output current =", i, "A")
vx = 4 V, output current = 2.0 A
vx = -2 V, output current = -1.0 A

Worked example 4: a CCCS

A CCCS has β = 3 and controlling current ix = 2 mA. Find the output current.

  1. Write the rule. iout = β ix.
  2. Substitute. iout = 3 × 2 mA = 6 mA.

Because β has no unit, the output has the same unit as the controlling current.

beta = 3
ix_mA = 2
out_mA = beta * ix_mA
print("controlling current =", ix_mA, "mA")
print("output current =", out_mA, "mA")
controlling current = 2 mA
output current = 6 mA

TrapWhen finding a Thevenin or Norton resistance by switching off sources, switch off only the independent sources. Dependent sources stay in the circuit. This is a very common GATE mistake.

Self-check

A VCVS has μ = 10 and vx = 0.3 V. What is vout?

vout = 10 × 0.3 = 3 V. μ has no unit, so the output has the unit of the controlling voltage.

A CCVS has r = 50 ohm and ix = 0.02 A. What is vout?

vout = 50 × 0.02 = 1 V. Ohm times ampere gives volt, which confirms the unit.

Source transformation

The idea

From the outside, a voltage source in series with a resistor looks the same as a current source in parallel with that resistor. Source transformation lets us swap one package for the other. The inside changes, but the outside does not.

The rule

Vs in series with R ⇔ Is = Vs/R in parallel with R

The new arrow points toward the terminal where the plus sign of the voltage source was. A voltage source pushes current out of its plus terminal, so the equivalent current source must push current into that same terminal.

Worked example 5: checking the rule

A 10 V ideal source in series with a 2 ohm resistor feeds a 3 ohm load. Find the load current.

  1. Solve the original loop. The total resistance is 2 + 3 = 5 ohm. The current is 10/5 = 2 A. This flows through the load.
  2. Transform the source. Replace the 10 V source and its 2 ohm resistor with a 5 A current source (Is = 10/2) in parallel with 2 ohm.
  3. Divide the current. The 5 A splits between the parallel 2 ohm and the 3 ohm load. The load gets Iload = 5 × 2/(2 + 3) = 5 × 2/5 = 2 A.

Both circuits give exactly 2 A through the load. In step 3, the other branch's resistance (2 ohm) goes in the numerator, because the current divides in inverse proportion to resistance.

from fractions import Fraction
Vs = 10
Rs = 2
RL = 3
I_orig = Fraction(Vs, Rs + RL)
Is = Fraction(Vs, Rs)
I_load_new = Is * Fraction(Rs, Rs + RL)
print("original load current =", I_orig, "A")
print("source current after transformation =", Is, "A")
print("load current after transformation =", I_load_new, "A")
original load current = 2 A
source current after transformation = 5 A
load current after transformation = 2 A

TipDraw the new arrow carefully. A wrong direction gives the wrong sign, and you may not notice until the end of the question.

An ideal voltage source with no series resistor, or an ideal current source with no parallel resistor, cannot be transformed. The formula would need an infinite value.

TrapTransformation keeps the outside voltage and current. It does not keep the power or the branch currents inside the transformed part. If a question asks for a quantity inside the original source, go back to the original circuit.

Self-check

A 6 V source is in series with 3 ohm. What is the equivalent current source?

Is = 6/3 = 2 A in parallel with 3 ohm. The arrow points toward the plus terminal of the original source.

Why can we not transform an ideal voltage source that has no series resistor?

The formula gives Is = Vs/R, which is infinite when R = 0. An infinite value has no meaning in the ideal model.

Dependent sources in node equations

The idea

A dependent source enters node analysis like any other source. Its current is one term in KCL, and its controlling variable is rewritten in node voltages.

Choose a reference node (usually ground), write KCL at each other node, write any dependent current using its controlling variable, and rewrite that variable in node voltages. Then solve.

Worked example 6: one node with a VCCS

A 2 A ideal current source pushes current into node V1. From V1 to ground there is a 4 ohm resistor and a VCCS carrying gm V1 with gm = 1 S. Find V1.

        2 A pushes into node V1
              |
  node V1 ----+----[ 4 ohm ]----- ground
              |
              +----(VCCS, 1 S x V1)----- ground
  1. Currents leaving V1. Through the resistor: V1/4. Through the VCCS: gm V1 = 1 × V1.
  2. KCL. Current in = current out, so 2 = V1/4 + V1.
  3. Combine. V1/4 + V1 = (5/4) V1.
  4. Solve. V1 = 2 × 4/5 = 8/5 = 1.6 V.
from fractions import Fraction
I_in = 2
R = 4
g = 1
V1 = Fraction(I_in) / (Fraction(1, R) + g)
print("V1 =", V1, "V =", float(V1), "V")
print("check KCL:", Fraction(1, R) * V1 + g * V1 == I_in)
V1 = 8/5 V = 1.6 V
check KCL: True

Key ideaA dependent source adds one extra step: write its controlling variable in node voltages. Without that step you have one more unknown than equations.

Self-check

In Worked example 6, what is V1 if gm = 3 S?

2 = V1/4 + 3V1 = (13/4) V1, so V1 = 8/13, about 0.615 V. A larger gm pulls the node voltage down.

Why does ground get no KCL equation?

Ground is the reference, so its voltage is zero by definition. The other equations already determine the answer, and one more equation would make the system overdetermined.

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original problems in the GATE style, not past exam questions. Try each one on paper first.

Ideal voltage source, two resistor values

An ideal 9 V voltage source is connected to a resistor R. First R = 3 ohm, then R = 6 ohm. Find the current and the voltage across the resistor in each case.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Two currents and two voltages.
  2. Which idea applies. The source voltage is fixed at 9 V. The current follows from Ohm's law.
  3. Work it out. Case 1: the voltage is 9 V, so the current is 9/3 = 3 A. Case 2: the voltage is still 9 V, so the current is 9/6 = 1.5 A.
  4. Check. Doubling the resistance halved the current, and the voltage stayed at 9 V.

Answer: 3 A and 9 V, then 1.5 A and 9 V.

V = 9
for R in [3, 6]:
    print("R =", R, "ohm: I =", V / R, "A, V =", V, "V")
R = 3 ohm: I = 3.0 A, V = 9 V
R = 6 ohm: I = 1.5 A, V = 9 V

Ideal current source across a kilo-ohm resistor

An ideal current source supplies 4 mA into a 2.5 kilo-ohm resistor. Find the voltage across the resistor.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A voltage, from a known current and resistance.
  2. Which idea applies. The source forces 4 mA through the resistor, so v = iR.
  3. Work it out. 4 mA = 0.004 A and 2.5 kilo-ohm = 2500 ohm. So v = 0.004 × 2500 = 10 V.
  4. Check. Milliamp times kilo-ohm gives volt, so the units are right.

Answer: 10 V.

print("V =", 4 * 2500 / 1000, "V")
V = 10.0 V

Statements about ideal sources (MSQ)

Select all that apply.

(A) An ideal voltage source has zero internal resistance.
(B) An ideal current source may be left open circuited.
(C) Two ideal voltage sources of different values may not be connected directly in parallel.
(D) An ideal current source has zero internal resistance.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true.
  2. Which idea applies. Internal resistance and open or short circuit behaviour of each source type.
  3. Work it out. (A) True: a fixed voltage needs zero internal resistance. (B) False: an open circuit forces infinite voltage. (C) True: two different voltages on one pair of points is a contradiction. (D) False: a current source has infinite internal resistance.
  4. Check. Statements A and C match the defining rules. B and D are the reverse of the true facts.

Answer: (A) and (C).

Output of a CCCS (NAT)

A CCCS has gain β = 4. Its controlling current is 0.5 A, which is the current through a 2 ohm resistor. Find the output current of the CCCS, in ampere.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The output current of a current controlled current source.
  2. Which idea applies. For a CCCS, iout = β ix.
  3. Work it out. iout = 4 × 0.5 = 2 A.
  4. Check. A gain of 4 applied to half an amp gives 2 A. The 2 ohm value is a distractor, since we are told the controlling current.

Answer: 2 A.

Load current after source transformation (NAT)

A 6 V ideal voltage source is in series with a 3 ohm resistor. This pair feeds a 6 ohm load. Find the load current in ampere, as a fraction and as a decimal.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The current through the 6 ohm load.
  2. Which idea applies. The source, the series resistor and the load form one loop. The current is the voltage over the total resistance.
  3. Work it out. Total resistance = 3 + 6 = 9 ohm. Current = 6/9 = 2/3 A, about 0.667 A. Transformation check: Is = 6/3 = 2 A, and the load gets 2 × 3/(3 + 6) = 2/3 A.
  4. Check. Both methods agree.

Answer: 2/3 A, about 0.667 A.

from fractions import Fraction as F
Vs, Rs, RL = 6, 3, 6
I = F(Vs, Rs + RL)
print("load current =", I, "A =", float(I), "A")
load current = 2/3 A = 0.6666666666666666 A

Node voltage with a VCCS (NAT)

A 3 A ideal current source pushes current into node V1. From V1 to ground there is a 3 ohm resistor and a VCCS carrying gm V1 with gm = 1 S. Find V1 in volt.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The node voltage V1.
  2. Which idea applies. KCL at V1, with the VCCS current written in V1.
  3. Work it out. In: 3 A. Out: V1/3 + V1 = (4/3) V1. So V1 = 3 × 3/4 = 9/4 = 2.25 V.
  4. Check. The resistor carries 2.25/3 = 0.75 A and the VCCS carries 2.25 A. Together they make 3 A.

Answer: 9/4 V, which is 2.25 V.

from fractions import Fraction as F
V1 = F(3) / (F(1, 3) + F(1))
print("V1 =", V1, "=", float(V1), "V")
V1 = 9/4 = 2.25 V

Two-node circuit with a dependent source (NAT)

A 6 A ideal current source pushes current into node 1 from ground. Node 1 connects to ground through a 2 ohm resistor and to node 2 through a 4 ohm resistor. Node 2 has only a VCCS to ground, carrying current 0.5 V1 from node 2 to ground, where V1 is the voltage of node 1. Find V2 in volt.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The voltage of node 2.
  2. Which idea applies. KCL at node 2 and at node 1, with the VCCS written in V1.
  3. Work it out. KCL at node 2: (V1 − V2)/4 = 0.5 V1, so V1 − V2 = 2 V1, which gives V2 = −V1. KCL at node 1: 6 = V1/2 + (V1 − V2)/4. Substitute V2 = −V1: 6 = V1/2 + V1/2 = V1. So V1 = 6 V and V2 = −6 V.
  4. Check. At node 1: 3 A through the 2 ohm and 3 A through the 4 ohm, total 6 A. At node 2: 3 A in from the 4 ohm, and the VCCS takes 0.5 × 6 = 3 A out.

Answer: V2 = −6 V.

from fractions import Fraction as F
V1 = F(6) / (F(1, 2) + F(1, 4) * 2)
V2 = -V1
print("V1 =", V1, "V")
print("V2 =", V2, "V")
print("node 1 check:", F(1, 2) * V1 + F(1, 4) * (V1 - V2) == 6)
print("node 2 check:", F(1, 4) * (V1 - V2) == F(1, 2) * V1)
V1 = 6 V
V2 = -6 V
node 1 check: True
node 2 check: True

Statements about dependent sources (MSQ)

Select all that apply.

(A) When finding a Thevenin resistance, dependent sources are switched off along with the independent sources.
(B) The output of a VCCS depends on a controlling voltage somewhere in the circuit.
(C) Transforming a voltage source into a current source keeps the current inside the original source unchanged.
(D) The gain β of a CCCS has no unit.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true. Some are traps.
  2. Which idea applies. Thevenin resistance, the definition of a VCCS, what transformation keeps, and gain units.
  3. Work it out. (A) False: dependent sources stay in the circuit. (B) True: that is the definition. (C) False: only the outside behaviour is kept. (D) True: current divided by current has no unit.
  4. Check. A and C are the two classic traps.

Answer: (B) and (D).

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Practical source conversion (NAT, 2 marks)

A practical voltage source of 12 V with internal resistance 3 Ω is converted to a current source. Find the source current, and the power delivered to a 6 Ω load.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Norton current and load power.
  2. Which idea applies. IN = V/R and the internal resistance stays the same, now in parallel.
  3. Work it out. IN = 4 A. Load current = 12/9 = 4/3 A, so the load power is (4/3)² · 6 = 10.67 W.
  4. Check. The current divider gives 4 · 3/9 = 4/3 A, the same.
I = 12 / 9
print("%.2f W" % (I * I * 6))

10.67 W

Answer: 4 A, and 10.67 W in the load.

Exam-level 2: Dependent source circuit (NAT, 2 marks)

A 10 V source drives a series loop with 2 Ω and a CCVS of value 3i (positive terminal opposing the current), where i is the loop current. Find i.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The loop current with a dependent voltage source.
  2. Which idea applies. KVL: 10 = 2i + 3i.
  3. Work it out. i = 10/5 = 2 A.
  4. Check. The dependent source behaves like a 3 Ω resistor here.

Answer: 2 A.

Exam-level 3: Source facts (MSQ, 2 marks)

Which are TRUE? (A) An ideal voltage source has zero internal resistance. (B) An ideal current source has infinite internal resistance. (C) Two ideal voltage sources of different values may be put in parallel. (D) Dependent sources must not be turned off in superposition.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Rules for sources.
  2. Which idea applies. Ideal sources impose one quantity regardless of load.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) True. (C) That violates KVL. False. (D) True.
  4. Check. Similarly, unequal ideal current sources cannot be in series.

Answer: (A), (B) and (D).

Summary

Ideal voltage source
Keeps a fixed voltage whatever current flows. Internal resistance is zero.
Ideal current source
Keeps a fixed current whatever voltage appears. Internal resistance is infinite.
Dependent source
A source whose value is a multiple of a controlling variable elsewhere.
Controlling variable
The voltage or current that sets the value of a dependent source.
VCVS
Voltage output set by a controlling voltage. Gain μ has no unit.
VCCS
Current output set by a controlling voltage. Gain gm is in siemens.
CCVS
Voltage output set by a controlling current. Gain r is in ohms.
CCCS
Current output set by a controlling current. Gain β has no unit.
Source transformation
Swapping Vs in series with R for Is = Vs/R in parallel with R, keeping the outside behaviour.
Transformation direction
The new current arrow points toward the plus terminal of the original source.
Node analysis
Writing KCL at each non-reference node, with node voltages as unknowns.
Reference node
The zero-volt node, usually ground. It gets no KCL equation.
KCL
Currents entering a node equal currents leaving it.
Current divider
Splits a current between parallel branches in inverse proportion to resistance.
Thevenin resistance
Found by switching off independent sources only.

Remember these

  1. A voltage source fixes the voltage. A current source fixes the current.
  2. A circle means independent. A diamond means dependent.
  3. Dependent sources are not switched off when finding a Thevenin resistance.
  4. Source transformation keeps the outside behaviour, not the inside.
  5. In node analysis, write the dependent current in node voltages.
  6. Never short an ideal voltage source or open an ideal current source.

Gradient, divergence and curl

The gradient, divergence and curl are three ways of asking how a field changes from place to place. The gradient points uphill, the divergence measures how much stuff spreads out, and the curl measures how much a field swirls. They appear in GATE RA questions on fluid flow, potential fields and robot force control, and in GATE DA questions on optimization.

The idea in plain words

Picture the temperature in a room. At every point there is one number, the temperature. That is a scalar field: one number attached to each point in space. Now picture the wind in the same room. At every point there is an arrow that says where the air moves and how fast. That is a vector field: an arrow attached to each point.

Three operations ask useful questions about these fields.

  • Gradient is for a scalar field. It asks: in which direction does the temperature rise fastest, and how fast? The answer is an arrow that points uphill.
  • Divergence is for a vector field. It asks: is more air flowing out of a tiny box than flowing in? A positive answer means the point is a source, like a fan. A negative answer means a sink, like a drain.
  • Curl is also for a vector field. It asks: does the air swirl around a tiny spot? A large curl means a small whirlpool or spinning motion at that point.

All three are written with the symbol ∇ (called "nabla" or "del"). It acts like a vector of derivatives: (∂/∂x, ∂/∂y, ∂/∂z). The rules below follow from that picture.

Key ideaGradient turns a scalar into a vector. Divergence and curl turn a vector field into a scalar (divergence) or a vector (curl). The three answer three different questions about the same field.

Key terms

Scalar field
A rule that gives one number at every point in space, such as temperature f(x,y,z).
Vector field
A rule that gives an arrow at every point, F = (P, Q, R), where P, Q and R are functions of x, y and z.
Partial derivative
The rate of change in one direction only, written ∂f/∂x, with the other variables held fixed.
Gradient
∇f = (∂f/∂x, ∂f/∂y, ∂f/∂z). A vector that points in the direction of steepest increase.
Directional derivative
The rate of change of f in a chosen direction u, equal to ∇f · u when u has length 1.
Divergence
∇ · F = ∂P/∂x + ∂Q/∂y + ∂R/∂z. A scalar. Positive means a source, negative means a sink.
Curl
∇ × F, a vector whose direction is the axis of rotation and whose size measures how fast the field spins.
Source and sink
A point where the field spreads out (source) or gathers in (sink). Divergence measures this.
Irrotational field
A field with zero curl everywhere. It does not swirl.
Solenoidal (incompressible) field
A field with zero divergence everywhere. Nothing is created or destroyed.
Laplacian
∇²f = ∇ · ∇f = ∂²f/∂x² + ∂²f/∂y² + ∂²f/∂z². It measures how much f differs from its average around a point.

Step 1: The gradient of a scalar field

What it is

Suppose the height of a hill is f(x,y). The partial derivatives tell you the slope in the east direction and the slope in the north direction. The gradient packs both slopes into one arrow:

∇f = (∂f/∂x, ∂f/∂y) (in three dimensions, add ∂f/∂z as a third component)

This arrow points in the direction in which the height increases fastest. Its length is the steepest slope. Walking in the direction of the gradient takes you uphill as quickly as possible. Walking perpendicular to it keeps you at the same height, along a contour line.

Worked example: every step

Let f(x,y,z) = x2y + z. Find ∇f at the point (1, 2, 0), and the steepest rate of increase there.

  1. Partial with respect to x. Treat y and z as constants. x2y gives 2xy. z gives 0. So ∂f/∂x = 2xy.
  2. Partial with respect to y. Treat x and z as constants. x2y gives x2. So ∂f/∂y = x2.
  3. Partial with respect to z. z gives 1. So ∂f/∂z = 1.
  4. Gradient. ∇f = (2xy, x2, 1).
  5. Evaluate at (1, 2, 0). 2×1×2 = 4, 12 = 1, and 1. So ∇f = (4, 1, 1).
  6. Steepest rate. The length is √(42 + 12 + 12) = √18 = 3√2, about 4.243.

Directional derivative

The rate of change in a direction u (a unit vector, length 1) is the dot product:

Du f = ∇f · u

The dot product is largest when u points the same way as ∇f, which is why the gradient is the steepest direction. It is zero when u is perpendicular to ∇f, which is why the gradient is perpendicular to contour lines.

TrapThe gradient needs a unit vector for the directional derivative. If the direction is (3, 0, 4), divide by its length 5 first. Using the unnormalised vector gives a number that is too big by a factor of 5.

Question: At a local maximum of f, what is ∇f?

It is the zero vector. At a maximum (or minimum or saddle) every partial derivative is zero, so the gradient vanishes. This matches the critical point idea from calculus of several variables.

Question: Which direction does the gradient point compared with the contour lines of f?

The gradient is perpendicular to the contour lines. Moving along a contour keeps f constant, so the directional derivative along it is zero.

Step 2: Divergence of a vector field

The idea of spreading out

Imagine a tiny cube of space filled with water that flows through it. Water enters through some faces and leaves through others. If more water leaves than enters, the cube is a source of water: the field spreads out from it. If more enters than leaves, the cube is a sink. Divergence measures this imbalance per unit volume, for a cube shrunk to a point.

∇ · F = ∂P/∂x + ∂Q/∂y + ∂R/∂z, where F = (P, Q, R)

Notice the answer is a single number, a scalar. Each term differentiates one component in its own direction. You add the three results.

Worked example: every step

Let F = (x2, y2, z2). Find the divergence at (1, 2, 3).

  1. Identify the components. P = x2, Q = y2, R = z2.
  2. Differentiate each in its own direction. ∂P/∂x = 2x, ∂Q/∂y = 2y, ∂R/∂z = 2z.
  3. Add. ∇ · F = 2x + 2y + 2z.
  4. Evaluate at (1, 2, 3). 2×1 + 2×2 + 2×3 = 2 + 4 + 6 = 12.

So the field spreads out strongly at (1, 2, 3). The divergence is positive, so that point is a source.

A field with no sources

The field F = (-y, x, 0) has P = -y, Q = x and R = 0. Then ∂P/∂x = 0, ∂Q/∂y = 0 and ∂R/∂z = 0. So its divergence is 0 everywhere. Nothing is created or destroyed. This is the mathematical picture of an incompressible fluid.

GATE loves thisA question that gives a field and asks "is the fluid incompressible?" is asking whether the divergence is zero. Compute only the three diagonal partial derivatives and add them.

TrapDivergence uses only the matching pairs: P with x, Q with y, R with z. A term like ∂P/∂y does not belong to the divergence. It appears in the curl instead.

Question: What is the divergence of F = (x, y, z), the position vector?

∂x/∂x + ∂y/∂y + ∂z/∂z = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3. The field points straight outward everywhere, so it is a source of constant strength 3.

Question: Is a field with divergence 0 everywhere always the zero field?

No. The field (-y, x, 0) has zero divergence but is not zero. Divergence zero means no net spreading. It does not mean no motion. The curl can still be nonzero.

Step 3: Curl of a vector field

The idea of spinning

Place a tiny paddle wheel in a flowing river. If the water spins the wheel, the flow has curl at that spot. The curl is a vector. Its direction is the axis around which the water spins (by the right-hand rule), and its size tells you how fast the spin is.

∇ × F = ( ∂R/∂y - ∂Q/∂z , ∂P/∂z - ∂R/∂x , ∂Q/∂x - ∂P/∂y )

Each component compares two partial derivatives of the two components that are perpendicular to its axis. The first component uses the y and z parts, the second uses the z and x parts, and the third uses the x and y parts. The pattern is cyclic, as in the cross product.

Worked example: a rotating field

Let F = (-y, x, 0). Find its curl.

  1. Identify the components. P = -y, Q = x, R = 0.
  2. First component. ∂R/∂y - ∂Q/∂z = 0 - 0 = 0.
  3. Second component. ∂P/∂z - ∂R/∂x = 0 - 0 = 0.
  4. Third component. ∂Q/∂x - ∂P/∂y = 1 - (-1) = 2.
  5. Result. ∇ × F = (0, 0, 2). The field spins counterclockwise around the z-axis, with rate 2.

Worked example: a mixed field

Let F = (xy, yz, zx). Find the curl at (1, 2, 3).

  1. Components. P = xy, Q = yz, R = zx.
  2. Needed derivatives. ∂R/∂y = 0, ∂Q/∂z = y, ∂P/∂z = 0, ∂R/∂x = z, ∂Q/∂x = 0, ∂P/∂y = x.
  3. Components. First: 0 - y = -y. Second: 0 - z = -z. Third: 0 - x = -x.
  4. Result. ∇ × F = (-y, -z, -x). At (1, 2, 3) this is (-2, -3, -1).

TrapThe curl has a minus sign in each component that comes from the second term. Write each component as a difference of two derivatives, then subtract them in exactly the order the formula gives, the second derivative from the first.

Question: What is the curl of the gradient of any scalar f?

It is the zero vector. The mixed second partial derivatives are equal (∂²f/∂x∂y = ∂²f/∂y∂x), so each subtraction cancels. A gradient field never swirls.

Question: The curl of F = (0, 0, z) is what?

All partial derivatives of P and Q are zero, and R depends only on z. The first component is ∂R/∂y - ∂Q/∂z = 0, the second is ∂P/∂z - ∂R/∂x = 0, and the third is ∂Q/∂x - ∂P/∂y = 0. So the curl is zero.

Step 4: The Laplacian and the key identities

The Laplacian

The Laplacian is the divergence of the gradient. It adds the second derivatives in all directions:

∇²f = ∇ · (∇f) = ∂²f/∂x² + ∂²f/∂y² + ∂²f/∂z²

Think of a function that is a bowl. Its Laplacian is positive, because the bowl curves up on every side. A hill has a negative Laplacian. A function with zero Laplacian is called harmonic. Its value at a point equals the average of its values around that point, so it has no peaks or valleys inside a region.

Worked example: a Laplacian

Let f = x2 + y2 + z2. Find ∇²f.

  1. Second derivative in x. ∂f/∂x = 2x, so ∂²f/∂x² = 2.
  2. Second derivative in y. Similarly ∂²f/∂y² = 2.
  3. Second derivative in z. Similarly ∂²f/∂z² = 2.
  4. Add. ∇²f = 2 + 2 + 2 = 6, the same at every point.

The identities

Two identities hold for all smooth fields. They are worth memorising because GATE uses them to skip work.

∇ × (∇f) = 0 and ∇ · (∇ × F) = 0

The first says a gradient never has curl. The second says the divergence of any curl is zero. Both come from the fact that mixed second partial derivatives are equal, so the terms cancel in pairs.

Worked check of an identity

Take F = (xy, yz, zx) from Step 3. Its curl is (-y, -z, -x). The divergence of that curl is ∂(-y)/∂x + ∂(-z)/∂y + ∂(-x)/∂z = 0 + 0 + 0 = 0, as the identity says.

# Numerical check with central differences (h small, so the error is tiny)
def partial(f, point, i, h=1e-5):
    p1 = list(point)
    p2 = list(point)
    p1[i] += h
    p2[i] -= h
    return (f(*p1) - f(*p2)) / (2 * h)

def grad(f, p):
    return tuple(round(partial(f, p, i), 4) + 0.0 for i in range(3))

# f = x^2 y + z at the point (1, 2, 0)
f = lambda x, y, z: x*x*y + z
print("grad f =", grad(f, (1.0, 2.0, 0.0)))

# F = (xy, yz, zx) at the point (1, 2, 3)
def comp(i):
    return lambda x, y, z: (x*y, y*z, z*x)[i]

def div(p):
    return round(sum(partial(comp(i), p, i) for i in range(3)), 4) + 0.0

def curl(p):
    P, Q, R = comp(0), comp(1), comp(2)
    c1 = partial(R, p, 1) - partial(Q, p, 2)
    c2 = partial(P, p, 2) - partial(R, p, 0)
    c3 = partial(Q, p, 0) - partial(P, p, 1)
    return tuple(round(c, 4) + 0.0 for c in (c1, c2, c3))

p = (1.0, 2.0, 3.0)
print("div F =", div(p))
print("curl F =", curl(p))
grad f = (4.0, 1.0, 1.0)
div F = 6.0
curl F = (-2.0, -3.0, -1.0)

Key ideaWhen an expression looks heavy, check whether it is a curl of a gradient or a divergence of a curl. If it is, the answer is zero without any further work.

Question: What is the Laplacian of f = x2 - y2?

fxx = 2 and fyy = -2, so ∇²f = 2 - 2 = 0. The function is harmonic.

Question: Why is ∇ · (∇ × F) always zero?

Write out the divergence of the curl. Each term is a mixed second partial derivative, such as ∂²R/∂x∂y, paired with its opposite such as ∂²Q/∂x∂z in a different order. The pairs cancel because the order of differentiation does not matter for smooth functions.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original GATE-style questions written for practice. They are not past exam questions.

Gradient at a point

Find ∇f at the point (1, 1) for f(x,y) = x2 + 3y2.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A vector of two partial derivatives, evaluated at (1, 1).
  2. Which idea applies. The gradient is (∂f/∂x, ∂f/∂y).
  3. Work it out. ∂f/∂x = 2x, which is 2 at x = 1. ∂f/∂y = 6y, which is 6 at y = 1. So ∇f = (2, 6).
  4. Check. The steeper direction is y, because the coefficient 3 on y2 makes the function rise faster in y. The second component, 6, is larger than the first, 2, which agrees.

Answer: ∇f = (2, 6).

Which facts about the position field?

Let F = (x, y, z) be the position vector. Which of the following are true? Select all that apply.

(A) The divergence of F is 3. (B) The curl of F is the zero vector. (C) The divergence of F is 0. (D) The curl of F is (1, 1, 1).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Test each statement.
  2. Which idea applies. Divergence adds the three diagonal partial derivatives. Curl compares off-diagonal partial derivatives.
  3. Work it out. ∇ · F = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3, so (A) is true and (C) is false. Every off-diagonal derivative of F is zero, since P = x depends only on x, and so on. So ∇ × F = 0, making (B) true and (D) false.
  4. Check. The position field points straight outward and does not swirl, which matches a divergence of 3 and a curl of 0.

Answer: (A) and (B).

Rate of change in a direction

Let f = x2y + z. Find the rate of change of f at the point (1, 2, 0) in the direction of the vector (3, 0, 4). Give the answer as a decimal (NAT style).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A directional derivative, so the direction must be a unit vector.
  2. Which idea applies. Du f = ∇f · u, with u of length 1.
  3. Work it out. The gradient at (1, 2, 0) is (4, 1, 1), from the worked example. The length of (3, 0, 4) is 5, so u = (3/5, 0, 4/5). Then ∇f · u = 4×3/5 + 1×0 + 1×4/5 = 12/5 + 4/5 = 16/5 = 3.2.
  4. Check. The gradient's length is about 4.243, which must be at least as large as any directional derivative. Here 3.2 is smaller, which is consistent.

Answer: 3.2.

Divergence with three terms

Let F = (x2y, yz2, xz). Find ∇ · F at (1, 1, 1) (NAT style).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A single number, the divergence at one point.
  2. Which idea applies. Add ∂P/∂x, ∂Q/∂y and ∂R/∂z.
  3. Work it out. ∂(x2y)/∂x = 2xy. ∂(yz2)/∂y = z2. ∂(xz)/∂z = x. So ∇ · F = 2xy + z2 + x. At (1, 1, 1) this is 2 + 1 + 1 = 4.
  4. Check. Each term is an independent part of the total. Recomputing each at (1, 1, 1) gives 2, 1 and 1, which sum to 4.

Answer: 4.

Curl and divergence of a shear field

Let F = (y2, x2, 0). Which of the following are true? Select all that apply.

(A) The curl of F is zero at (1, 1). (B) The z-component of the curl at (2, 1) is 2. (C) The x-component of the curl is nonzero. (D) The divergence of F is zero everywhere.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Test each statement.
  2. Which idea applies. Compute the curl and the divergence.
  3. Work it out. With P = y2, Q = x2 and R = 0, the curl is (0, 0, ∂Q/∂x - ∂P/∂y) = (0, 0, 2x - 2y). At (1, 1) the z-component is 0, so (A) is true. At (2, 1) it is 4 - 2 = 2, so (B) is true. The x-component is 0, so (C) is false. The divergence is ∂(y2)/∂x + ∂(x2)/∂y + 0 = 0 + 0 + 0 = 0, so (D) is true.
  4. Check. The curl is only in the z-direction, which is consistent with a field that moves in the xy-plane.

Answer: (A), (B) and (D).

Curl magnitude at a point

Let F = (x2y, y2z, z2x). Which of the following are true at the point (1, 1, 1)? Select all that apply.

(A) The curl of F is (-1, -1, -1). (B) The divergence of the curl of F is zero. (C) The magnitude of the curl is 3. (D) The divergence of F is 3.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Test four claims about the curl and divergence.
  2. Which idea applies. Compute the curl, then use the identity ∇ · (∇ × F) = 0.
  3. Work it out. P = x2y, Q = y2z, R = z2x. Then ∂R/∂y = 0, ∂Q/∂z = y2, so the first component is -y2. Similarly the second is -z2 and the third is -x2. So the curl is (-y2, -z2, -x2), which at (1, 1, 1) is (-1, -1, -1). So (A) is true. The divergence of any curl is zero, so (B) is true. The magnitude is √3, about 1.732, not 3, so (C) is false. The divergence of F is 2xy + 2yz + 2zx = 6 at (1, 1, 1), so (D) is false.
  4. Check. The curl components are each the negative square of one variable, which is consistent with the calculation at (1, 1, 1).

Answer: (A) and (B).

Laplacian of a mixed polynomial

Let f(x,y,z) = x2y + yz2. Find ∇²f at the point (1, 2, 3) (NAT style).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The sum of the three pure second derivatives, at one point.
  2. Which idea applies. ∇²f = fxx + fyy + fzz.
  3. Work it out. fx = 2xy, so fxx = 2y. fy = x2 + z2, so fyy = 0. fz = 2yz, so fzz = 2y. So ∇²f = 2y + 0 + 2y = 4y. At y = 2 this is 8.
  4. Check. Compute at (1, 2, 3) directly: fxx = 2×2 = 4 and fzz = 4, so the sum is 8, which agrees.

Answer: 8.

Fluid that spins but does not compress

A fluid has velocity v = (-y, x, 0). What is the divergence of v, and what is the z-component of its curl? Give the z-component of the curl as a number (NAT style).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Two results. The question asks for the curl's z-component as the number.
  2. Which idea applies. Divergence adds diagonal partial derivatives. Curl uses off-diagonal ones.
  3. Work it out. Divergence: ∂(-y)/∂x + ∂x/∂y + ∂0/∂z = 0 + 0 + 0 = 0, so the fluid is incompressible. Curl z-component: ∂Q/∂x - ∂P/∂y = ∂x/∂x - ∂(-y)/∂y = 1 - (-1) = 2.
  4. Check. The field is a rigid rotation: a point at distance r from the axis moves with speed r around it. The angular speed is ω = 1, and the curl equals 2ω = 2, which matches.

Answer: the divergence is 0, and the z-component of the curl is 2.

Exam-level 1: divergence of an air-flow field (NAT, 2 marks)

The air flow in a room is described by the vector field F(x,y,z) = (x2y, yz, xz2). The divergence of F measures how much air flows out of a tiny box around a point. Find the divergence of F at the point (1, 2, 3). Give the number. The Python check uses a small finite difference, which is a numerical way to estimate partial derivatives.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The value of the divergence at (1, 2, 3).
  2. Which idea applies. The divergence is the sum of the three partial derivatives: the first component with respect to x, the second with respect to y and the third with respect to z.
  3. Work it out. The partial derivative of x2y with respect to x is 2xy. The partial derivative of yz with respect to y is z. The partial derivative of xz2 with respect to z is 2xz. So div F = 2xy + z + 2xz. At (1, 2, 3) this is 2(1)(2) + 3 + 2(1)(3) = 4 + 3 + 6 = 13.
  4. Check. The Python check estimates each partial derivative with a central difference over a tiny step, then adds them.
def F(x, y, z):
    return (x*x*y, y*z, x*z*z)

def div_F(x, y, z, h=1e-5):
    dFx = (F(x + h, y, z)[0] - F(x - h, y, z)[0]) / (2*h)
    dFy = (F(x, y + h, z)[1] - F(x, y - h, z)[1]) / (2*h)
    dFz = (F(x, y, z + h)[2] - F(x, y, z - h)[2]) / (2*h)
    return dFx + dFy + dFz

print("div F at (1, 2, 3) about", round(div_F(1, 2, 3), 3))
div F at (1, 2, 3) about 13.0

Answer: 13.

Exam-level 2: steepest rise of a temperature field (NAT, 2 marks)

A temperature field is T(x,y,z) = xy2 + z2, measured in degrees. A robot stands at the point (1, 2, 1) and wants to move in the direction u = (1, 2, 2), which is not yet of unit length. Find the magnitude of the gradient of T at (1, 2, 1). Then find the rate of change of T in the direction of u per unit length, which is the directional derivative. Give the directional derivative to two decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The gradient magnitude, and the rate of change of T along a direction.
  2. Which idea applies. The gradient is the vector of partial derivatives. The directional derivative is the dot product of the gradient with the unit vector in the direction of travel.
  3. Work it out. The gradient is (y2, 2xy, 2z). At (1, 2, 1) it is (4, 4, 2), with magnitude sqrt(16 + 16 + 4) = sqrt(36) = 6. The length of u is sqrt(1 + 4 + 4) = 3, so the unit vector is (1/3, 2/3, 2/3). The directional derivative is (4)(1/3) + (4)(2/3) + (2)(2/3) = 16/3, which is about 5.33.
  4. Check. The Python check estimates the gradient numerically, then computes its magnitude and the directional derivative.
import math

def f(x, y, z):
    return x * y * y + z * z

h = 1e-6
p = (1, 2, 1)
g = []
for k in range(3):
    up = list(p)
    dn = list(p)
    up[k] += h
    dn[k] -= h
    g.append((f(*up) - f(*dn)) / (2 * h))
print("gradient:", tuple(round(v, 3) for v in g))
print("magnitude:", round(math.sqrt(sum(v * v for v in g)), 3))
u = (1/3, 2/3, 2/3)
print("directional derivative:", round(sum(gi * ui for gi, ui in zip(g, u)), 3))
gradient: (4.0, 4.0, 2.0)
magnitude: 6.0
directional derivative: 5.333

Answer: 5.33 (degrees per unit length).

Exam-level 3: rotating flow and its curl (MSQ, 2 marks)

A planar rotating flow is F(x,y,z) = (-y, x, 0). Its divergence and curl are computed with partial derivatives, and the curl is a vector that measures local rotation. Which of the following statements are true? Select all that apply.

(A) The divergence of F is zero everywhere. (B) The curl of F is (0, 0, 2) everywhere. (C) F is a conservative field, because its curl is zero. (D) The divergence of the curl of F is zero.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which of the four statements are true for this field.
  2. Which idea applies. Divergence is the sum of the diagonal partial derivatives. Curl is the vector of the off-diagonal differences. A field with zero curl is conservative.
  3. Work it out. Divergence: the partial derivatives of -y with respect to x, of x with respect to y, and of 0 with respect to z are all zero, so div F = 0. Curl: the z-component is (partial of x with respect to x) - (partial of -y with respect to y) = 1 - (-1) = 2, and the other components are zero, so curl F = (0, 0, 2). Because the curl is not zero, F is not conservative. The curl is constant, so its divergence is zero.
  4. Check. The Python check estimates the divergence and curl with central differences at one point.
def F(x, y, z):
    return (-y, x, 0.0)

def d(i, j, p, h=1e-6):
    # partial derivative of component i with respect to coordinate j
    up = list(p)
    dn = list(p)
    up[j] += h
    dn[j] -= h
    return (F(*up)[i] - F(*dn)[i]) / (2 * h)

Exam-level 4: Laplacian of a potential (NAT, 2 marks)

The Laplacian of a scalar field f is the sum of its three second partial derivatives: the Laplacian of f equals the second partial with respect to x, plus the second partial with respect to y, plus the second partial with respect to z. It appears in heat flow and in potential fields. For f(x,y,z) = x2y2 + z3, find the Laplacian at the point (1, 2, 1). Compute each second derivative separately, add them, and give the number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The value of the Laplacian of f at (1, 2, 1).
  2. Which idea applies. The Laplacian is the sum of the three pure second partial derivatives.
  3. Work it out. The second partial with respect to x of x2y2 is 2y2, which is 8 at y = 2. The second partial with respect to y is 2x2, which is 2 at x = 1. The second partial with respect to z of z3 is 6z, which is 6 at z = 1. The total is 8 + 2 + 6 = 16.
  4. Check. The Python check estimates each second derivative with a finite difference and adds them.
def f(x, y, z):
    return x * x * y * y + z ** 3

h = 1e-4
p = (1, 2, 1)
lap = 0.0
for k in range(3):
    up = list(p)
    dn = list(p)
    up[k] += h
    dn[k] -= h
    lap += (f(*up) - 2 * f(*p) + f(*dn)) / h ** 2
print("laplacian about", round(lap, 3))
laplacian about 16.0

Answer: 16.

Summary

Scalar field
One number at every point, such as temperature.
Vector field
An arrow at every point, such as wind velocity.
Gradient
A vector of partial derivatives pointing in the steepest uphill direction.
Directional derivative
∇f · u, with u a unit vector. The rate of change in direction u.
Divergence
A scalar, the sum of the diagonal partial derivatives. Measures spreading out.
Source
A point with positive divergence, where the field spreads out.
Sink
A point with negative divergence, where the field gathers in.
Incompressible field
A field with zero divergence everywhere.
Curl
A vector that measures spin. Its direction is the axis of rotation.
Irrotational field
A field with zero curl everywhere.
Laplacian
The divergence of the gradient, the sum of the three pure second derivatives.
Harmonic function
A function whose Laplacian is zero.
Identity: curl of a gradient
∇ × (∇f) = 0 for any smooth f.
Identity: divergence of a curl
∇ · (∇ × F) = 0 for any smooth F.

Remember these

  • Gradient gives a vector and points uphill. It is perpendicular to contour lines.
  • Divergence gives a number. Positive is a source, negative is a sink.
  • Curl gives a vector. Its direction is the axis of spin.
  • For the directional derivative, the direction must have length 1.
  • The curl of a gradient is always zero, and the divergence of a curl is always zero.
  • Check each term of the divergence against its own variable.

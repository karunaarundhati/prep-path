Thin cylinders

A pressure vessel such as a gas cylinder, a boiler drum or a pipe carries an inside pressure that pushes the walls outward. A thin-walled cylinder or sphere has simple formulas for the stress in its wall, and GATE Robotics and Automation questions often ask for the hoop stress, the longitudinal stress or the change in size under pressure.

The idea in plain words

Welcome. Have you ever blown up a balloon, or pumped a bicycle tyre? In both cases, the air inside pushes outward on the rubber in every direction. The rubber stretches, and the stretch is what holds the air in. A pressure vessel is the same idea with stronger material: a tank, a pipe, a boiler drum or a gas cylinder that holds fluid at a pressure higher than the air outside. Its wall must hold the pressure without breaking or swelling too much.

Here is the everyday picture that makes the whole topic easy. Imagine a long tin can with air pressure inside. The pressure pushes the sides of the can outward, trying to split the can along its length. It also pushes the two ends outward, trying to pull the can apart along its axis. So the wall feels two different stresses: a stress going around the circle (the hoop direction, like the hoop of a barrel) and a stress going along the length. The hoop stress is usually about twice the length stress, and this fact is the first thing to remember.

Why does this matter for GATE? Questions about pressure vessels appear in the mechanics of materials part of GATE Robotics and Automation (RA). They usually give a diameter, a thickness and a pressure, and ask for a stress or a change in size. The formulas are short. The main challenge is to know when the thin-wall formulas are allowed, and to keep the units consistent. We will practise both.

We will go slowly. Each section adds one idea. Each formula is followed by a small example with every step, and a Python check confirms the numbers.

Key terms

Read this list once now, and return to it whenever a word feels new.

Pressure vessel
A closed container that holds fluid at a pressure different from the surroundings. Plain meaning: a tank or pipe under pressure.
Internal pressure (p)
The pressure of the fluid inside the vessel, pushing the walls outward. Unit: MPa in these problems.
Thin-walled vessel
A vessel whose wall is thin compared with its diameter, so the stress is almost the same across the thickness. Plain meaning: a thin skin holding the pressure.
Diameter (d)
The inside diameter of the vessel, in mm. In thin-wall formulas, d is often taken as the mean diameter.
Wall thickness (t)
The thickness of the vessel wall, in mm.
Hoop stress (sigma h)
The stress in the wall acting around the circumference. Plain meaning: the stress that tries to split the cylinder along its length.
Longitudinal stress (sigma l)
The stress in the wall acting along the length of the cylinder. Plain meaning: the stress that tries to pull the ends apart.
Circumferential stress
Another name for hoop stress.
Axial stress
Another name for longitudinal stress in a closed cylinder.
Closed end
An end that is sealed, so the pressure pushes on it and creates a longitudinal stress.
Thin sphere
A thin-walled spherical vessel, which has the same stress in every direction in its wall.
Volumetric strain
The fractional change in volume. Plain meaning: how much bigger the vessel gets in volume.
Poisson's ratio (nu)
The ratio of lateral strain to axial strain for the wall material, with a minus sign. Typical steel value is about 0.3.
Young's modulus (E)
The stiffness of the wall material in tension, in MPa or GPa.
Allowable stress
The largest stress the design permits, usually the yield stress divided by a safety factor.

When a wall counts as thin

The thin-wall idea

The simple formulas in this topic assume that the wall is thin. A wall is thin when its thickness is small compared with its diameter. In that case, the stress is almost the same through the thickness, and we can use the mean diameter in the formulas. A common rule in textbooks is to call a vessel thin when the ratio d/t is at least about 10 to 20, and the exact limit varies between books. In GATE questions, the question will usually say "thin cylinder" or "thin shell" when the formulas are allowed.

Think of a thick pipe, like a heavy steel tube for a gas line. Its inside wall is more stressed than its outside wall, and the stress changes across the thickness. The thin formulas would miss this change. A thin soft-drink can has almost the same stress across its wall, because the wall is so small compared with the can.

Key ideaThe thin-wall formulas are approximations. They are good when the wall is thin, and they are the ones you use in an exam unless the question says otherwise.

A cylinder has diameter 500 mm and wall thickness 10 mm. Is it thin by the d/t rule of thumb?

Yes. The ratio d/t is 500/10 = 50, which is well above 10 to 20, so the thin formulas apply.

Why do we use the mean diameter in thin-wall formulas?

Because the stress is almost even across the thin wall, so a single average diameter describes the wall well. The difference is small when t is small compared with d.

Hoop stress and longitudinal stress in a cylinder

Why the hoop stress is larger

Cut a thin cylinder across its middle and look at one side of the cut. The inside pressure pushes outward on the wall. The force on the whole wall is spread around the circle. Here is the key point: the pressure acts over the inside surface, and the wall must carry this force on its cut edges. Let us count the forces on a long slice of the cylinder, of length L.

Cut the cylinder along its length, through the axis, and look at one half. The pressure p pushes on the inside of the half. The projected area of the inside, seen from the cut, is d times L. So the total outward force on the half is p d L. The wall is holding this force at the two cut edges, where the wall thickness t and the length L give an area of 2tL in total. The stress in the wall is:

sigma h = p d L / (2 t L) = p d / (2 t)

Here sigma h is the hoop stress in MPa, p is the internal pressure in MPa, d is the diameter in mm, and t is the wall thickness in mm. The length L cancels, which is why the formula has no length in it.

Now cut the cylinder across its width, at right angles to its axis. The pressure acts on the closed ends, pulling them apart. The total force on the end is p times the area pi d2/4, and the wall carries it over the ring area pi d t. So:

sigma l = p (pi d2/4) / (pi d t) = p d / (4 t)

Here sigma l is the longitudinal stress. Compare the two formulas. The hoop stress is pd/(2t), and the longitudinal stress is pd/(4t). The hoop stress is exactly twice the longitudinal stress. This is the fact to remember.

Worked example

A closed cylinder has diameter d = 500 mm, wall thickness t = 10 mm, and internal pressure p = 2 MPa. Find the hoop stress and the longitudinal stress.

  • Step 1: hoop stress. sigma h = p d/(2t) = 2 x 500/(2 x 10) = 1000/20 = 50 MPa.
  • Step 2: longitudinal stress. sigma l = p d/(4t) = 1000/40 = 25 MPa.
  • Step 3: check the ratio. 50/25 = 2, as expected for a closed cylinder.

The Python check at the end of the page repeats these numbers with the strains too.

TrapUse the longitudinal formula pd/(4t) for the stress along the length, not pd/(2t). Many errors come from using the hoop formula for both.

GATE loves thisExpect a question that asks which stress is larger in a closed cylinder, or what the ratio is. The answer is that the hoop stress is twice the longitudinal stress. Learn this ratio as a single fact.

What is the hoop stress in a cylinder with p = 1.5 MPa, d = 400 mm and t = 8 mm?

sigma h = pd/(2t) = 1.5 x 400/16 = 600/16 = 37.5 MPa.

For an open-ended cylinder, which stress is zero?

The longitudinal stress, because the pressure does not push on closed ends. The hoop stress still acts.

Stress in a thin sphere

The same stress in every direction

A sphere has no ends and no preferred direction. So the stress in the wall is the same in every direction around it. The force balance is the same as for the longitudinal direction of a cylinder: the pressure acts over the circle of diameter d, and the wall carries the force over a ring of area pi d t. This gives:

sigma sphere = p d / (4 t)

This is the same as the longitudinal stress in the cylinder. A thin sphere therefore has less stress than the hoop stress in a cylinder of the same diameter and thickness. This is one reason spherical tanks are used for gas storage: the wall carries the pressure more evenly, and it needs only half the thickness of a cylinder for the same stress.

TipFor a thin sphere, the answer is the same as the longitudinal stress in a cylinder. If a question asks for a sphere, use pd/(4t) and do not double it.

A thin sphere of diameter 200 mm and thickness 5 mm carries 1 MPa of pressure. What is the wall stress?

sigma = pd/(4t) = 1 x 200/20 = 10 MPa.

Why does a spherical tank need less wall thickness than a cylinder for the same stress?

Because the sphere stress pd/(4t) is half the hoop stress pd/(2t) of the cylinder. For the same allowable stress, a sphere can use half the thickness.

Change in diameter and volume under pressure

Stress makes strain

A stressed wall also stretches. Two stresses act at once, so each strain includes the effect of the other stress. This is the same idea as Poisson's ratio from the elastic constants topic. For a closed thin cylinder, the strains are:

epsilon h = (sigma h - nu sigma l) / E
epsilon l = (sigma l - nu sigma h) / E

Here epsilon h is the hoop strain and epsilon l is the longitudinal strain. The hoop strain is the change in circumference divided by the original circumference, and it equals the change in diameter divided by the original diameter. So the change in diameter is:

delta d = epsilon h x d

The longitudinal strain gives the change in length, delta L = epsilon l x L.

Volumetric strain

The volumetric strain of a closed thin cylinder combines the two directions. For a small box, the volume strain is the sum of the strains in its three directions. For a cylinder under pressure, the radial strain is small compared with the others, so the result is 2 epsilon h + epsilon l. Substituting the formulas above gives the standard result:

epsilon v = p d (5 - 4 nu) / (4 t E)

This formula is a standard result from thin-wall mechanics for a closed cylinder. It is the one to use when the question asks for the volumetric strain.

Worked example

Take the same cylinder: d = 500 mm, t = 10 mm, p = 2 MPa. Let E = 200000 MPa and nu = 0.3. The hoop stress is 50 MPa and the longitudinal stress is 25 MPa.

  • Step 1: hoop strain. epsilon h = (50 - 0.3 x 25)/200000 = (50 - 7.5)/200000 = 42.5/200000 = 0.0002125.
  • Step 2: change in diameter. delta d = 0.0002125 x 500 = 0.10625 mm. The cylinder grows by about a tenth of a millimetre across its diameter.
  • Step 3: longitudinal strain. epsilon l = (25 - 0.3 x 50)/200000 = (25 - 15)/200000 = 0.00005.
  • Step 4: volumetric strain. epsilon v = 2 x 0.0002125 + 0.00005 = 0.000475. The standard formula gives the same value: 2 x 500 x (5 - 1.2)/(4 x 10 x 200000) = 3800/8000000 = 0.000475.

TrapThe change in diameter uses the hoop strain, not the longitudinal strain. The longitudinal strain changes the length, not the diameter.

In the example, what is the change in length if the cylinder is 2000 mm long?

delta L = epsilon l x L = 0.00005 x 2000 = 0.1 mm.

Does the pressure make a cylinder's diameter grow or shrink?

It grows, because the hoop strain is positive when the hoop stress is tension and larger than the Poisson effect of the longitudinal stress.

Choosing a safe wall thickness

Turning the formula around

In design, the pressure and the diameter are usually given, and the engineer must choose the thickness. The hoop stress is the largest stress in a closed cylinder, so the hoop formula sets the thickness. Solve sigma h = pd/(2t) for t:

t = p d / (2 sigma allow)

Here sigma allow is the allowable hoop stress, which is the strength of the material divided by a safety factor. Once t is found, a manufacturer picks the next standard thickness above it.

Worked example. A cylinder of diameter 600 mm must hold 2 MPa of pressure. The allowable hoop stress is 100 MPa. Then t = 2 x 600/(2 x 100) = 1200/200 = 6 mm. A wall of 6 mm is the minimum. Because the thin formula is approximate, a designer would add a corrosion allowance, but that is outside this topic.

TipThe hoop stress is always the one that controls the wall thickness in a closed cylinder, because it is twice the longitudinal stress.

A cylinder of diameter 800 mm carries 1 MPa. The allowable hoop stress is 80 MPa. What is the minimum thickness?

t = pd/(2 sigma allow) = 1 x 800/(160) = 5 mm.

Why does the longitudinal stress not set the thickness of a closed cylinder?

Because the hoop stress is larger, so it is the first to reach the allowable value as the pressure rises.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original, written in the style of GATE. They are not real past questions. Try each one before you read the solution.

Hoop stress in a cylinder

A thin cylinder has internal pressure 1.5 MPa, diameter 400 mm and wall thickness 8 mm. Find the hoop stress in MPa. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The hoop stress in MPa.
  2. Which idea applies. sigma h = pd/(2t).
  3. Work it out. sigma h = 1.5 x 400/(2 x 8) = 600/16 = 37.5 MPa.
  4. Check. The longitudinal stress is half of this, 18.75 MPa, so the ratio is 2 as expected.

Answer: 37.5 MPa.

Longitudinal stress from the same cylinder

Using the cylinder of the previous problem (pressure 1.5 MPa, diameter 400 mm, thickness 8 mm), find the longitudinal stress in MPa. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The stress along the length.
  2. Which idea applies. sigma l = pd/(4t).
  3. Work it out. sigma l = 600/(4 x 8) = 600/32 = 18.75 MPa.
  4. Check. Twice this value is 37.5 MPa, the hoop stress from the previous problem.

Answer: 18.75 MPa.

Stress in a spherical vessel (NAT)

A thin spherical vessel of diameter 200 mm and wall thickness 5 mm holds a pressure of 1 MPa. Find the wall stress in MPa. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The stress in the spherical wall.
  2. Which idea applies. sigma = pd/(4t) for a thin sphere.
  3. Work it out. sigma = 1 x 200/(4 x 5) = 200/20 = 10 MPa.
  4. Check. A cylinder with the same d and t would have hoop stress 20 MPa. The sphere is half of that, as expected.

Answer: 10 MPa.

Which statements are correct? (MSQ)

For a closed thin-walled cylinder under internal pressure, which of the following statements are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) The hoop stress is twice the longitudinal stress
(B) The thin-wall formulas assume the wall thickness is small compared with the diameter
(C) A thin sphere of the same diameter and thickness has the same stress as the longitudinal stress
(D) The hoop stress is the smaller of the two stresses

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Every correct statement.
  2. Which idea applies. The formulas pd/(2t), pd/(4t) and the thin-wall assumption.
  3. Work it out. (A) is correct, since pd/(2t) is twice pd/(4t). (B) is correct, which is the thin-wall assumption. (C) is correct, since the sphere stress pd/(4t) equals the longitudinal stress. (D) is wrong, because the hoop stress is the larger.
  4. Check. Three correct statements, and the wrong one reverses the ratio.

Answer: (A), (B) and (C).

Minimum wall thickness (NAT)

A cylinder of diameter 600 mm carries an internal pressure of 2 MPa. The allowable hoop stress is 100 MPa. Find the minimum wall thickness in mm using the thin-wall formula. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The minimum t in mm.
  2. Which idea applies. t = pd/(2 sigma allow).
  3. Work it out. t = 2 x 600/(2 x 100) = 1200/200 = 6 mm.
  4. Check. Put it back: sigma h = 2 x 600/(2 x 6) = 100 MPa. The check works.

Answer: 6 mm.

Change in diameter (NAT)

A thin closed cylinder has diameter 500 mm, wall thickness 10 mm and internal pressure 2 MPa. Take E = 200000 MPa and nu = 0.3. Find the increase in diameter in mm to five decimal places. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. delta d in mm.
  2. Which idea applies. First the stresses, then epsilon h = (sigma h - nu sigma l)/E, then delta d = epsilon h x d.
  3. Work it out. sigma h = 50 MPa and sigma l = 25 MPa. epsilon h = (50 - 7.5)/200000 = 0.0002125. delta d = 0.0002125 x 500 = 0.10625 mm.
  4. Check. The Python check in the chapter gives 0.10625 mm, the same value.

Answer: 0.10625 mm.

Volumetric strain (NAT)

For the same cylinder as in the previous problem (d = 500 mm, t = 10 mm, p = 2 MPa, E = 200000 MPa, nu = 0.3), find the volumetric strain multiplied by 10000. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. 10000 times the volumetric strain.
  2. Which idea applies. epsilon v = pd(5 - 4nu)/(4tE).
  3. Work it out. epsilon v = 2 x 500 x (5 - 1.2)/(4 x 10 x 200000) = 1000 x 3.8/8000000 = 0.000475. Multiply by 10000: 4.75.
  4. Check. The strain of 0.000475 matches the Python check in the chapter.

Answer: 4.75.

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Hoop and longitudinal stress (NAT, 2 marks)

A thin cylinder of diameter 1 m and wall 10 mm holds 2 MPa. Find the hoop and longitudinal stresses.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. σh = pd/(2t), σl = pd/(4t).
  2. Which idea applies. Thin wall theory.
  3. Work it out. σh = 2 · 1000/20 = 100 MPa. σl = 50 MPa.
  4. Check. The hoop stress is always twice the longitudinal.

Answer: 100 MPa and 50 MPa.

Exam-level 2: Change in diameter (NAT, 2 marks)

For the same cylinder with E = 200 GPa and ν = 0.3, find the increase in diameter in mm.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Δd = dεh.
  2. Which idea applies. εh = (σh − νσl)/E.
  3. Work it out. (100 − 15)/200000 = 4.25 × 10−4. Δd = 0.425 mm.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
eh = (100 - 0.3 * 50) / 200e3
print("%.3f mm" % (1000 * eh))

0.425 mm

Answer: 0.425 mm.

Exam-level 3: Required thickness (NAT, 2 marks)

A thin spherical vessel of diameter 2 m holds 1.5 MPa. The allowable stress is 100 MPa. Find the minimum wall thickness in mm.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. t from σ = pd/(4t).
  2. Which idea applies. A sphere has the same stress in all directions.
  3. Work it out. t = 1.5 · 2000/400 = 7.5 mm.
  4. Check. A cylinder of the same size would need 15 mm.

Answer: 7.5 mm.

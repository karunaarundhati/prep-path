Hydraulic and pneumatic actuators

Hydraulic and pneumatic actuators push and pull with pressurised fluid instead of a motor and gears. They give large forces, and GATE asks you to compare them and to calculate force, pressure and power.

The idea in plain words

An actuator is the part of a machine that produces motion. A motor is an actuator, and so is a cylinder that pushes a piston in and out. A hydraulic or pneumatic actuator uses a pressurised fluid to make the push. A hydraulic actuator uses oil or water. A pneumatic actuator uses compressed air.

Everyday analogy. Think of squeezing a toothpaste tube. A small push at the end creates pressure inside the tube, and the paste comes out of the hole, which is a bigger opening. Squeeze the wide end and you get a small output force at the narrow end. A hydraulic cylinder works the same way, with oil in place of toothpaste.

Why it matters. A small pump can lift a very heavy load with a hydraulic cylinder. This is why excavators, lifts and heavy robot legs use hydraulics. Pneumatic cylinders are cheap, fast and clean, so they are common in factory grippers and pick-and-place machines.

Where it appears in GATE. In the Robotics and Automation (RA) paper, expect questions on Pascal's law, on the force from a piston of known area and pressure, on hydraulic power, and on the strengths and weaknesses of each type of actuator.

Key terms

Actuator
A device that turns energy into motion. Precisely: a device that produces mechanical force or motion when driven by an energy source.
Pressure (p)
Force per unit area, p = F/A. Precisely: the force acting normally on a surface divided by that surface area.
Pascal (Pa)
The SI unit of pressure. 1 Pa = 1 N/m². Engineers often use the bar, where 1 bar = 100 000 Pa.
Pascal's law
A pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted equally in all directions and to every part of the container.
Piston
A disc that slides inside a cylinder. The fluid pushes on its face.
Cylinder
A tube with a piston inside. Fluid entering one side pushes the piston along.
Piston rod
The shaft fixed to the piston that carries the force out of the cylinder.
Pump
A machine that pushes fluid into the system and raises its pressure.
Valve
A device that starts, stops or directs the flow of fluid. A directional control valve sends fluid to one side or the other of the piston.
Relief valve
A safety valve that opens when pressure becomes too high, so the system does not burst.
Reservoir (tank)
The container that stores the hydraulic oil.
Flow rate (Q)
The volume of fluid passing a point each second, in m³/s. Engineers often use litres per minute (L/min), where 1 L = 0.001 m³.
Hydraulic power
Pressure times flow rate, P = p × Q, in watts.
Compressible
Able to be squeezed into a smaller volume. Air is compressible, and oil is almost not.
Force density
The force an actuator gives for its size or weight. Hydraulics have a high force density.
Compressor
A pump that squeezes air to a higher pressure for a pneumatic system.
Gauge pressure
Pressure measured above the surrounding air pressure. Most calculations in this page use gauge pressure.

Pascal's law and the hydraulic press

Why pressure is the same everywhere

Fill a closed container with oil and push on a small piston at one end. The oil is squeezed, and the pressure rises. Because liquids flow easily and do not compress much, the same pressure appears at every point in the oil, including on a second, larger piston at the other end. This is Pascal's law.

Pressure is the same on both pistons, but force is not. Force equals pressure times area. A larger area gives a larger force for the same pressure:

p = F1 / A1 = F2 / A2, so F2 = F1 × (A2 / A1)

Here F1 and A1 are the force and area of the small (input) piston, and F2 and A2 are those of the large (output) piston. The ratio A2/A1 is the force multiplication.

      small piston (A1)              large piston (A2)
        F1 down                         F2 up
          |                               ^
   +------+-------+                +-------+------+
   |  |=========|  |  <- oil ->   |     ==========   |
   +--------------+                +------------------+
        \_________________ pipe _____________________/

Worked example: a hydraulic press

A small piston has area A1 = 2 cm² and is pushed with F1 = 100 N. The large piston has A2 = 100 cm². Find the pressure and the output force.

  1. Pressure: p = F1 / A1 = 100 / 2 = 50 N/cm².
  2. The same pressure acts on the large piston: 50 N/cm².
  3. Output force: F2 = p × A2 = 50 × 100 = 5000 N.
  4. Force multiplication: 5000 / 100 = 50 times.
A1 = 2.0        # small piston area in cm^2
F1 = 100.0      # input force in newtons
A2 = 100.0      # large piston area in cm^2

p = F1 / A1     # pressure in N/cm^2
F2 = p * A2     # output force in newtons

print("Pressure = %.1f N/cm^2" % p)
print("Output force = %.0f N" % F2)
print("Force multiplication = %.0f" % (A2 / A1))
Pressure = 50.0 N/cm^2
Output force = 5000 N
Force multiplication = 50

TrapThe force goes up by the area ratio, but the piston moves through a smaller distance. The large piston moves 50 times less than the small one in this example. Energy is conserved, so a big force gives a small movement. Do not claim a free gain.

Question: a small piston of area 4 cm² is pushed with 200 N. The large piston has area 400 cm². What force does it give?

p = 200 / 4 = 50 N/cm². F2 = 50 × 400 = 20 000 N.

Question: why does a hydraulic press use oil and not air?

Oil is almost incompressible, so the input movement is passed directly to the output. Air is compressible, so part of the movement would be lost in squeezing the air.

Hydraulic components and power

The parts of a hydraulic system

A hydraulic system is a loop of fluid that the pump pushes around. The main parts are:

  • Reservoir, which stores the oil and lets air and dirt settle out.
  • Pump, which takes oil from the reservoir and pushes it into the system at high pressure.
  • Directional control valve, which sends oil to one side of the piston, and lets the oil on the other side return to the tank.
  • Cylinder, which turns the oil pressure into a pushing or pulling force.
  • Relief valve, which opens to protect the system when the pressure is too high.
  • Filter, which keeps dirt out of the valves, because dirt scratches the precision parts.

Hydraulic power

Power is the rate of doing work. For a hydraulic system, the power is the pressure times the flow rate:

P = p × Q

Here P is the power in watts, p is the pressure in pascals, and Q is the flow rate in cubic metres per second. Convert litres per minute to m³/s by dividing by 1000 and by 60.

Worked example: pump power

A pump delivers Q = 10 L/min at a pressure of 100 bar.

  1. Pressure in pascals: 100 bar = 100 × 100 000 = 10 000 000 Pa.
  2. Flow in m³/s: 10 / 1000 / 60 = 0.0001667 m³/s.
  3. Power: P = 10 000 000 × 0.0001667 = 1667 W, which is about 1.7 kW.
p = 100e5               # 100 bar in pascals (1 bar = 100 000 Pa)
Q_lpm = 10.0            # flow rate in litres per minute
Q = Q_lpm / 1000 / 60   # flow rate in cubic metres per second

P = p * Q
print("Flow = %.3e m^3/s" % Q)
print("Hydraulic power = %.0f W" % P)
Flow = 1.667e-04 m^3/s
Hydraulic power = 1667 W

Piston speed

The piston speed depends on how fast fluid enters the cylinder. If the flow is Q and the piston area is A, the speed is v = Q / A. A larger piston moves more slowly for the same flow.

Question: why is the flow rate needed to find power, and not just the pressure?

Pressure alone is a force per area. Power needs the rate at which fluid moves, so P = p × Q needs both.

Question: what happens to piston speed if the piston area is doubled and the flow stays the same?

v = Q / A, so doubling A halves the speed.

Pneumatic actuators

How a pneumatic system works

A pneumatic system uses compressed air. A compressor squeezes air into a tank called an air receiver. Air then passes through a filter, a pressure regulator and a lubricator, and into solenoid valves. A solenoid valve is an electrically switched valve. It sends air to the front or back of a cylinder, and the cylinder moves.

  compressor -> receiver -> filter/regulator -> solenoid valve -> cylinder
                                                     |
                                              electric signal from controller

Typical industrial systems run at around 6 bar. That is much lower than hydraulic systems, so a pneumatic cylinder gives less force for the same size.

Force from a pneumatic cylinder

The force is still pressure times area: F = p × A. The area of a circular piston is A = πd²/4, where d is the bore (the piston diameter). Use gauge pressure, the pressure above the air around us.

Worked example: a 50 mm pneumatic cylinder

A pneumatic cylinder has a bore of 50 mm and runs at 6 bar gauge. Find the force, ignoring friction and the piston rod.

  1. Convert: d = 50 mm = 0.05 m, and p = 6 bar = 600 000 Pa.
  2. Area: A = π × 0.05² / 4 = 0.0019635 m², which is 19.63 cm².
  3. Force: F = 600 000 × 0.0019635 = 1178 N.
import math

p = 6e5             # 6 bar gauge in pascals
d = 0.050           # bore diameter in metres (50 mm)

A = math.pi * d ** 2 / 4    # piston area in square metres
F = p * A                   # force in newtons
print("Piston area = %.2f cm^2" % (A * 1e4))
print("Force = %.0f N" % F)
Piston area = 19.63 cm^2
Force = 1178 N

Strengths and weaknesses of air

Because air is compressible, a pneumatic cylinder acts a little like a spring. It can be pushed and it springs back a little. This is useful for gentle gripping and for safety, but it makes precise positioning hard. A pneumatic cylinder also cannot hold a position against a changing load as well as a hydraulic cylinder can.

Air is cheap and clean. Air that leaks out does no harm, unlike oil. The exhaust is simply released to the room.

Question: a pneumatic cylinder has a bore of 40 mm at 6 bar. Roughly what force does it give?

A = π × 0.04² / 4 = 0.0012566 m². F = 600 000 × 0.0012566 = 754 N, about 750 N.

Question: why is a pneumatic gripper a good choice for picking up fragile items?

Air is compressible, so the grip is springy. The gripper applies a limited, fairly gentle force set by the air pressure, which avoids crushing the item.

Comparing hydraulic, pneumatic and electric actuation

No actuator is best for every job. A designer picks the one whose strengths match the task. The table compares the three common choices in general terms.

PropertyHydraulicPneumaticElectric (motor plus gears)
Force densityVery highModerateModerate to high, depends on gearing
SpeedModerateHighModerate to high
Precise position controlGood with valves and feedbackHard, because air is compressibleVery good with feedback
Holding a position under loadGoodWeakGood with brakes or drive current

Notice that the table compares general tendencies. A GATE question may give specific numbers, and then you must use the formulas, not the table.

Choosing for a robot

A heavy legged robot needs large forces and fast reactions, so designers often choose hydraulics. A pick-and-place arm in a factory that moves light parts quickly and cleanly often uses pneumatics for its grippers. A precise, quiet arm for a laboratory usually uses electric actuators with feedback sensors. Designers also combine them, for example a motor for the arm joints and a pneumatic gripper for the hand.

GATE loves thisExpect a question that asks which actuator suits a task. Look at the key words. "Large force, heavy load" points to hydraulics. "Clean, fast, simple back and forth motion" points to pneumatics. "Precise position with feedback" points to electric actuators.

Question: name one reason a pneumatic actuator is not used for precise positioning.

Air is compressible, so the position moves with the load and does not stay exactly where the controller sends it.

Question: which actuator would you choose for a clean food-packing line that moves light cartons quickly?

Pneumatics. It is clean, fast and cheap for simple back and forth motion with light loads.

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original problems in the GATE style. They are not real past paper questions.

Force from a larger piston

A hydraulic press has a small piston of area 5 cm² and a large piston of area 500 cm². A force of 200 N is applied to the small piston. Find the output force on the large piston.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The output force F2 in newtons.
  2. Which idea applies. Pascal's law: F2 = F1 × A2 / A1.
  3. Work it out. F2 = 200 × 500 / 5 = 200 × 100 = 20 000 N.
  4. Check. The area ratio is 100, so the force must be 100 times the input. 200 × 100 = 20 000 N. Good.
F1 = 200.0
A1 = 5.0
A2 = 500.0
F2 = F1 * A2 / A1
print("Output force = %.0f N" % F2)
Output force = 20000 N

Answer: 20 000 N.

Piston speed

Oil flows into a cylinder at 2 L/min. The piston area is 20 cm². Find the piston speed in cm/s.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The speed in cm/s.
  2. Which idea applies. v = Q / A. Work in cm for both quantities.
  3. Work it out. Q = 2 L/min = 2000 cm³/min. Divide by 60 for cm³/s: 33.33 cm³/s. Then v = 33.33 / 20 = 1.67 cm/s.
  4. Check. 1.67 cm/s times 20 cm² equals 33.3 cm³/s, which matches the flow. Good.
Q_cm3_per_s = 2000 / 60
A = 20.0
v = Q_cm3_per_s / A
print("Piston speed = %.2f cm/s" % v)
Piston speed = 1.67 cm/s

Answer: 1.67 cm/s.

Which statements about actuators are true?

Consider the following statements about actuators. Select all that apply.

(A) Oil is almost incompressible, so hydraulic actuators give a stiff, precise response.
(B) Pneumatic cylinders hold a position against a changing load better than hydraulic cylinders.
(C) A hydraulic system needs a reservoir and a return path for the oil.
(D) For the same bore, a pneumatic cylinder gives more force than a hydraulic cylinder.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which of the four statements are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. The compressibility of oil and air, the pressure levels, and the parts of a hydraulic loop.
  3. Work it out. (A) True, incompressible oil transmits movement directly. (B) False, air is compressible and springy, so it holds a position less well. (C) True, the oil must be stored and returned. (D) False, hydraulic systems run at much higher pressure, so for the same bore they give much more force.
  4. Check. Statements (B) and (D) both reverse the true picture, so they are wrong.

Answer: (A) and (C).

Force from a larger pneumatic bore

A pneumatic cylinder has a bore of 80 mm and runs at 6 bar gauge. Ignore friction and the rod. Find the force in newtons, correct to the nearest newton.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The force F in newtons.
  2. Which idea applies. A = πd² / 4, then F = p × A.
  3. Work it out. d = 0.08 m, so A = π × 0.0064 / 4 = 0.0050265 m². F = 600 000 × 0.0050265 = 3015.9 N, which rounds to 3016 N.
  4. Check. The bore is twice the earlier 40 mm, so the area is four times larger. The earlier answer was about 754 N, and 4 × 754 = 3016 N. Good.
import math

p = 6e5
d = 0.080
A = math.pi * d ** 2 / 4
F = p * A
print("Force = %.0f N" % F)
Force = 3016 N

Answer: 3016 N.

Pump power at high pressure

A hydraulic pump delivers 20 L/min at 150 bar. Find the hydraulic power in kW.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The power in kW.
  2. Which idea applies. P = p × Q, with SI units.
  3. Work it out. p = 150 × 100 000 = 15 000 000 Pa. Q = 20 / 1000 / 60 = 0.00033333 m³/s. P = 15 000 000 × 0.00033333 = 5000 W = 5.0 kW.
  4. Check. Doubling the flow of the earlier example (10 L/min at 100 bar, 1667 W) and raising the pressure by 1.5 times gives 2 × 1.5 × 1667 = 5000 W. Good.
p = 150e5
Q = 20 / 1000 / 60
P = p * Q
print("Power = %.1f kW" % (P / 1000))
Power = 5.0 kW

Answer: 5.0 kW.

Extending and retracting a double-acting cylinder

A hydraulic double-acting cylinder has a bore of 100 mm and a piston rod of diameter 40 mm. The oil pressure is 100 bar. When the piston extends, the full piston area takes the pressure. When it retracts, the oil acts on the annular area (the piston area minus the rod area). Find the retraction force in kN, correct to one decimal place.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The retraction force in kN.
  2. Which idea applies. F = p × A, but with the annular area Aann = π(D² - d2) / 4, where D is the bore and d is the rod diameter.
  3. Work it out. p = 100 × 100 000 = 10 000 000 Pa. D = 0.1 m and d = 0.04 m. Aann = π(0.01 - 0.0016) / 4 = π × 0.0084 / 4 = 0.0065973 m². F = 10 000 000 × 0.0065973 = 65 973 N, which is 66.0 kN to one decimal place. Careful: 65.973 rounds to 66.0.
  4. Check. The extension force is 10 000 000 × π × 0.01 / 4 = 78 540 N, which is larger. The retraction force must be smaller because the annular area is smaller. 65.97 kN is smaller than 78.54 kN. Good.
import math

p = 100e5
D = 0.100
d = 0.040
A_ann = math.pi * (D ** 2 - d ** 2) / 4
F_retract = p * A_ann
F_extend = p * math.pi * D ** 2 / 4
print("Retraction force = %.1f kN" % (F_retract / 1000))
print("Extension force = %.1f kN" % (F_extend / 1000))
Retraction force = 66.0 kN
Extension force = 78.5 kN

Answer: 66.0 kN for retraction. The rounded value 65.97 kN becomes 66.0 kN.

Retraction speed from the annular area

The same cylinder (bore 100 mm, rod 40 mm) is retracted by oil flowing at 30 L/min into the annular side. Find the retraction speed in cm/s, correct to two decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The retraction speed in cm/s.
  2. Which idea applies. Speed is flow divided by the area that the oil fills, here the annular area: v = Q / Aann.
  3. Work it out. Q = 30 / 1000 / 60 = 0.0005 m³/s. Aann = 0.0065973 m². v = 0.0005 / 0.0065973 = 0.07579 m/s, which is 7.58 cm/s.
  4. Check. Multiply back: 0.0065973 × 0.07579 = 0.0005 m³/s, which is the flow. Good. The speed is slower than an extension at the same flow would be, because the full piston area is larger.
import math

Q = 30 / 1000 / 60
A_ann = math.pi * (0.100 ** 2 - 0.040 ** 2) / 4
v = Q / A_ann
print("Retraction speed = %.2f cm/s" % (v * 100))
Retraction speed = 7.58 cm/s

Answer: 7.58 cm/s.

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Cylinder force (NAT, 2 marks)

A hydraulic cylinder has bore 80 mm and rod 40 mm. At 10 MPa, find the extending force and the retracting force in kN.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Forces on the two sides of the piston.
  2. Which idea applies. Extend: p · πD²/4. Retract: p · π(D² − d²)/4.
  3. Work it out. Extend: 10e6 · π · 0.0064/4 = 50.27 kN. Retract: 10e6 · π · 0.0048/4 = 37.70 kN.
  4. Check. The code computes both.
import math
p = 10e6
print("%.2f kN, %.2f kN" % (p * math.pi * 0.08 ** 2 / 4 / 1000, p * math.pi * (0.08 ** 2 - 0.04 ** 2) / 4 / 1000))

50.27 kN, 37.70 kN

Answer: about 50.27 kN and 37.70 kN.

Exam-level 2: Piston speed (NAT, 2 marks)

The same cylinder is fed 30 L/min. Find the extension speed in m/s, correct to 3 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. v = Q/A.
  2. Which idea applies. Convert flow to m³/s.
  3. Work it out. Q = 5 × 10−4 m³/s. A = 5.027 × 10−3 m². v = 0.099 m/s.
  4. Check. Retraction is faster because the annulus area is smaller.
import math
print("%.3f m/s" % (30e-3 / 60 / (math.pi * 0.08 ** 2 / 4)))

0.099 m/s

Answer: about 0.099 m/s.

Exam-level 3: Pneumatics versus hydraulics (MSQ, 2 marks)

Which are TRUE? (A) Pneumatic systems are easier to hold at an exact mid position than hydraulic ones. (B) Hydraulic systems give higher force density. (C) Air is compressible, which makes pneumatic motion springy. (D) Pneumatic systems often exhaust to atmosphere.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Comparisons of the two technologies.
  2. Which idea applies. Oil is nearly incompressible.
  3. Work it out. (A) False, compressibility makes it harder. (B) True. (C) True. (D) True.
  4. Check. Pneumatics suits fast, light, two position tasks.

Answer: (B), (C) and (D).

Summary

Each line gives the plain idea first, then the short definition.

Actuator
A device that turns energy into force or motion.
Pressure
Force divided by area, p = F/A.
Pascal's law
Pressure in an enclosed fluid acts equally in all directions.
Force multiplication
The output force is the input force times the ratio of the two areas.
Hydraulic actuator
A cylinder driven by pressurised oil. Very high force density.
Pneumatic actuator
A cylinder driven by compressed air. Clean, fast and cheap, with a spring-like response.
Reservoir
The tank that stores hydraulic oil.
Relief valve
A safety valve that opens when the pressure becomes too high.
Directional control valve
A valve that sends fluid to one side or the other of a piston.

P = p × Q, pressure times flow rate.
Piston area
A = πd²/4 for a circular piston of diameter d.
Piston speed
v = Q / A.
Gauge pressure
Pressure measured above the surrounding air pressure.
Compressible
Able to be squeezed into a smaller volume. Air is compressible, oil is nearly not.

An electrically switched valve that directs air in a pneumatic system.
Annular area
The piston area minus the rod area, used on the rod side of a double-acting cylinder.

Remember these

  1. Pressure is the same everywhere in a closed fluid, but force depends on area.
  2. A bigger piston gives a bigger force and a slower speed.
  3. Convert bar to Pa (×100 000) and litres per minute to m³/s (÷ 60 000) before you calculate.
  4. Hydraulic power is p × Q.
  5. Hydraulics give high force, pneumatics give speed and cleanliness, and electric actuators give precise control.
  6. Air is compressible, so pneumatic cylinders are springy and less precise.

Euler's theory of columns

A long, thin column can suddenly bow sideways and collapse even when the material itself is strong enough. Euler's formula gives the load at which this buckling starts, and it is one of the most reliable GATE numericals in the strength of materials part of the paper.

The idea in plain words

Welcome. Try a small experiment in your mind. Take a long plastic ruler and press on its two ends, pushing straight towards each other. At first the ruler stays straight, and it simply gets slightly shorter. Keep pushing. Suddenly the ruler jumps sideways and bows into a curve. The ruler has not broken, and the plastic is not crushed. It has buckled. That sudden sideways failure is what this whole topic is about.

Here is the everyday picture. Think about a tall, thin pole holding up a tent. If the pole is short and fat, you can lean on it all day. If the pole is long and thin, a small push from the wind makes it bend, and then the whole tent falls. The thinness and the length decide whether a small load is safe. Strength of the material is not the main question. Stiffness and geometry are.

Why does this matter for GATE? In Robotics and Automation (RA) papers, robot links, linear actuator rods, and support columns are often long and slender. A designer must check that the compressive load stays below the buckling load. GATE RA questions on this topic typically give a column with a cross-section, an end condition, a length, and a modulus of elasticity, then ask for the critical buckling load. Once you know the formula and the effective length, the question becomes a clean, step-by-step calculation.

The plan for this page: first we see buckling as a separate kind of failure. Then we meet Euler's formula, and each symbol in it. Then we learn the effective length for four classic end conditions, and the slenderness ratio that tells us whether Euler's formula applies. We finish with worked numericals.

Key terms

Read this list now, and come back whenever a word feels unfamiliar. Each entry gives the plain meaning first, then the precise definition.

Column
A vertical or straight member that carries a compressive load along its length. Plain meaning: a long post that holds weight from above. Precise definition: a slender structural member loaded axially in compression.
Axial load (P)
A force that acts along the length of the member, through its axis. Plain meaning: a push or pull straight down the middle. Precise definition: a force whose line of action coincides with the longitudinal axis.
Buckling
A sudden sideways bending of a slender column under compression. Plain meaning: the straight column bows out. Precise definition: loss of stability in which a compressed member deflects laterally, with a large increase in deflection for a small increase in load.
Critical buckling load (Pcr)
The smallest axial load at which a perfectly straight column can stay bent. Plain meaning: the load at which the column gives way. Precise definition: the axial load at which the straight equilibrium state becomes unstable.
Euler's formula
The formula for the critical buckling load of an ideal elastic column. Plain meaning: a rule that gives the buckling load from stiffness and length. Precise definition: Pcr = π² E I / Le².
Young's modulus (E)
The stiffness of the material. Plain meaning: how hard it is to stretch or squeeze the material. Precise definition: the ratio of stress to strain in the elastic range, in MPa or GPa.
Second moment of area (I)
A measure of how the cross-section resists bending. Plain meaning: how stiff the shape is against bending, which depends on how far the material sits from the centre. Precise definition: I = integral of y² dA over the area, in mm⁴.
Minimum I
The smaller of the two second moments about the principal axes. Plain meaning: the weaker direction of the shape. Precise definition: the smallest value of I about any axis through the centroid; buckling happens about this axis.
Effective length (Le)
The length of an equivalent pinned-pinned column that buckles in the same way. Plain meaning: the length the buckling curve behaves as if the column had. Precise definition: Le = K × L, where K depends on the end conditions.
Effective length factor (K)
The number that converts the actual length into the effective length. Plain meaning: a multiplier for how well the ends are held. Precise definition: K = 1 for pinned-pinned, K = 2 for fixed-free, K = 0.5 for fixed-fixed, and about 0.7 for fixed-pinned.
Pinned end
An end that can rotate freely but cannot move sideways. Plain meaning: a hinge. Precise definition: an end with zero deflection but free rotation.
Fixed end
An end that can neither rotate nor move sideways. Plain meaning: a column built into concrete. Precise definition: an end with zero deflection and zero slope.
Free end
An end that can move sideways and rotate. Plain meaning: the top of a flagpole with nothing holding it. Precise definition: an end with no restraint on deflection or slope.
Radius of gyration (r)
A length that describes how the area is spread out about an axis. Plain meaning: an average distance of the material from the centre. Precise definition: r = square root of (I / A).
Slenderness ratio (lambda)
The ratio of effective length to radius of gyration. Plain meaning: how long and thin the column is. Precise definition: lambda = Le / r, which has no units.
Euler stress (sigmacr)
The critical buckling load divided by the area. Plain meaning: the compressive stress at which buckling starts. Precise definition: sigmacr = Pcr / A = π² E / lambda².
Yield strength (sigmay)
The stress at which the material starts to deform permanently. Plain meaning: the point where the material gives in. Precise definition: the stress at the onset of plastic deformation.
Short column (stub)
A column so short and thick that it fails by crushing before it buckles. Plain meaning: a squat block. Precise definition: a column with low slenderness whose failure is governed by yield.

Concept 1: Buckling is a different kind of failure

Most beginners think a column fails when the material is squeezed too hard, like crushing a biscuit. That is true for a short, thick column. A long, thin column behaves differently. It can fail while the compressive stress is far below the strength of the material, because it becomes unstable.

Think of balance. A pencil standing on its point is balanced in the vertical direction, but the smallest sideways nudge makes it fall. A column under load is in a similar position. For a small load, the straight shape is stable: if you push it sideways slightly, it springs back. At a particular load, the straight shape becomes unstable: a tiny push and the column bows, and it does not return to straight. That particular load is the critical buckling load.

Here is a text picture of a column with pinned ends, shown before and after buckling.

Key ideaBuckling depends on stiffness and length, not on the strength of the material. A stiff, long column can buckle at a load far below the load that would crush it.

This is why engineers say a column is controlled by stability, not by strength, when it is long and slender.

Question 1: A column is much stronger than the load on it, but it still buckles. Is this possible?

Yes. Buckling is an instability, which depends on stiffness and length. A long, slender column can buckle long before the material reaches its crushing stress.

Question 2: What is the critical buckling load?

It is the smallest axial load at which a perfectly straight column can stay in a bent position. For loads below it, the straight shape is stable.

Concept 2: Euler's buckling formula

Leonhard Euler worked out the buckling load for an ideal column. Ideal means: perfectly straight, loaded exactly through the centroid, made of an elastic material that obeys Hooke's law, and with no initial curvature. The result is:

Pcr = π² × E × I / Le²

Let us say what each symbol means, because each one matters for the answer.

  • Pcr is the critical buckling load, in N. It is the axial force at which buckling starts.
  • π² is pi multiplied by itself. Its value is about 9.87. It is a pure number that comes from the shape of the buckled curve.
  • E is Young's modulus, in MPa (N/mm²). A stiffer material gives a larger buckling load.
  • I is the second moment of area of the cross-section about the axis of buckling, in mm⁴. A column that is wide in the buckling direction resists more.
  • Le is the effective length, in mm. A longer effective length gives a much smaller buckling load, because the load is squared in the denominator.

Two facts follow from the formula straight away. First, the buckling load is inversely proportional to the square of the effective length. Doubling Le cuts Pcr to one quarter. Second, the buckling load uses the minimum I. The column will bow in the direction where it is weakest, so you must use the smaller value of I.

Worked example. A steel column has E = 200 000 MPa, I = 1 × 106 mm⁴, and effective length Le = 1000 mm. Find Pcr.

  1. Write the formula: Pcr = π² E I / Le².
  2. Find Le² = 1000 × 1000 = 1 × 106 mm².
  3. Find E × I = 200 000 × 1 × 106 = 2 × 1011.
  4. Divide: 2 × 1011 / 1 × 106 = 2 × 105 N.
  5. Multiply by π² ≈ 9.8696: Pcr = 9.8696 × 2 × 105 = 1 973 920 N, which is about 1974 kN.
import math
E = 200000      # MPa
I = 1e6         # mm^4
Le = 1000       # mm
Pcr = math.pi**2 * E * I / Le**2
print("Pcr =", round(Pcr / 1000, 2), "kN")

Pcr = 1973.92 kN

TrapUse the length in the same units as I. If I is in mm⁴, use Le in mm. Mixing metres and millimetres is the most common arithmetic error in this topic.

Question 3: If the effective length of the column above is doubled, what happens to Pcr?

It falls to one quarter of its value, because Pcr is proportional to 1 / Le². So it becomes about 493.48 kN.

Question 4: Which second moment of area should you use for a rectangular column of sides 40 mm and 80 mm?

The smaller one. The column buckles in the weaker direction, so you use the minimum of the two second moments of area of the rectangle.

Concept 3: Effective length for four end conditions

The actual length L of a column is not always the length used in Euler's formula. What matters is how the ends are held. Think of a column as a wave. The buckled shape has a length between points where the curve is straight, and that length is the effective length. For a pinned-pinned column, the buckled curve is a single half-wave, and the effective length equals the actual length. For other end conditions, the buckled shape is different, and the effective length changes.

Worked example. A column of actual length 3 m is fixed at one end and free at the other. What is its effective length? Since K = 2, Le = 2 × 3 = 6 m. This is a long effective length, so the buckling load will be small.

Question 5: A column of length 4 m is fixed at both ends. What is its effective length?

Le = 0.5 × 4 = 2 m.

Question 6: Which end condition gives the largest buckling load for the same column?

Fixed at both ends, because it has the smallest effective length factor, K = 0.5.

Concept 4: The slenderness ratio and radius of gyration

Two columns with the same effective length can behave differently, because one may be fat and the other thin. To compare them fairly, engineers use a single number that combines length and shape. This number is the slenderness ratio.

First, the radius of gyration r tells us how the area is spread out about the buckling axis. Its formula is:

r = square root of (I / A)

Here I is the second moment of area in mm⁴, and A is the cross-sectional area in mm². The radius of gyration is in mm. A larger r means the material sits further from the axis, which makes the column stiffer.

Next, the slenderness ratio is:

lambda = Le / r

It has no units, because mm divided by mm is a pure number. A large lambda means a long, thin column. A small lambda means a short, stubby column.

Substituting r into the buckling formula gives the Euler stress, which is the critical load per unit area. Since Pcr = π² E I / Le² and I = A r², we have Pcr / A = π² E r² / Le² = π² E / lambda². So:

sigmacr = π² E / lambda²

This is a useful form, because it shows directly that a larger slenderness ratio gives a much smaller buckling stress. Here is a worked example with a square column.

Worked example. A square column is 100 mm by 100 mm, with pinned ends and an actual length of 3000 mm. Find r, lambda and sigmacr, using E = 200 000 MPa.

  1. Area: A = 100 × 100 = 10 000 mm².
  2. Second moment: I = b⁴ / 12 = 100⁴ / 12 = 100 000 000 / 12 = 8 333 333 mm⁴.
  3. Radius of gyration: r = square root of (8 333 333 / 10 000) = square root of 833.33 = 28.87 mm.
  4. Effective length: pinned-pinned, so Le = 3000 mm.
  5. Slenderness: lambda = 3000 / 28.87 = 103.92.
  6. Euler stress: sigmacr = π² × 200 000 / 103.92² = 1 973 921 / 10 800 = 182.8 MPa.
import math
b = 100          # mm, side of the square
A = b * b        # mm^2
I = b**4 / 12    # mm^4
r = math.sqrt(I / A)
L = 3000         # mm, pinned at both ends so Le = L
lam = L / r
sigma_cr = math.pi**2 * 200000 / lam**2
print("r =", round(r, 2), "mm")
print("slenderness =", round(lam, 2))
print("Euler stress =", round(sigma_cr, 1), "MPa")

r = 28.87 mm
slenderness = 103.92
Euler stress = 182.8 MPa

TrapThe radius of gyration must be taken about the buckling axis, which is the axis with the smaller I. For a 40 mm by 80 mm rectangle, that is the axis parallel to the 80 mm side, where the depth in the bending direction is 40 mm.

Question 7: Why does a larger slenderness ratio give a smaller Euler stress?

Because sigmacr = π² E / lambda², and lambda is squared in the denominator. Doubling lambda reduces the Euler stress to one quarter.

Question 8: What is the unit of the slenderness ratio?

It has no unit. It is a ratio of two lengths, both in mm, so the units cancel.

Concept 5: When Euler's formula is valid

Euler's formula assumes the material stays elastic. If the stress at buckling is above the yield strength, the material yields first, and the formula over-predicts the load. So Euler's formula is valid only for long, slender columns. For short columns, the failure is by crushing, and the yield load is the right answer.

The dividing line is the transition slenderness, found by setting the Euler stress equal to the yield strength:

lambdac = π × square root of (E / sigmay)

If lambda is greater than lambdac, the column is long, and Euler's formula applies. If lambda is less than lambdac, the column is short or intermediate, and the yield or empirical methods apply instead.

Worked example. A steel column has E = 200 000 MPa and yield strength sigmay = 250 MPa. Find lambdac.

  1. E / sigmay = 200 000 / 250 = 800.
  2. Square root of 800 = 28.28.
  3. Multiply by π: lambdac = 3.1416 × 28.28 = 88.86.
  4. So columns with lambda greater than about 89 are long enough for Euler's formula.
import math
E = 200000
sy = 250
lam_c = math.pi * math.sqrt(E / sy)
print("transition slenderness =", round(lam_c, 2))

transition slenderness = 88.86

GATE loves thisBefore using Euler's formula in a question, check the slenderness. If the question gives a yield strength, or if lambda is clearly small, the answer is probably the yield load, not the Euler load. Stating this check earns the marks even when the arithmetic is simple.

Question 9: A column has lambda = 40 and the transition slenderness is 89. Is Euler's formula valid?

No. lambda is less than lambdac, so the column is short or intermediate, and Euler's formula over-predicts the buckling load.

Question 10: Does a higher yield strength raise or lower the transition slenderness?

It lowers it. lambdac = π × square root of (E / sigmay), so a larger yield strength makes the fraction smaller. Stronger materials therefore need longer columns before Euler applies.

Concept 6: A routine for solving buckling numericals

Most GATE buckling questions follow the same route. Use this checklist every time, and the question becomes calm.

  1. Read the end conditions. Decide the effective length factor K, and write Le = K × L.
  2. Find I and A. Use the minimum I for the buckling axis, and find A for the area.
  3. Find lambda. Compute r = square root of (I / A) and lambda = Le / r.
  4. Check validity. Compare lambda with lambdac if a yield strength is given.
  5. Compute Pcr. Use π² E I / Le² in mm and N, then convert to kN if asked.

Here is a last example that uses the whole routine. A solid circular steel rod of diameter 20 mm is pinned at both ends. Its actual length is 1 m. Take E = 200 000 MPa. Find the critical load.

import math
d = 20           # mm
I = math.pi * d**4 / 64
A = math.pi * d**2 / 4
L = 1000         # mm, pinned-pinned so Le = L
Le = 1.0 * L
Pcr = math.pi**2 * 200000 * I / Le**2
print("I =", round(I, 1), "mm^4")
print("Pcr =", round(Pcr, 1), "N")

I = 7854.0 mm^4
Pcr = 15503.1 N

The answer is about 15.5 kN. The rod has a small I, so it buckles at a modest load. Notice how much the small diameter reduces the answer, because I depends on the fourth power of the diameter.

TrapUsing the diameter in place of the radius for I. The second moment of a solid circle is π d⁴ / 64, not π d⁴ / 4. Also check that the buckling axis is the one with the smaller I, which for a circle is every axis.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original GATE-style questions written for practice. They are not past exam questions. Work each one on paper first.

Which end condition gives the largest load

Four identical columns of the same length and cross-section differ only in their end conditions. Which of the following statements are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) Fixed at both ends gives a larger buckling load than pinned at both ends.
(B) Fixed at one end and free at the other gives the smallest buckling load.
(C) Pinned at both ends gives the same buckling load as fixed at one end and pinned at the other.
(D) The buckling load does not depend on the end conditions.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct. This is an MSQ.
  2. Which idea applies. Pcr is proportional to 1 / Le², and Le depends on the end conditions through K.
  3. Work it out. Fixed-fixed has K = 0.5, pinned-pinned has K = 1, so fixed-fixed gives the larger load: (A) is true. Fixed-free has K = 2, the largest, so it gives the smallest load: (B) is true. K = 1 and K = 0.7 are different, so (C) is false. (D) is false, because the end conditions set K.
  4. Check. A larger K always gives a smaller load. Fixed-free has the largest K, which agrees with (B).

Answer: (A) and (B) only.

Effective length of a fixed-free column

A column of actual length 2.5 m is fixed at its base and free at its top. What is its effective length, in metres? This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The effective length in m.
  2. Which idea applies. Le = K × L, with K = 2 for fixed-free.
  3. Work it out. Le = 2 × 2.5 = 5.0 m.
  4. Check. The effective length must be larger than the actual length for a fixed-free column, and 5.0 is larger than 2.5.

Answer: 5.0 m.

Critical load of a steel tube

A steel column has E = 200 GPa and I = 4 × 106 mm⁴. It is pinned at both ends and has an actual length of 2 m. Find the critical buckling load, in kN. This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Pcr in kN.
  2. Which idea applies. Euler's formula with pinned-pinned, so Le = L.
  3. Work it out. E = 200 000 MPa, Le = 2000 mm. Le² = 4 × 106. E × I = 200 000 × 4 × 106 = 8 × 1011. Divide: 8 × 1011 / 4 × 106 = 2 × 105 N. Multiply by π² = 9.8696: 1 973 920 N = 1973.92 kN.
  4. Check. This matches the first worked example in Concept 2: the same E I / Le² ratio gives the same answer, 1973.92 kN.

Answer: about 1974 kN (1973.92 kN).

Buckling load of a column with changed end support

A pinned-pinned steel column carries a buckling load of 500 kN. The same column is changed to be fixed at both ends. What is its new buckling load, in kN? Assume the same E, I and actual length. This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The buckling load for fixed-fixed, in kN.
  2. Which idea applies. Pcr is proportional to 1 / Le², and fixed-fixed has Le = L / 2 while pinned-pinned has Le = L.
  3. Work it out. The ratio of effective lengths is (L/2) / L = 0.5. The ratio of loads is 1 / 0.5² = 4. So the new load is 4 × 500 = 2000 kN.
  4. Check. Stronger ends should increase the load, and 2000 kN is four times 500 kN, as the formula predicts.

Answer: 2000 kN.

Which columns are long enough for Euler

A steel column has E = 200 000 MPa and yield strength 250 MPa. Which of the following columns can have Euler's formula applied? Select all that apply. Take the transition slenderness as about 89.

(A) A column with lambda = 120
(B) A column with lambda = 60
(C) A column with lambda = 95
(D) A column with lambda = 45

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which columns satisfy lambda greater than lambdac. This is an MSQ.
  2. Which idea applies. Euler's formula applies when lambda is greater than the transition slenderness.
  3. Work it out. 120 is greater than 89, so (A) is valid. 60 is less, so (B) is not. 95 is greater than 89, so (C) is valid. 45 is less, so (D) is not.
  4. Check. The two valid values are above 89, and the two invalid values are below it, which matches the rule.

Answer: (A) and (C) only.

Buckling of a rectangular column

A rectangular steel column has cross-section 40 mm by 80 mm. Its effective length is 2000 mm. Take E = 200 000 MPa. Find the critical buckling load, in kN, about the axis that gives the smaller second moment of area. Use the minimum I. This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Pcr in kN using the minimum I.
  2. Which idea applies. The second moment of a rectangle is b h³ / 12, and the buckling axis is the one with the smaller value.
  3. Work it out. There are two bending axes. Bending about the axis parallel to the 80 mm side means the depth in the bending direction is 40 mm: I = 80 × 40³ / 12 = 80 × 64 000 / 12 = 426 667 mm⁴. Bending about the axis parallel to the 40 mm side means the depth is 80 mm: I = 40 × 80³ / 12 = 1 706 667 mm⁴. The smaller value is 426 667 mm⁴, so that is the one used. Then Pcr = π² × 200 000 × 426 667 / 2000² = π² × 200 000 × 426 667 / (4 × 106) = π² × 21 333.3 = 9.8696 × 21 333.3 = 210 552 N = 210.55 kN.
  4. Check. The larger I would give exactly four times the answer, so using the smaller I is the choice that the code requires. A Python check is shown below.

Answer: about 210.55 kN.

Statements about slender columns

Which of the following statements about Euler buckling are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) Doubling the effective length reduces the critical load to one quarter.
(B) Euler's formula uses the largest second moment of area.
(C) A column with a high slenderness ratio is controlled by stability rather than by yield.
(D) A stiffer material gives a larger critical load for the same geometry.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct. This is an MSQ.
  2. Which idea applies. Pcr = π² E I / Le², the minimum I, and the role of slenderness.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true, because Pcr is proportional to 1 / Le². (B) is false: Euler uses the minimum I. (C) is true, since long columns buckle before they yield. (D) is true, because Pcr is proportional to E.
  4. Check. Three statements match the formula. The one with the largest I is the wrong choice, so (B) is false.

Answer: (A), (C) and (D) only.

Slenderness of a hollow column

A hollow circular steel column has outer diameter 60 mm and inner diameter 40 mm. It is pinned at both ends and has an actual length of 1.2 m. Find the slenderness ratio lambda. Take the second moment of area of a hollow circle as π (D⁴ − d⁴) / 64 and the area as π (D² − d²) / 4. Give lambda to two decimal places. This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. lambda to two decimal places.
  2. Which idea applies. r = square root of (I / A), then lambda = Le / r with Le = L.
  3. Work it out. The Python check below computes I, A, r and then lambda. Working by hand: D⁴ − d⁴ = 60⁴ − 40⁴ = 12 960 000 − 2 560 000 = 10 400 000, so I = π × 10 400 000 / 64. The area is π (3600 − 1600) / 4 = π × 500. The ratio I / A = (10 400 000 / 64) / 500 = 325 mm². So r = square root of 325 = 18.03 mm. Then lambda = 1200 / 18.03 = 66.56.
  4. Check. A hollow tube has a larger r than a solid rod of the same outer size, so its lambda is lower. Lambda 66.56 is below the transition value of about 89 for steel with 250 MPa yield, so Euler's formula should not be trusted here without further checks. A Python check follows.

Answer: lambda = 66.56. Because this is below the transition slenderness for the steel in the earlier examples, the yield-based method is the right tool for this column, not Euler's formula.

Transition slenderness of a stronger steel

A steel with E = 200 000 MPa has yield strength 400 MPa. Find the transition slenderness lambdac, to two decimal places. This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. lambdac to two decimal places.
  2. Which idea applies. lambdac = π × square root of (E / sigmay).
  3. Work it out. E / sigmay = 200 000 / 400 = 500. Square root of 500 = 22.36. Multiply by π: 3.1416 × 22.36 = 70.24.
  4. Check. A stronger steel gives a smaller transition slenderness (70.24 against 88.86 for 250 MPa), as the concept predicts.

Answer: 70.24.

import math
b, h = 40, 80          # mm, rectangle sides
I1 = b * h**3 / 12     # about the axis with the 80 mm depth
I2 = h * b**3 / 12     # about the other axis
I = min(I1, I2)        # buckling uses the smaller value
Pcr = math.pi**2 * 200000 * I / 2000**2
print("I min =", round(I, 0), "mm^4")
print("Pcr =", round(Pcr / 1000, 2), "kN")
D, d = 60, 40          # mm, outer and inner diameters
Ihol = math.pi * (D**4 - d**4) / 64
Ahol = math.pi * (D**2 - d**2) / 4
r = math.sqrt(Ihol / Ahol)
lam = 1200 / r
print("r =", round(r, 2), "mm")
print("lambda =", round(lam, 2))

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Critical load (NAT, 2 marks)

A pinned-pinned steel column (E = 200 GPa) of length 3 m has I = 8 × 106 mm⁴. Find the Euler load in kN, correct to 1 decimal place.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Pcr = π²EI/L².
  2. Which idea applies. Effective length = L for pinned ends.
  3. Work it out. π² · 200e9 · 8e−6/9 = 1754.6 kN.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
import math
print("%.1f kN" % (math.pi ** 2 * 200e9 * 8e-6 / 9 / 1000))

1754.6 kN

Answer: about 1754.6 kN.

Exam-level 2: End conditions (NAT, 2 marks)

The same column is made fixed at both ends. By what factor does the Euler load change?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The ratio from effective lengths.
  2. Which idea applies. Fixed-fixed effective length = L/2.
  3. Work it out. P ∝ 1/Le², so the factor is 4.
  4. Check. A cantilever (fixed-free, Le = 2L) gets 1/4.

Answer: 4.

Exam-level 3: Slenderness (NAT, 2 marks)

A solid circular column of diameter 40 mm and length 2 m is pinned at both ends. Find the slenderness ratio.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Le/k.
  2. Which idea applies. k = d/4 for a solid circle.
  3. Work it out. k = 10 mm. 2000/10 = 200.
  4. Check. High slenderness means Euler's formula applies.

Answer: 200.

Summary

Buckling
Sudden sideways failure of a slender column under compression. Precise definition: loss of stability at the critical load.
Critical buckling load
The smallest load at which a straight column can stay bent. Precise definition: Pcr = π² E I / Le².
Euler's formula
The rule for the critical load of an ideal elastic column. Precise definition: Pcr = π² E I / Le².
Effective length
The length of an equivalent pinned-pinned column. Precise definition: Le = K × L.
Effective length factor
The multiplier for the end conditions. Precise definition: K = 1, 2, 0.5 or about 0.7 for the four cases.
Second moment of area
Resistance of a section to bending. Precise definition: I = integral of y² dA, in mm⁴.
Minimum second moment
The smaller I, about the weak axis. Precise definition: buckling occurs about this axis.
Radius of gyration
Average distance of the area from the axis. Precise definition: r = square root of (I / A).
Slenderness ratio
How long and thin the column is. Precise definition: lambda = Le / r.
Euler stress
Critical stress for a long column. Precise definition: sigmacr = π² E / lambda².
Transition slenderness
The dividing line for Euler's formula. Precise definition: lambdac = π × square root of (E / sigmay).
Short column
A stubby column that fails by crushing. Precise definition: lambda less than lambdac.
Pinned end
A hinge that allows rotation but not sideways movement. Precise definition: zero deflection, free rotation.
Fixed end
An end held against both sideways movement and rotation. Precise definition: zero deflection and zero slope.
Yield strength
Stress at which permanent deformation starts. Precise definition: sigmay.

Remember these

  1. Long slender columns can buckle at a load far below the crushing load.
  2. Pcr = π² E I / Le², with the minimum I and the effective length.
  3. Doubling the effective length cuts the buckling load to one quarter.
  4. Fixed-fixed K = 0.5, pinned-pinned K = 1, fixed-pinned about 0.7, fixed-free K = 2.
  5. Slenderness is lambda = Le / r, with r = square root of (I / A).
  6. Euler's formula applies only when lambda is greater than the transition slenderness.
  7. Always convert units to mm and N before you finish the calculation.

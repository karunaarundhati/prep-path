R, L, C, M elements

Resistors, inductors, capacitors and mutually coupled coils are the basic building blocks of electric circuits. Each one has a simple rule that links voltage and current, and these rules are the starting point for every network theorem in the Robotics and Automation (RA) paper.

The idea in plain words

An electric circuit is a set of wires and parts that carry electric charge. Three quantities describe what happens in a circuit. Voltage is the push that moves charge, measured in volts (V). Current is the flow of charge past a point each second, measured in amperes (A). Power is how fast energy is used or stored, measured in watts (W).

Think of water in a pipe. Voltage is like the pressure that pushes the water. Current is like the flow rate of the water. A narrow pipe resists the flow. A tank stores water and releases it later. A heavy wheel keeps spinning once it is moving, and resists any change in speed.

Circuits contain four basic two-terminal elements that do not generate energy: the resistor (R), the inductor (L), the capacitor (C) and the mutual inductance (M), which links two coils. Each one has a rule that connects its voltage and current. Learn these rules well, because the network theorems and circuit solving methods in later GATE topics all start from them.

Why it matters for GATE. In the Robotics and Automation (RA) paper, the electric network part asks you to find a current or voltage in a small circuit. You need the element rules, the energy formulas and the rules for combining elements. Many questions are short, but the details are easy to mix up, so this topic goes slowly.

Key terms

Voltage (V)
Plain: the push that drives charge. Precise: the energy per unit charge needed to move a charge between two points, in volts.
Current (I or i)
Plain: the flow of charge. Precise: the rate of charge flow, in amperes, where 1 A is 1 coulomb per second.
Power (P or p)
Plain: energy per second. Precise: the product of voltage and current, in watts.
Energy
Plain: the ability to do work. Precise: power integrated over time, in joules (J).
Passive sign convention
A rule for drawing reference directions: current enters the + terminal of an element, so that p = v i gives the power absorbed by the element.
Resistor (R)
An element that opposes current and turns electrical energy into heat. Unit: ohm (Ω).
Inductor (L)
A coil that stores energy in a magnetic field. Its current cannot change instantly. Unit: henry (H).
Capacitor (C)
Two conductors separated by an insulator that store energy in an electric field. Its voltage cannot change instantly. Unit: farad (F).
Mutual inductance (M)
The link between two coils, so that a change in current in one induces voltage in the other. Unit: henry (H).
Coupling coefficient (k)
A number from 0 to 1 that measures how strongly two coils are linked.
Impedance (Z)
The complex generalization of resistance for sinusoidal signals. Unit: ohm (Ω).
Angular frequency (ω)
How fast a sine wave oscillates, in radians per second. For a frequency f in hertz, ω = 2πf.

Concept 1: Voltage, current and the passive sign convention

Before any element rule, you must know how to draw a circuit. Each element has two terminals. We draw a reference arrow for the current through the element, and a + and - sign for the voltage across it. These reference directions are just choices we make. They are not the true directions, and the answer can come out negative if the true direction is opposite.

The passive sign convention says: draw the current arrow entering the + terminal. With this choice, the power absorbed by the element is

p = v × i

Here p is power in watts, v is voltage in volts, and i is current in amperes. If p is positive, the element absorbs power, as a resistor does. If p is negative, the element delivers power, as a battery does.

Example. An element has v = 6 V and i = 2 A, with the current entering the + terminal. Then p = 6 × 2 = 12 W, so the element absorbs 12 W. If the same element had i = -2 A, the current would really flow out of the + terminal, and p = 6 × (-2) = -12 W. The element then delivers 12 W.

TrapA negative answer is not a mistake. It tells you the true direction is opposite to the reference arrow. Always keep the reference arrow fixed once you draw it, and then read the sign.

Question: An element has v = 5 V and i = 3 A with the passive sign convention. Does it absorb or deliver power?

It absorbs power, because p = 5 × 3 = 15 W is positive.

Question: What unit do we get from volts times amperes?

Watts. One volt times one ampere is one watt.

Concept 2: The resistor R

A resistor is the simplest element. It resists the flow of current, and the energy it takes from the circuit is turned into heat. Think of a narrow section of pipe, or a thin wire that gets warm when current flows through it.

Ohm's law gives the rule for a resistor:

v = i R

Here v is the voltage across the resistor in volts, i is the current through it in amperes, and R is the resistance in ohms. The rule works because the current is proportional to the voltage. Double the voltage and the current doubles. Double the resistance and the current halves.

Example. A resistor R = 4 Ω carries i = 3 A. Its voltage is v = 3 × 4 = 12 V. Its power is p = v i = 12 × 3 = 36 W. The same power can be found from p = i2 R = 32 × 4 = 9 × 4 = 36 W, or from p = v2/R = 122/4 = 144/4 = 36 W. All three forms give the same answer.

p = v i = i2 R = v2 / R

The symbol i2 means i times i. These three forms are useful when you know two of the three quantities. For a resistor, power is always positive, which means a resistor always absorbs energy.

Key ideaA resistor absorbs power at every instant, whatever the current direction. The power is i2 R, which is never negative.

Here is a short Python check of the 4 Ω example, and of series and parallel resistors that will be used in Concept 6.

from fractions import Fraction as F

R1, R2 = 4, 12                       # ohms
print("Series resistance (ohm):", R1 + R2)
print("Parallel resistance (ohm):", F(1, F(1, R1) + F(1, R2)))
V = 12                               # volts across the 4 ohm resistor
print("Power in the 4 ohm resistor (W):", F(V * V, R1))
Series resistance (ohm): 16
Parallel resistance (ohm): 3
Power in the 4 ohm resistor (W): 36
Question: A 10 Ω resistor has 2 A through it. What is its power?

p = i2 R = 22 × 10 = 4 × 10 = 40 W.

Question: If the voltage across a resistor doubles and R stays the same, what happens to the power?

It becomes four times larger. Power is v2/R, and doubling v multiplies v2 by 4.

Concept 3: The inductor L and its energy

An inductor is a coil of wire. When current flows through it, it creates a magnetic field. The inductor stores energy in this field. When the current changes, the magnetic field changes, and the coil produces a voltage that opposes the change. This is an important idea: an inductor resists any change in current.

The element rule for an inductor uses a derivative, which measures how fast a quantity changes:

v = L × (di/dt)

Here v is voltage in volts, L is inductance in henries, and di/dt is how fast the current i is changing, in amperes per second. A large rate of change gives a large voltage. If the current is steady, di/dt = 0, and the voltage is zero.

Example. An inductor has L = 2 H. The current rises steadily from 0 A to 5 A in 1 second, so di/dt = 5 A/s. The voltage is v = 2 × 5 = 10 V while the current is rising.

The energy stored in the magnetic field is

W = (1/2) L i2

Here W is energy in joules. For the inductor above, when i = 3 A, W = (1/2) × 2 × 32 = 1 × 9 = 9 J.

Two facts that follow from the rule

  • The current through an inductor cannot change instantly. An instant change would need an infinite voltage, because di/dt would be infinite. The current is continuous.
  • In steady DC, an inductor acts like a short circuit. With a constant current, di/dt = 0, so v = 0. The inductor has zero voltage across it, just like a plain wire.

TrapThe voltage across an inductor depends on the rate of change of current, not on the current itself. A large steady current gives zero voltage. Students often write v = L i by mistake.

Question: An inductor carries a constant 4 A. What is its voltage?

Zero. A constant current has di/dt = 0, so v = L × 0 = 0.

Question: What is the energy in a 2 H inductor carrying 1 A?

W = (1/2) × 2 × 12 = 1 J.

Concept 4: The capacitor C and its energy

A capacitor is two conducting plates separated by an insulator. When a voltage is applied, charge builds up on the plates. The capacitor stores energy in the electric field between them. It resists any change in voltage, because changing the voltage means moving charge on or off the plates, and that takes current.

The element rule for a capacitor is the mirror image of the inductor's:

i = C × (dv/dt)

Here i is current in amperes, C is capacitance in farads, and dv/dt is how fast the voltage is changing, in volts per second. A capacitor passes current only when its voltage changes.

Example. A capacitor has C = 2 µF (microfarads, which is 2 × 10-6 F). Its voltage rises from 0 V to 5 V in 1 ms (1 millisecond, which is 10-3 s). Then dv/dt = 5 / 0.001 = 5000 V/s. The current is i = 2 × 10-6 × 5000 = 0.01 A, which is 10 mA.

The energy stored is

W = (1/2) C v2

Here W is energy in joules and v is the voltage in volts. For a 0.5 F capacitor at 4 V, W = (1/2) × 0.5 × 42 = 0.25 × 16 = 4 J.

Two facts that follow from the rule

  • The voltage across a capacitor cannot change instantly. An instant change would need an infinite current, because dv/dt would be infinite.
  • In steady DC, a capacitor acts like an open circuit. With a constant voltage, dv/dt = 0, so i = 0. No current flows, just as if the wire were cut.

Key ideaInductors and capacitors are duals. The inductor blocks changes in current and shorts out in DC. The capacitor blocks changes in voltage and opens up in DC.

Question: A capacitor has a constant 9 V across it. What is the current?

Zero. The voltage is constant, so dv/dt = 0, and i = C × 0 = 0.

Question: Can the voltage across a capacitor jump from 2 V to 6 V instantly?

No. That would need an infinite current. The voltage changes continuously.

Concept 5: Mutual inductance M

Now bring two coils close together. Current in coil 1 creates a magnetic field that passes through coil 2. A changing current in coil 1 then induces a voltage in coil 2. This link is called mutual inductance, written M. Two coils can also have their own self-inductances L1 and L2, which act as in Concept 3.

Draw the two coils with dots at one end of each. The dot convention tells us the sign of the mutual term. If both currents enter their dotted terminals, the two magnetic fields add, and the mutual term is positive. If one current enters a dot and the other leaves a dot, the fields oppose, and the mutual term is negative. The element equations are:

v1 = L1 (di1/dt) + M (di2/dt)
v2 = M (di1/dt) + L2 (di2/dt)

Here v1 and v2 are the voltages across the two coils, i1 and i2 are their currents, and the derivatives give the rates of change. The symbol M multiplies the rate of change of the other coil's current. This is the key difference from a single coil: each coil's voltage depends on both currents.

Coupling coefficient

How strongly are the coils linked? The coupling coefficient k measures this:

k = M / √(L1 L2)

The symbol √ means the square root. Here k is between 0 and 1. The value k = 0 means no coupling, and k = 1 means perfect coupling, where all the flux links both coils. There is also a fact you must remember: M2 is never larger than L1 L2.

Example. Let L1 = 4 H, L2 = 9 H and M = 3 H. Then √(L1 L2) = √36 = 6, so k = 3/6 = 0.5. Also M2 = 9, which is less than L1 L2 = 36. The values are valid.

TrapThe mutual inductance M is not the same as a coupling coefficient. M is in henries, and k is a pure number. Also, a larger M does not always mean stronger coupling, because the coupling depends on both M and the self-inductances.

from math import sqrt

L1, L2, M = 4, 9, 3                  # henry
k = M / sqrt(L1 * L2)
print("Coupling coefficient k:", k)
print("Valid (M squared <= L1 L2):", M * M <= L1 * L2)
Coupling coefficient k: 0.5
Valid (M squared <= L1 L2): True
Question: Two coils with L1 = 1 H and L2 = 1 H have M = 1 H. What is k?

k = 1 / √(1 × 1) = 1. This is perfect coupling.

Question: If the dots are placed so that the fields oppose, what is the sign of the mutual term in v1?

Negative. The term becomes -M (di2/dt).

Concept 6: Series, parallel and impedance

Series means elements are joined end to end, so the same current flows through each one. Parallel means elements share the same two end points, so they have the same voltage across them. Combining elements into one equivalent element makes many circuits simpler.

ElementSeries combinationParallel combination
Resistors RReq = R1 + R2 + ...1/Req = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ...
Inductors L (no mutual)Leq = L1 + L2 + ...1/Leq = 1/L1 + 1/L2 + ...
Capacitors C1/Ceq = 1/C1 + 1/C2 + ...Ceq = C1 + C2 + ...

Notice the duality. Capacitors combine like resistors and inductors combine in reverse. Series capacitors need the reciprocal formula, just as parallel resistors do.

Example. R1 = 4 Ω and R2 = 12 Ω. In series, Req = 4 + 12 = 16 Ω. In parallel, 1/Req = 1/4 + 1/12 = 3/12 + 1/12 = 4/12 = 1/3, so Req = 3 Ω. Notice that the parallel value is smaller than either resistor. Each path gives the current another route.

Series inductors with mutual coupling

When two coils are in series and coupled, the mutual term adds to the total. If the dots make the fields aid each other, Leq = L1 + L2 + 2M. If they oppose, Leq = L1 + L2 - 2M.

Impedance for sinusoidal signals

When the source is a sine wave, the voltage and current are sinusoids at the same frequency, and each element behaves like a complex resistance called impedance. Robotics and electrical engineering use j for the imaginary unit, where j2 = -1. For angular frequency ω, the impedances are:

ZR = R
ZL = jωL
ZC = 1/(jωC) = -j/(ωC)

Impedances in series add, and parallel impedances combine like resistors. Use the same formulas, with Z in place of R. The real part of Z is the resistance, and the imaginary part is the reactance. The inductor's reactance is positive and the capacitor's is negative.

Python can check a small impedance. The values are ω = 100 rad/s, R = 10 Ω, L = 0.1 H and C = 100 µF.

from math import sqrt

w = 100.0                 # angular frequency in radians per second
R = 10.0                  # ohms
L = 0.1                   # henry
C = 0.0001                # farad (100 microfarad)
ZL = 1j * w * L           # inductor impedance
ZC = 1 / (1j * w * C)     # capacitor impedance
Z = R + ZL + ZC
print("Real part (ohm):", round(Z.real, 6))
print("Imaginary part (ohm):", round(Z.imag, 6))
print("Magnitude (ohm):", round(abs(Z), 2))
Real part (ohm): 10.0
Imaginary part (ohm): -90.0
Magnitude (ohm): 90.55

The inductor contributes +j10 Ω, and the capacitor contributes -j100 Ω. The imaginary parts add to -90 Ω, and the magnitude is √(102 + 902) = √8200, which is about 90.55 Ω.

GATE loves thisLearn the table of series and parallel rules by heart. Then check every answer. The parallel value must be smaller than the smallest element, and the series value must be bigger than the largest.

Question: Two capacitors, 3 µF and 6 µF, are in series. What is the equivalent capacitance?

1/Ceq = 1/3 + 1/6 = 2/6 + 1/6 = 3/6 = 1/2, so Ceq = 2 µF.

Question: What is the impedance of an inductor with L = 2 H at ω = 3 rad/s?

ZL = jωL = j × 3 × 2 = j6 Ω.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original, written in the style of GATE. They are not past exam questions.

Power in a resistor

A 4 Ω resistor has 12 V across it. What power does it absorb, in watts?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The power absorbed by the resistor, in watts.
  2. Which idea applies. p = v2/R for a resistor, or p = v i with i = v/R.
  3. Work it out. p = 122/4 = 144/4 = 36 W. The current is i = 12/4 = 3 A, and p = 12 × 3 = 36 W, the same answer.
  4. Check. Both routes give 36 W, so the answer is consistent.

Answer: 36 W.

Current in a capacitor

A 2 µF capacitor has its voltage rise steadily from 0 V to 5 V in 1 ms. What is the current, in milliamperes?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The current in mA.
  2. Which idea applies. i = C dv/dt, with a steady rise so dv/dt = Δv / Δt.
  3. Work it out. dv/dt = 5 V / 0.001 s = 5000 V/s. i = 2 × 10-6 × 5000 = 0.01 A = 10 mA.
  4. Check. A small capacitor with a fast voltage rise gives a small current, so a few milliamperes is a sensible size.

Answer: 10 mA.

Properties of an inductor (select all that apply)

Which statements about an ideal inductor are true? Select all that apply.

(A) The current through it cannot change instantly.
(B) In steady DC it acts like an open circuit.
(C) The energy it stores is (1/2) L i2.
(D) Its voltage equals L times the rate of change of its current.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. The inductor rule v = L di/dt and the energy formula.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true, an instant change needs infinite voltage. (B) is false, in steady DC di/dt = 0, so v = 0, which is a short circuit. (C) is true, the energy formula. (D) is true, the element rule.
  4. Check. The capacitor does the opposite in DC: it is an open circuit, not a short. Keep the two elements separate in your mind.

Answer: (A), (C) and (D).

Inductors in series with mutual coupling

Two coils with L1 = 2 H and L2 = 3 H are connected in series. The mutual inductance is M = 1 H, and the dots make the fields aid each other. What is the equivalent inductance, in henries? Give a whole number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Leq in henries.
  2. Which idea applies. Series aiding: Leq = L1 + L2 + 2M.
  3. Work it out. Leq = 2 + 3 + 2 × 1 = 2 + 3 + 2 = 7 H.
  4. Check. Without coupling it would be 5 H. Aiding coupling adds 2M = 2 H, which is a sensible increase.

Answer: 7 H.

Two capacitors in series

A 3 µF and a 6 µF capacitor are connected in series. What is the equivalent capacitance, in microfarads? Give a whole number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Ceq in µF.
  2. Which idea applies. Series capacitors use the reciprocal rule: 1/Ceq = 1/C1 + 1/C2.
  3. Work it out. 1/Ceq = 1/3 + 1/6 = 2/6 + 1/6 = 3/6 = 1/2. So Ceq = 2 µF.
  4. Check. The series value is smaller than the smaller capacitor, 3 µF, which is always true for series capacitors.

Answer: 2 µF.

Magnitude of a series impedance

A series circuit has R = 3 Ω, an inductor with reactance XL = 4 Ω (so ZL = j4 Ω), and a capacitor with ZC = -j8 Ω. Find the magnitude |Z| of the total impedance, in ohms. Give a whole number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The magnitude of Z = R + ZL + ZC.
  2. Which idea applies. Add impedances in series, then find the magnitude with √(real2 + imaginary2).
  3. Work it out. Z = 3 + j4 - j8 = 3 - j4. The magnitude is √(32 + (-4)2) = √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5 Ω.
  4. Check. The 3-4-5 right triangle is a well-known pattern, so 5 is the expected answer.

Answer: 5 Ω.

Statements about coupled coils (select all that apply)

Two coils have self-inductances L1 and L2, and mutual inductance M. Which statements are true? Select all that apply.

(A) The coupling coefficient k is always between 0 and 1.
(B) M2 is never larger than L1 L2.
(C) M can be larger than √(L1 L2) if the coils are well wound.
(D) If k = 0, the coils are not coupled at all.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which facts about coupled coils are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. k = M / √(L1 L2), with 0 ≤ k ≤ 1.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true, that is the standard range. (B) is true, it is the same fact as k ≤ 1 written without a square root. (C) is false, since k ≤ 1 forces M ≤ √(L1 L2). (D) is true, because k = 0 means M = 0.
  4. Check. Take the earlier example: L1 = 4, L2 = 9, M = 3. Here k = 0.5, and M2 = 9 is less than 36.

Answer: (A), (B) and (D).

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Energy in an inductor (NAT, 2 marks)

A 50 mH inductor carries i(t) = 4 sin(100t) A. Find the maximum stored energy in joules.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The peak of (1/2)Li².
  2. Which idea applies. Energy is largest when |i| is largest.
  3. Work it out. (1/2)(0.05)(16) = 0.4 J.
  4. Check. The energy oscillates between 0 and 0.4 J at twice the current frequency.

Answer: 0.4 J.

Exam-level 2: Capacitors in series and parallel (NAT, 2 marks)

A 6 µF and a 3 µF capacitor are in series, and this pair is in parallel with a 4 µF capacitor. Find the equivalent capacitance in µF.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The combined capacitance.
  2. Which idea applies. Series: C = C1C2/(C1 + C2). Parallel: add.
  3. Work it out. Series: 18/9 = 2. Add 4: 6 µF.
  4. Check. Series combinations are always smaller than either part.

Answer: 6 µF.

Exam-level 3: Coupled coils (NAT, 2 marks)

Two coils of 4 H and 9 H have coupling coefficient k = 0.5. Find the total inductance when they are connected in series aiding.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. L = L1 + L2 + 2M.
  2. Which idea applies. M = k√(L1L2).
  3. Work it out. M = 0.5 · 6 = 3 H. L = 4 + 9 + 6 = 19 H.
  4. Check. In series opposing, L = 13 − 6 = 7 H.

Answer: 19 H.

Dot convention
Sets the sign of the mutual term.

Add them.

Add reciprocals, then invert.

Remember these

  1. Inductor voltage depends on the rate of change of current, not on the current.
  2. Capacitor current depends on the rate of change of voltage, not on the voltage.
  3. Inductors are shorts and capacitors are opens in steady DC.
  4. Series inductors add, but series capacitors use the reciprocal rule.
  5. Coupled coils: k = M / √(L1 L2), with 0 ≤ k ≤ 1.
  6. Impedance replaces resistance for sine waves: jωL for an inductor and 1/(jωC) for a capacitor.

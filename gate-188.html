Springs

A spring stores energy, pushes back when squeezed or stretched, and gives a machine its bounce. This page builds the helical spring formulas from a simple picture, shows how springs combine in series and parallel, and links stiffness to vibration, a common GATE Robotics and Automation topic.

The idea in plain words

Welcome. Springs are everywhere in machines. A pen clicks because of a spring. A car's suspension absorbs bumps because of springs. A robot joint is often held in place by a spring when power is off. The main job of a spring is simple: it pushes back when you push it, and it pulls back when you pull it. The harder it pushes back for each unit of squeeze, the stiffer the spring.

Think of a slinky toy. Hold one end and let the other hang. The slinky stretches. If you add a heavier toy at the bottom, it stretches more. The amount of stretch is in proportion to the force, as long as you do not stretch it too far. This simple rule, force proportional to stretch, is the foundation of everything on this page. It is called Hooke's law.

Why does this matter for GATE? In the Machine Design part of the Robotics and Automation paper, you will meet questions about the spring rate of a coil, the shear stress in its wire, the energy it stores, and the frequency at which a mass on a spring vibrates. These are all short calculations, provided you know the formulas and what each symbol means. This page explains each formula with a worked example and a small check.

We will go slowly. We begin with one idea, stiffness, and build from there.

Key terms

Read this list once, and come back to it when a word feels unfamiliar.

Spring
An elastic element that stores energy when deformed and returns to its shape. Precisely: a component whose force and deflection are related, and which recovers its shape when the load is removed.
Helical spring
A spring made from wire wound into a coil, like a telephone cord or a compression spring in a pen. Precisely: a wire bent into a helix, loaded along its axis.
Wire diameter (d)
The thickness of the spring wire. Precisely: the diameter of the cross section of the wire, in mm.
Mean coil diameter (D)
The average diameter of the coil, measured from the centre of the wire on one side to the centre on the other. Precisely: the mean diameter of the helix, in mm.
Active coils (n)
The number of coils that can deflect. Precisely: the coils free to bend, not counting the ends that are clamped or ground flat.
Spring index (C)
The ratio of coil diameter to wire diameter. Precisely: C = D/d, a dimensionless number, usually between 4 and 12 in good design.
Spring rate or stiffness (k)
The force needed per unit deflection. Precisely: k = F/delta, in N/mm or N/m.
Deflection (delta)
How much the spring moves under load. Precisely: the change in length of the spring, in mm.
Shear modulus (G)
The stiffness of a material in shear. Precisely: the ratio of shear stress to shear strain, for steel about 80 GPa (80000 MPa).
Wahl factor (KW)
A correction factor that raises the shear stress to allow for the curvature of the wire and the direct shear. Precisely: KW = (4C - 1)/(4C - 4) + 0.615/C.
Series springs
Springs placed one after another, so that the same force passes through each. Precisely: a chain of springs whose total flexibility is the sum of their individual flexibilities.
Parallel springs
Springs placed side by side, so that they share the same deflection. Precisely: springs whose stiffnesses add up.
Strain energy
The energy stored in a deformed spring. Precisely: the work done in deforming the spring, written U.
Natural frequency
The frequency at which a system vibrates freely after a disturbance. Precisely: omegan = square root of (k/m) in rad/s, and fn = omegan/(2 pi) in Hz.
Hz (hertz)
One cycle per second. Precisely: the SI unit of frequency.

Spring rate: how stiff a helical spring is

The spring rate, or stiffness, k, tells you how much force is needed to move the spring by one millimetre. A stiff spring needs a big force for a small movement. A soft spring moves a lot with a small force. The definition is simple.

k = F / delta

Here F is the force in newtons and delta is the deflection in millimetres, so k is in N/mm. For a helical spring, the stiffness is found from the geometry of the coil and the material's shear modulus.

k = G d4 / (8 D3 n)

Each symbol has a clear meaning. G is the shear modulus of the material, in MPa (N/mm2). d is the wire diameter in mm. D is the mean coil diameter in mm. n is the number of active coils. Look at the powers. The wire diameter appears to the fourth power, so doubling the wire diameter makes the spring about 16 times stiffer. The coil diameter appears to the third power in the denominator, so doubling the coil diameter makes the spring about 8 times softer. This is why the wire size is the strongest lever a designer has.

Worked example. A steel spring has G = 80000 MPa, wire diameter d = 4 mm, mean coil diameter D = 32 mm and n = 10 active coils. Find k.

  1. Write the formula: k = G d4/(8 D3 n).
  2. Find the powers: d4 = 44 = 256. D3 = 323 = 32768.
  3. Numerator: 80000 times 256 = 20480000.
  4. Denominator: 8 times 32768 times 10 = 2621440.
  5. Divide: k = 20480000/2621440 = 7.8125 N/mm.

So the spring needs 7.8125 N to move 1 mm.

TipWrite the powers out first, as in step 2. Then the arithmetic is simply multiplying and dividing. Most spring errors come from forgetting that d is raised to the fourth power and D to the third.

TrapUse the number of active coils n, not the total number of coils. Plain ends that are closed and ground do not deflect in the same way, and questions usually give n directly.

If the wire diameter doubles and nothing else changes, how does k change?

k becomes 2 to the fourth power = 16 times bigger. The spring is 16 times stiffer.

If the number of active coils doubles, what happens to k?

k halves. More coils means more length that bends, so the spring is softer. Stiffness is inversely proportional to n.

Shear stress in a spring and the Wahl factor

When you squeeze a spring, the wire does not get pulled straight. It is twisted. Picture holding a pencil at both ends and twisting it. The wire is in torsion, and the stress inside it is shear stress. The simple torsion formula gives the shear stress as a starting point.

tauplain = 8 F D / (pi d3)

Here F is the force in newtons, D and d are in mm, and tau is in MPa. But this simple formula is not enough for a real spring. Two things add to the stress. The wire curves around the coil, which crowds material on the inside of the curve. And the force also gives a direct shear across the wire. The Wahl factor combines both effects into one number.

KW = (4C − 1)/(4C − 4) + 0.615/C, where C = D/d
tau = KW times 8 F D / (pi d3)

The Wahl factor is always greater than 1. For a spring index C = 8, which is a common design choice, it is about 1.18. This means the real stress is about 18 percent higher than the simple torsion formula predicts. A designer who forgets the Wahl factor will overestimate the safety margin.

Worked example. The spring above has d = 4 mm and D = 32 mm, so C = 32/4 = 8. Find the shear stress for F = 50 N.

  1. Spring index: C = D/d = 32/4 = 8.
  2. Wahl factor: KW = (4 times 8 − 1)/(4 times 8 − 4) + 0.615/8 = 31/28 + 0.0769 = 1.1071 + 0.0769 = 1.184.
  3. Plain stress: 8 times 50 times 32/(pi times 43) = 12800/(pi times 64) = 12800/201.06 = 63.66 MPa.
  4. Corrected stress: tau = 1.184 times 63.66 = 75.38 MPa.

Key ideaStress in a spring is always found with the Wahl factor in a careful design. The spring index C controls the factor: a small C (a tight coil with thick wire) gives a large correction, and a large C gives a small one.

GATE loves thisWatch the units. The stress comes out in MPa only when F is in newtons and the diameters are in millimetres. A common slip is to leave D in metres and d in millimetres.

What happens to the Wahl factor when the spring index C gets very large?

It approaches 1. The term (4C - 1)/(4C - 4) tends to 1 and the term 0.615/C tends to 0, so the correction becomes small. A large coil compared with the wire is nearly a straight wire in torsion.

What is the spring index of a spring with D = 24 mm and d = 3 mm?

C = 24/3 = 8.

Springs in series and in parallel

Designers often combine springs. There are two simple arrangements, and their rules are different.

Parallel springs sit side by side and are pressed together by the same plate. Each spring has the same deflection, and the forces add up. Think of two people pushing a box together. Their pushes add. The total stiffness is the sum.

Parallel: keq = k1 + k2

Series springs are one after another, like links in a chain. The same force passes through each spring, and the deflections add up. The total is softer than either spring alone, because the deflections add. Notice that it is the flexibilities (1/k) that add, not the stiffnesses.

Series: 1/keq = 1/k1 + 1/k2, so keq = k1 k2/(k1 + k2)

Worked example, series. Two springs with k1 = 6 N/mm and k2 = 6 N/mm are placed in series.

  1. Series formula: keq = 6 times 6/(6 + 6) = 36/12 = 3 N/mm.
  2. Check: the series value is less than either spring alone, which is correct.

Worked example, parallel. The same two springs in parallel give keq = 6 + 6 = 12 N/mm. The arrangement makes the mechanism four times as stiff as the series arrangement, because the two springs share the load.

TrapDo not add stiffnesses for springs in series. A common mistake is to write keq = k1 + k2 for a chain. That formula is only for parallel springs.

Key ideaParallel adds stiffness, series adds flexibility. Remember this one contrast and you can solve most combination questions.

Two springs of 4 N/mm and 12 N/mm are in series. What is the equivalent stiffness?

1/k = 1/4 + 1/12 = 3/12 + 1/12 = 4/12, so k = 3 N/mm.

Why is the series combination softer than either spring?

Because the deflections of the two springs add up. Each spring moves, and the total movement is their sum, so the same force gives a larger total deflection.

Energy stored in a spring

A stretched or squeezed spring holds energy, like a drawn bow. The energy is stored because work was done to deform it. When you let go, the spring gives back that energy. This is why springs are used in clocks, toy cars and door closers.

The force is not constant while the spring moves. It starts at zero and grows in proportion to the deflection. So the energy is the area under a triangle on a force-deflection graph. That area gives the famous half factor.

U = (1/2) F delta = (1/2) k delta2 = F2/(2k)

Here U is the energy in N mm (or joules if you use metres), F is the final force, delta is the final deflection and k is the spring rate. The three forms are equal, and you choose the one that matches the data you have. If you know F and k, use F squared over 2k. If you know delta and k, use half k delta squared.

Worked example. A spring with k = 7.8125 N/mm is compressed by a force F = 50 N.

  1. Find the deflection: delta = F/k = 50/7.8125 = 6.4 mm.
  2. Energy: U = (1/2) times 50 times 6.4 = 160 N mm.
  3. Check with the other formula: (1/2) times 7.8125 times 6.4 squared = 0.5 times 7.8125 times 40.96 = 160 N mm. The two answers agree.

TrapDo not use F times delta for the energy. That is twice the energy, because the force started at zero. The factor one half is always there for a linear spring.

A spring with k = 2 N/mm is stretched by 10 mm. How much energy is stored?

U = (1/2) times 2 times 10 squared = 100 N mm.

Why is the energy formula not F times delta?

Because the force rises from zero to its final value as the spring moves. The average force is half the final force, which gives the factor one half.

Natural frequency of a spring-mass system

Hang a mass on a spring, pull it down and let go. It bounces up and down. The number of bounces per second is set by the stiffness of the spring and the mass. This rate is the natural frequency. A stiff spring with a light mass bounces fast. A soft spring with a heavy mass bounces slowly. Think of a child on a swing: a short swing goes back and forth quickly, a long swing slowly.

omegan = square root of (k/m) rad/s
fn = omegan/(2 pi) Hz

Here k is the stiffness in N/m, m is the mass in kg, omegan is the angular natural frequency in radians per second, and fn is the natural frequency in hertz (cycles per second). The units must be consistent. If k is in N/mm, multiply by 1000 to get N/m before you use the formula with kg.

Worked example. A spring with k = 7.8125 N/mm carries a mass of m = 2 kg.

  1. Convert the stiffness to N/m: k = 7.8125 times 1000 = 7812.5 N/m.
  2. Angular frequency: omegan = square root of (7812.5/2) = square root of 3906.25 = 62.5 rad/s.
  3. Frequency in hertz: fn = 62.5/(2 pi) = 62.5/6.2832 = 9.947 Hz.

Key ideaStiffer springs and lighter masses give higher natural frequencies. Doubling the stiffness raises the frequency by a factor of the square root of 2, which is about 1.41.

A mass of 1 kg is on a spring of 1000 N/m. What is omegan?

omegan = square root of (1000/1) = 31.62 rad/s.

If the mass is quadrupled, how does the natural frequency change?

It is divided by the square root of 4, which is 2. The frequency halves.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original, written in the GATE style, and they are not copied from any real exam paper. Try the "How to think about it" steps yourself before you read the answer.

Spring rate from geometry

A steel helical spring has G = 80000 MPa, wire diameter d = 2 mm, mean coil diameter D = 16 mm and n = 8 active coils. What is its spring rate, in N/mm, to three decimal places? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The stiffness k in N/mm.
  2. Which idea applies. k = G d4/(8 D3 n).
  3. Work it out. d4 = 16. D3 = 4096. Numerator: 80000 times 16 = 1280000. Denominator: 8 times 4096 times 8 = 262144. So k = 1280000/262144 = 4.883 N/mm.
  4. Check. The wire here is thinner than in the worked example, so the spring should be softer than 7.8 N/mm. The answer 4.88 is smaller, as expected.

Answer: 4.883 N/mm.

Energy in a compressed spring

A spring has a stiffness of 4 N/mm. A force of 20 N compresses it. How much energy, in N mm, is stored in the spring? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The strain energy U.
  2. Which idea applies. U = (1/2) F delta, with delta = F/k.
  3. Work it out. delta = 20/4 = 5 mm. Then U = (1/2) times 20 times 5 = 50 N mm.
  4. Check. Using U = (1/2) k delta squared gives (1/2) times 4 times 25 = 50 N mm. The two agree.

Answer: 50 N mm.

Statements about springs

Which of the following statements about springs are correct? Select all that apply. (MSQ)

  • (A) Doubling the wire diameter makes a helical spring about 16 times stiffer, with other things equal.
  • (B) Two springs in series have a combined stiffness equal to the sum of their stiffnesses.
  • (C) The Wahl factor is always greater than 1 for a helical spring.
  • (D) The energy stored in a linear spring is F times delta, with no factor of one half.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true. More than one may be correct.
  2. Which idea applies. k depends on d to the fourth power. Series adds flexibilities. The Wahl factor adds a correction. Energy carries the factor one half.
  3. Work it out. (A) True: 2 to the fourth power is 16. (B) False: in series, the reciprocals add, not the stiffnesses. (C) True: the factor is above 1 for any practical C. (D) False: the energy is one half of F times delta.
  4. Check. Series springs are softer than either spring, so their combined stiffness must be less than the sum. That confirms (B) is wrong.

Answer: (A) and (C) are correct.

Equivalent stiffness of series springs

Two springs with stiffnesses 6 N/mm and 6 N/mm are placed in series. A second pair of the same springs is placed in parallel with the first combination. What is the total stiffness, in N/mm? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The total stiffness of a series pair in parallel with another series pair.
  2. Which idea applies. First combine each series pair with 1/k = 1/k1 + 1/k2. Then add the two parallel branches.
  3. Work it out. Each series pair gives 3 N/mm, as computed earlier. Two such branches in parallel add: 3 + 3 = 6 N/mm.
  4. Check. The total must be larger than one branch alone (3) and smaller than four springs in parallel (24). The value 6 is in that range.

Answer: 6 N/mm.

Natural frequency of a light spring-mass system

A spring with stiffness 5000 N/m carries a mass of 1.25 kg. What is the natural frequency, in hertz, to two decimal places? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The natural frequency fn in Hz.
  2. Which idea applies. omegan = square root of (k/m), then fn = omegan/(2 pi).
  3. Work it out. k/m = 5000/1.25 = 4000. Square root: omegan = 63.25 rad/s. Divide by 2 pi = 6.2832: fn = 10.07 Hz.
  4. Check. A little over 10 cycles per second is a sensible value for a light mass on a moderate spring.

Answer: 10.07 Hz.

Number of active coils for a target stiffness

A helical spring must have a stiffness of 10 N/mm. Its material has G = 80000 MPa, the wire diameter is d = 3 mm and the mean coil diameter is D = 24 mm. How many active coils, to two decimal places, does the spring need? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The number of active coils n.
  2. Which idea applies. Rearrange k = G d4/(8 D3 n) to get n = G d4/(8 D3 k).
  3. Work it out. d4 = 81. D3 = 13824. Numerator: 80000 times 81 = 6480000. Denominator: 8 times 13824 times 10 = 1105920. So n = 6480000/1105920 = 5.86.
  4. Check. Substitute back: k = 80000 times 81/(8 times 13824 times 5.86) = 10.0 N/mm. The check works. In practice a designer would round up to a whole or half coil, but the calculation gives 5.86.

Answer: 5.86 active coils.

Corrected shear stress in a spring

A helical spring has wire diameter d = 3 mm and mean coil diameter D = 24 mm. It carries a force F = 40 N. Find the corrected shear stress in the wire using the Wahl factor, in MPa, to one decimal place. (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The corrected shear stress tau.
  2. Which idea applies. First C = D/d, then KW, then tau = KW 8 F D/(pi d3).
  3. Work it out. C = 24/3 = 8. KW = 31/28 + 0.615/8 = 1.184. Plain stress: 8 times 40 times 24/(pi times 27) = 7680/84.82 = 90.53 MPa. Corrected: 1.184 times 90.53 = 107.2 MPa.
  4. Check. The correction raised the stress, as it should. The answer is about 18 percent above the plain value.

Answer: 107.2 MPa.

Static sag and natural frequency of a hanging mass

A mass of 3 kg hangs from a spring of stiffness 12 kN/m. Take g = 9.81 m/s2. (a) How far, in mm, does the spring stretch under the weight? (b) What is the natural frequency of the system in hertz? Give the frequency to two decimal places. (NAT: enter the frequency.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The static stretch, which is useful for part (a), and the frequency for part (b).
  2. Which idea applies. Static stretch: delta = mg/k. Frequency: fn = (1/(2 pi)) square root of (k/m).
  3. Work it out. Weight = 3 times 9.81 = 29.43 N. Stretch = 29.43/12000 = 0.002453 m = 2.45 mm. Frequency: k/m = 12000/3 = 4000, square root = 63.25 rad/s, and fn = 63.25/6.2832 = 10.07 Hz.
  4. Check. The stretch is small, about 2.5 mm, which is plausible for a stiff spring carrying 29 N. The frequency matches the previous practice problem, since the stiffness-to-mass ratio is 4000 in both.

Answer: (a) about 2.45 mm; (b) 10.07 Hz.

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Spring stiffness (NAT, 2 marks)

A helical spring has wire diameter 5 mm, mean coil diameter 40 mm, 10 active coils, and G = 80 GPa. Find the stiffness in N/mm, correct to 3 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. k = Gd⁴/(8D³n).
  2. Which idea applies. Standard helical spring formula.
  3. Work it out. 80000 · 625/(8 · 64000 · 10) = 9.766 N/mm.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
print("%.3f N/mm" % (80e3 * 5 ** 4 / (8 * 40 ** 3 * 10)))

9.766 N/mm

Answer: about 9.766 N/mm.

Exam-level 2: Spring stress with Wahl factor (NAT, 2 marks)

For the same spring under 300 N, find the maximum shear stress using the Wahl factor, correct to 1 decimal place.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. τ = Kw8FD/(πd³).
  2. Which idea applies. C = D/d = 8, Kw = (4C − 1)/(4C − 4) + 0.615/C.
  3. Work it out. Kw = 31/28 + 0.0769 = 1.184. Base stress = 8 · 300 · 40/(π · 125) = 244.5 MPa. τ = 289.4 MPa.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
import math
C = 8; K = (4 * C - 1) / (4 * C - 4) + 0.615 / C
print("K = %.3f, tau = %.1f MPa" % (K, K * 8 * 300 * 40 / (math.pi * 125)))

K = 1.184, tau = 289.4 MPa

Answer: about 289.4 MPa.

Exam-level 3: Springs in series and parallel (NAT, 1 mark)

Springs of 10 N/mm and 15 N/mm are in series, and that pair is in parallel with a 4 N/mm spring. Find the combined stiffness.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Equivalent stiffness.
  2. Which idea applies. Series: 1/k = 1/k1 + 1/k2. Parallel: add.
  3. Work it out. Series 6. Plus 4 = 10 N/mm.
  4. Check. Springs combine like capacitors.

Answer: 10 N/mm.

Summary

Here are the one-line meanings of the main ideas on this page.

Spring
An elastic element that stores energy and returns to its shape.
Helical spring
A wire wound into a coil and loaded along its axis.
Wire diameter d
The thickness of the spring wire, in mm.
Mean coil diameter D
The average diameter of the coil, in mm.
Active coils n
The number of coils free to deflect.
Spring index C
D divided by d, usually between 4 and 12.
Spring rate k
Force per unit deflection, in N/mm.
Shear modulus G
Stiffness of the material in shear, about 80000 MPa for steel.
Wahl factor
The correction, above 1, that raises the calculated shear stress in the wire.
Series springs
Springs carrying the same force, with flexibilities that add.
Parallel springs
Springs sharing the same deflection, with stiffnesses that add.
Strain energy U
Energy stored in a spring, equal to one half F times delta.
Natural frequency
The free vibration frequency of a spring-mass system.
Angular frequency omegan
The natural frequency in radians per second, square root of k/m.
Hertz
One cycle per second, the unit of frequency in hertz.

Remember these

  1. k = G d4/(8 D3 n). Wire diameter has the strongest effect, to the fourth power.
  2. Use the Wahl factor for spring shear stress. It is above 1, and about 1.18 for C = 8.
  3. Parallel springs add their stiffnesses. Series springs add their flexibilities.
  4. Stored energy is one half F times delta, never F times delta.
  5. Convert k to N/m before using the natural frequency formula with kg.

Vibrations: free & forced vibration of single DOF systems

Vibration is the back and forth motion of a system around a rest position. This page explains how a single mass on a spring moves when it is left alone (free vibration) and when a repeating force keeps pushing it (forced vibration), including natural frequency, steady-state amplitude and resonance.

The idea in plain words

Pull a ruler to one side, hold it, and let go. It swings back and forth, and the swings slowly die away. Or push a child on a swing. If you push at the right rhythm, the swing rises higher and higher. Both examples are vibrations: a system that moves to and fro about a rest position.

The simplest model is a mass on a spring. The spring pulls the mass back towards its rest position, and the mass overshoots, so it swings back and forth. Every real machine, car, bridge and robot arm has parts that vibrate in this way. Engineers want to know two things: how fast the system naturally wants to swing, and how big the swings become when something keeps pushing it.

In GATE, this topic is important in the Robotics and Automation (RA) paper, where robot links and mechanisms must avoid resonance. The ideas on this page are the foundation for damping, vibration isolation and critical speeds of shafts, which come later.

Key terms

Each term has a plain meaning first, then a precise definition.

Vibration
Repeated motion of a system about a rest (equilibrium) position.
Degree of freedom (DOF)
An independent way in which a system can move. A single mass moving along a line has one DOF. Plain meaning: one direction of motion.
Mass (m)
How hard it is to change the motion of the object, in kg.
Stiffness (k)
How strongly a spring pushes back, in N/m. A stiff spring needs a big force for each metre of stretch.
Displacement (x)
The distance of the mass from its rest position, in metres.
Free vibration
Motion after the system is disturbed, with no outside force acting.
Forced vibration
Motion caused by an outside force that keeps acting.
Natural frequency (ωn)
The angular frequency at which a free undamped system swings, in rad/s. ωn = √(k/m).
Natural frequency in hertz (fn)
Cycles per second, fn = ωn/(2π).
Period (T)
Time for one full cycle, T = 1/fn seconds.
Amplitude (X)
The largest displacement from the rest position.
Harmonic force
A force that varies as a sine or cosine of time, F = F0 cos ωt.
Forcing frequency (ω)
The angular frequency of the outside force, in rad/s.
Frequency ratio (r)
The forcing frequency divided by the natural frequency, r = ω/ωn.
Steady-state response
The motion that remains after the initial transient has died away, when the forced system repeats at the forcing frequency.
Resonance
The condition where the forcing frequency is close to the natural frequency, so the amplitude becomes large.
Damping ratio (ζ)
A number that measures how much energy is lost each cycle. It is introduced here and explored fully on the damping page.

TrapAngular frequency (ω) is in rad/s, while frequency (f) is in hertz. The two differ by the factor 2π. Most GATE formulas use ω, so convert a given f before you use the formula.

The mass and spring model, and Newton's law

Why start with one mass and one spring

Many vibrating systems can be simplified to a single mass moving along a line. A car's body on its suspension springs, a machine on its mounts, and a pendulum for small swings all behave like this. The single mass model is the first step that every vibration problem uses.

        rest position (x = 0)
              |
   |----/\/\/\/----[ mass m ]----> x (positive to the right)
   wall      spring k

   The spring force on the mass is -k x (it always pulls back to x = 0).

Newton's second law

Newton's second law says that the net force on a mass equals its mass times its acceleration. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, which is written as x'' (the second derivative of x with respect to time). For the spring above, the only force is the spring force -kx, so

m x'' = -k x, or m x'' + k x = 0

Read each symbol. m is the mass, k is the stiffness, x is the displacement and x'' is the acceleration. The minus sign means the force points back towards the rest position. This equation is called the equation of motion. Its solution tells us the position of the mass at every time.

Key ideaThe force always pushes the mass back towards its rest position. That push is the reason the mass swings, and the mass and stiffness decide how fast.

Self-check: in m x'' + k x = 0, what happens to the force if x is negative?

The force -kx is positive, so the force points to the right, back towards zero. The spring always pushes towards the rest position.

Self-check: why do we write the equation as m x'' + k x = 0 instead of m x'' = -k x?

They are the same equation. Moving all terms to one side makes it easier to find the solution, because the form A x'' + B x = 0 is the standard linear form.

Natural frequency of a free undamped system

The everyday idea

Deriving the natural frequency

Try the solution x = cos ωnt. Its second derivative is x'' = -ωn² cos ωnt. Substitute into m x'' + k x = 0:

m(-ωn²) cos ωnt + k cos ωnt = 0

Divide by cos ωnt, which is not zero in general. Then -mωn² + k = 0, so

ωn = √(k/m) rad/s

The natural frequency in hertz is fn = ωn/(2π), and the period is T = 1/fn = 2π/ωn.

Worked example

A mass of 2 kg is held by a spring of stiffness 800 N/m. Find the natural frequency in rad/s, in hertz and the period.

Step 1. Angular natural frequency: ωn = √(800/2) = √400 = 20 rad/s.

Step 2. Frequency in hertz: fn = 20/(2π) = 20/6.2832 = 3.183 Hz.

Step 3. Period: T = 1/3.183 = 0.314 s, which is also 2π/20.

Step 4. Check: a stiffer spring should give a higher frequency. The formula gives a higher value when k increases, which is correct.

import math

m, k = 2.0, 800.0
wn = math.sqrt(k / m)
fn = wn / (2 * math.pi)
T = 1 / fn

print(f"omega_n = {wn:.2f} rad/s")
print(f"f_n = {fn:.3f} Hz")
print(f"period = {T:.3f} s")
omega_n = 20.00 rad/s
f_n = 3.183 Hz
period = 0.314 s

TrapDo not stop at ωn when the question asks for frequency in hertz. Divide by 2π. Likewise, if a question gives a frequency in rpm, convert it to rad/s before using it with ω.

Self-check: the mass is doubled and k stays the same. How does ωn change?

It falls by a factor of √2, because ωn = √(k/m) and the mass is inside the square root.

Self-check: a mass hangs at rest on a spring and stretches it by a static deflection δ. Is there a shortcut for ωn?

Yes. At rest, mg = kδ, so k/m = g/δ, and ωn = √(g/δ). This is a common GATE shortcut when the static deflection is given.

Free motion: amplitude, period and phase

The general solution

Any solution of m x'' + k x = 0 can be written as a combination of cosine and sine:

x(t) = A cos ωnt + B sin ωnt

Here A and B are constants found from the starting conditions. The constant A is the displacement at t = 0, and the constant B depends on the starting velocity. Both terms repeat every period T.

Amplitude and phase

A cosine plus a sine of the same frequency is a single cosine shifted in time. We write it as

x(t) = X cos(ωnt - φ), where X = √(A² + B²) and tan φ = B/A

X is the amplitude (the largest displacement) and φ is the phase angle, which shows where the cycle starts. The phase angle depends on how the motion was started.

Worked example: release from rest

The same mass of 2 kg and spring of 800 N/m is pulled 5 mm from rest and released. Write the motion.

Step 1. Starting conditions: x(0) = 0.005 m and velocity v(0) = 0.

Step 2. From x(0) = A cos 0 + B sin 0 = A, so A = 0.005 m.

Step 3. Velocity: v = dx/dt = -Aωn sin ωnt + Bωn cos ωnt. At t = 0, v = Bωn = 0, so B = 0.

Step 4. The motion is x(t) = 0.005 cos 20t. The amplitude is 5 mm, the phase angle is 0, and the motion repeats every 0.314 s.

Key ideaStarting from rest gives a pure cosine, so the phase is zero. Starting from the rest position with a speed gives a sine, and the amplitude then depends on both the displacement and the speed.

Self-check: if the mass starts at x = 0 with speed 0.1 m/s, what is the amplitude?

At t = 0, x = 0 so A = 0, and v = Bωn = 0.1, so B = 0.1/20 = 0.005 m. The amplitude is X = 0.005 m, or 5 mm.

Self-check: does the amplitude of a free undamped motion ever decrease?

No. Without damping, energy stays in the spring and mass, so the amplitude stays constant.

Forced vibration with a harmonic force

The equation with an outside force

Now let an outside force push the mass. A common case is a harmonic force F = F0 cos ωt, such as the out-of-balance force of a motor running at speed ω. The equation of motion becomes

m x'' + k x = F0 cos ωt

The solution has two parts. The first part is the free vibration, which fades away when damping is present. The second part is the steady-state response. It repeats at the forcing frequency ω. We look for it in the form x = X cos ωt.

The steady-state amplitude

Substitute x = X cos ωt. Then x'' = -ω²X cos ωt, and the equation gives X(k - mω²) = F0. Divide the top and bottom by k and use ωn² = k/m:

X = (F0/k) / (1 - r²), where r = ω/ωn

The quantity F0/k is the static deflection, the displacement the force would cause if it were applied slowly. The factor 1/(1 - r²) is the magnification factor. It tells us how much the forced motion is larger than the static deflection.

Key ideaForced amplitude = static deflection × magnification factor, where the magnification factor is 1/(1 - r²) in the undamped case.

Worked example

A machine of mass 10 kg sits on a support of stiffness 10,000 N/m. An unbalance gives a force of amplitude F0 = 100 N at ω = 20 rad/s. Find the amplitude of the steady-state motion.

Step 1. Natural frequency: ωn = √(10000/10) = √1000 = 31.62 rad/s.

Step 2. Frequency ratio: r = 20/31.62 = 0.632, so r² = 400/1000 = 0.4.

Step 3. Static deflection: F0/k = 100/10000 = 0.01 m = 10 mm.

Step 4. Amplitude: X = 10 mm / (1 - 0.4) = 10/0.6 = 16.67 mm.

Step 5. Check: r is less than 1, so the amplitude is larger than the static deflection (16.67 mm is bigger than 10 mm), which is correct.

import math

m, k, F0 = 10.0, 10000.0, 100.0
wn = math.sqrt(k / m)
w = 20.0
r = w / wn

print(f"r^2 = {r * r:.3f}")
X = (F0 / k) / (1 - r * r)
print(f"X = {X * 1000:.2f} mm")
print(f"magnification = {1 / (1 - r * r):.3f}")
r^2 = 0.400
X = 16.67 mm
magnification = 1.667
Self-check: when the forcing frequency is very small (r is near 0), what is the amplitude?

The magnification is about 1, so the amplitude is approximately the static deflection F0/k. The force acts almost like a slowly applied load.

Self-check: what happens to the sign of X when r is greater than 1?

The denominator 1 - r² becomes negative. The response is 180 degrees out of phase with the force, which means the mass moves opposite to the force.

Steady-state amplitude with damping and the frequency response

Adding a damper

A real system always loses some energy, usually in a dashpot (a piston in oil), in rubber, or in friction. We model this as a force that opposes velocity, c x'. The equation becomes

m x'' + c x' + k x = F0 cos ωt

Here c is the damping coefficient in N s/m. Engineers use the damping ratio ζ, defined as

ζ = c / (2 m ωn)

With damping, the steady-state amplitude becomes

X = (F0/k) / √((1 - r²)² + (2ζr)²)

Look at the new term (2ζr)² under the square root. It stops the amplitude from becoming infinite at r = 1. The phase angle between the force and the response is given by tan φ = 2ζr/(1 - r²).

A worked example with damping

Use the same machine, with F0/k = 10 mm, and damping ratio ζ = 0.1. Find the amplitude at r = 1, which is resonance.

Step 1. At r = 1: 1 - r² = 0, so the first term under the root vanishes.

Step 2. Under the root we are left with (2ζr)² = (2 × 0.1 × 1)² = 0.04, and its square root is 0.2.

Step 3. Amplitude: X = 10 mm / 0.2 = 50 mm. Resonance gives five times the static deflection.

Step 4. Check: without damping the amplitude would be infinite. The damping limits it to 50 mm, which is a finite and sensible value.

import math

F0_over_k = 0.01   # static deflection in m
zeta = 0.1

for r in (0.5, 1.0, 2.0):
    X = F0_over_k / math.sqrt((1 - r * r) ** 2 + (2 * zeta * r) ** 2)
    print(f"r = {r:.1f}: X = {X * 1000:.2f} mm")
r = 0.5: X = 13.22 mm
r = 1.0: X = 50.00 mm
r = 2.0: X = 3.30 mm
Self-check: with ζ = 0.1, is the amplitude at r = 2 bigger or smaller than the static deflection?

Smaller. At r = 2 the output is 3.30 mm, which is less than 10 mm. Above r = 1 the mass cannot follow the force, so the amplitude falls.

Self-check: why is the damper useful even though it makes the amplitude at r = 2 a little bigger than without damping?

Damping limits the peak at resonance, which is often the dangerous case. The small increase far from resonance is a trade-off that the damping page covers in detail.

Resonance and the frequency response

What resonance means

Resonance happens when the forcing frequency matches the natural frequency, so r = 1. The force arrives at exactly the right moment in each cycle to add to the motion. Think of pushing a swing at the same point in each cycle. Each push adds energy, and the swing goes higher and higher.

GATE loves thisAt r = 1 an undamped system has an infinite steady-state amplitude in theory. In practice damping, nonlinearity or stopping the forcing limits it. GATE often asks which frequency causes the largest amplitude, and the answer is the natural frequency.

The frequency response table

The table below shows the undamped magnification factor for several values of r. The phase column shows where the response sits relative to the force.

r = ω/ωnMagnification 1/|1 - r²|Phase of responseWhat happens
0.51.330 degrees (in phase)Slightly larger than static
0.82.780 degreesGrowing towards resonance
1.0infinite in theory90 degreesResonance

2.00.33180 degreesMuch smaller than static

TrapPhase changes by 180 degrees as r passes 1 in the undamped case, not 90 degrees. The 90 degrees at exactly r = 1 is the midpoint for a damped system. Use the rule: in phase below resonance, out of phase above it.

Worked example: a machine running above resonance

A motor of mass 100 kg is mounted on springs with a natural frequency of 10 Hz. It runs at 1200 rpm, and the unbalance force is 500 N. Find the magnification factor in the undamped case.

Step 1. Forcing frequency: 1200 rpm = 20 Hz, so ω = 2π × 20 = 125.66 rad/s.

Step 2. Natural frequency: ωn = 2π × 10 = 62.83 rad/s.

Step 3. r = 20/10 = 2, so r² = 4.

Step 4. Magnification 1/|1 - 4| = 1/3 = 0.333. The response is one third of the static deflection and is out of phase with the force.

Step 5. Check: the machine runs well above its natural frequency, so the response is small. This is the reason many machines are designed to run above resonance.

Self-check: which frequency ratio gives the largest undamped response?

r = 1, which is resonance. For any r other than 1 the magnification is finite.

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original GATE-style problems for practice, not real past paper questions. Work each one on paper before you read the answer.

Natural frequency of a small block

A block of mass 5 kg is attached to a spring of stiffness 2000 N/m. Find the natural frequency in rad/s and in hertz.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Two frequencies, one in rad/s and one in Hz.
  2. Which idea applies. ωn = √(k/m), then fn = ωn/(2π).
  3. Work it out. ωn = √(2000/5) = √400 = 20 rad/s. fn = 20/(2π) = 3.18 Hz.
  4. Check. The same answer as the worked example, because the mass and stiffness are scaled to give the same ratio. 2000/5 = 800/2.

Answer: 20 rad/s, which is 3.18 Hz.

Machine on mounts

A machine of mass 40 kg rests on mounts with a total stiffness of 90 kN/m. Find its natural frequency in hertz (numerical answer).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. fn in hertz.
  2. Which idea applies. Convert kN/m to N/m, then use ωn = √(k/m).
  3. Work it out. k = 90000 N/m. ωn = √(90000/40) = √2250 = 47.43 rad/s. fn = 47.43/(2π) = 7.55 Hz.
  4. Check. A stiffer and heavier system gives a moderate frequency. 7.55 Hz is a reasonable value for a machine.
import math

m, k = 40.0, 90000.0
wn = math.sqrt(k / m)
print(f"f_n = {wn / (2 * math.pi):.2f} Hz")
f_n = 7.55 Hz

Answer: 7.55 Hz.

Which statements are true?

Consider these statements about a single degree of freedom system.

(A) In an undamped free system, the natural frequency depends only on mass and stiffness.
(B) Doubling the mass halves the natural frequency.
(C) In an undamped forced system with r greater than 1, the response is 180 degrees out of phase with the force.
(D) An undamped forced system has a finite amplitude at r = 1.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true (multiple selection).
  2. Which idea applies. ωn = √(k/m), and the magnification 1/(1 - r²) changes sign at r = 1.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true. (B) is false, because doubling m multiplies ωn by 1/√2, which is about 0.71. (C) is true, since 1 - r² is negative above resonance. (D) is false, because the denominator is zero at r = 1.
  4. Check. A quick number test: with m doubled, ωn = √(k/2m) = 0.707 √(k/m), which is not one half.

Answer: (A) and (C).

Forced amplitude of a light system

A mass of 2 kg is on a spring of stiffness 800 N/m with no damping. It is driven by a harmonic force of amplitude 40 N at 10 rad/s. Find the steady-state amplitude in millimetres (numerical answer).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The amplitude X in mm.
  2. Which idea applies. X = (F0/k)/(1 - r²) with r = ω/ωn.
  3. Work it out. ωn = 20 rad/s, so r = 0.5 and 1 - r² = 0.75. F0/k = 40/800 = 0.05 m. X = 0.05/0.75 = 0.0667 m = 66.67 mm.
  4. Check. Since r is below 1, X is a bit larger than the static deflection 50 mm, which fits with 66.67 mm.
import math

m, k, F0, w = 2.0, 800.0, 40.0, 10.0
wn = math.sqrt(k / m)
r = w / wn
X = (F0 / k) / (1 - r * r)
print(f"X = {X * 1000:.2f} mm")
X = 66.67 mm

Answer: 66.67 mm.

Amplitude of a motor on a stand

A motor of mass 100 kg runs at 1200 rpm. Its unbalance produces a harmonic force of amplitude 500 N. The natural frequency of the mounting is 10 Hz, and damping is negligible. Find the steady-state amplitude in micrometres (numerical answer, rounded to the nearest integer).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The amplitude X in micrometres.
  2. Which idea applies. First find k from ωn, then X = (F0/k)/|1 - r²|.
  3. Work it out. ωn = 2π × 10 = 62.83 rad/s, so k = m ωn² = 100 × 3947.8 = 394784 N/m. Forcing ω = 2π × 20 = 125.66 rad/s, so r = 2 and r² = 4. F0/k = 500/394784 = 0.0012665 m. X = 0.0012665/3 = 0.00042217 m = 422 micrometres.
  4. Check. Since r = 2 is above resonance, X should be smaller than the static deflection, and 422 micrometres is smaller than 1267 micrometres.

Answer: 422 micrometres.

Peak amplitude with damping

A single degree of freedom system has a static deflection F0/k = 2 mm under the harmonic force amplitude. Its damping ratio is ζ = 0.04. Find the steady-state amplitude at resonance (r = 1), in millimetres. Then state whether the amplitude at r = 1 is larger or smaller than at r = 0.5 with the same damping.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The resonance amplitude and a comparison.
  2. Which idea applies. At r = 1 the damped amplitude is X = (F0/k)/(2ζ).
  3. Work it out. X = 2 mm/(2 × 0.04) = 2/0.08 = 25 mm. At r = 0.5 the denominator is √(0.75² + (0.04)²) = 0.7511, giving X = 2/0.7511 = 2.66 mm.
  4. Check. Resonance gives a much larger amplitude than r = 0.5, so the first is larger.
import math

d = 2.0          # F0/k in mm
zeta = 0.04
X_res = d / math.sqrt((1 - 1.0 ** 2) ** 2 + (2 * zeta * 1.0) ** 2)
X_half = d / math.sqrt((1 - 0.5 ** 2) ** 2 + (2 * zeta * 0.5) ** 2)
print(f"resonance X = {X_res:.1f} mm")
print(f"r = 0.5 X = {X_half:.2f} mm")
resonance X = 25.0 mm
r = 0.5 X = 2.66 mm

Answer: 25 mm at resonance, which is larger than 2.66 mm at r = 0.5.

Ratio of response with the forcing at twice the natural frequency

A machine is modelled as an undamped mass on a spring. Its natural frequency is 10 Hz. The machine is excited at 20 Hz by a harmonic force. Find the ratio of the steady-state amplitude to the static deflection, and state the phase relation between the response and the force.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The ratio X/(F0/k) and the phase.
  2. Which idea applies. r = ω/ωn = f/fn = 2, and the magnification factor is 1/|1 - r²|.
  3. Work it out. r = 2 so r² = 4 and 1 - r² = -3. The magnification is 1/|-3| = 1/3. Because 1 - r² is negative, the response is 180 degrees out of phase with the force.
  4. Check. A machine above resonance responds less than its static deflection. 1/3 is less than 1, which agrees.
from fractions import Fraction as F

fn, f = F(10), F(20)
r = f / fn
mag = 1 / abs(1 - r * r)
print(f"r = {r}")
print(f"magnification = {mag}")
print("sign of 1 - r^2 =", "negative" if 1 - r * r < 0 else "positive")
r = 2
magnification = 1/3
sign of 1 - r^2 = negative

Answer: the ratio is 1/3, and the response is 180 degrees out of phase with the force.

Exam-level 3: Forced response amplitude (NAT, 2 marks)

An undamped system with k = 4000 N/m and m = 10 kg is driven by F = 100 sin(10t) N. Find the steady amplitude in mm.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. X = (F0/k)/|1 − r²|.
  2. Which idea applies. r = ω/ωn.
  3. Work it out. r = 0.5. X = 0.025/0.75 = 0.0333 m = 33.3 mm.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
r = 10 / 20

33.3 mm

Answer: about 33.3 mm.

Summary

Vibration
Repeated motion of a system about its rest position.
Mass and spring model
A single mass attached to a spring, with one degree of freedom.
Equation of motion
m x'' + k x = 0 for a free undamped system.
Natural frequency
ωn = √(k/m) rad/s, the frequency of free undamped motion.
Frequency in hertz
fn = ωn/(2π), cycles per second.
Period
T = 2π/ωn seconds per cycle.
Amplitude and phase
x = X cos(ωnt - φ), with X the largest displacement.
Harmonic force
F = F0 cos ωt, an outside force that repeats.
Frequency ratio
r = ω/ωn, the forcing frequency over the natural frequency.
Static deflection
F0/k, the displacement a steady force would produce.
Magnification factor
1/(1 - r²) in the undamped case, the ratio of forced amplitude to static deflection.
Steady-state response
The forced motion that repeats at the forcing frequency after transients die out.
Resonance
The condition r = 1, where undamped amplitude is theoretically infinite.
Damping ratio
ζ = c/(2mωn), a measure of energy loss per cycle.
Damped amplitude
X = (F0/k)/√((1 - r²)² + (2ζr)²), finite at resonance.
Phase rule
The response is in phase with the force for r less than 1 and out of phase for r greater than 1, undamped.

Remember these

  • Natural frequency depends only on mass and stiffness in the undamped case.
  • Convert hertz to rad/s by multiplying by 2π before using ω formulas.
  • Forced amplitude equals static deflection times the magnification factor.
  • Resonance is at r = 1, and damping limits the peak.
  • Above resonance the response is out of phase with the force.
  • Machines are often run well above or well below their natural frequency.

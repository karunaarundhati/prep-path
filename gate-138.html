DC motors: principles & performance

A DC motor turns electrical energy into rotation. Its speed and torque follow a few simple equations, and GATE often asks you to use them to find speed, torque or efficiency from given numbers.

The idea in plain words

A DC motor is a machine that uses direct current (current that flows in one direction) to make a shaft turn. Most small robot joints, wheels and fans use one. Once you understand three or four equations, you can predict how fast it spins, how hard it pulls and how much energy it wastes.

Everyday analogy. Think of a playground roundabout pushed by children. The more children push, the faster it spins, but the more it spins, the harder it is for the children to keep pushing, because the roundabout pushes back. A DC motor behaves in the same way. The voltage you supply pushes the current in, the current makes torque, and the spinning coil makes its own opposing voltage that slows the current down.

Why it matters. A robot designer picks a motor by asking: will it turn fast enough, and will it give enough torque at that speed? The equations on this page answer both questions with numbers.

Where it appears in GATE. In the Robotics and Automation (RA) paper, DC motors appear under actuators. Expect numerical questions on back-EMF, speed, torque and efficiency, and conceptual questions on the types of DC motor and on speed control methods.

Key terms

Read this list now and come back to it when a word feels unfamiliar.

Armature
The rotating part of the motor that carries current. In a simple motor it is a coil of wire wound on an iron core.
Stator
The fixed outer part of the motor. It holds the magnets or the field winding.
Field
The magnetic field that the stator creates. Precisely: the magnetic flux Φ that crosses the air gap between stator and armature.
Armature resistance (Ra)
The resistance of the armature winding, in ohms. Current through it wastes energy as heat.
Armature current (Ia)
The current flowing through the armature, in amperes.
Supply voltage (V)
The voltage applied to the motor terminals, in volts.
Angular speed (ω)
How fast the shaft turns, in radians per second. Precisely: ω = 2πN/60, where N is the speed in revolutions per minute (rpm).
Radian
A unit of angle. One full turn is 2π radians, about 6.283 radians.
Torque (T)
The twisting effect the motor produces, in newton metres (N·m).
Lorentz force
The force on a current-carrying wire placed in a magnetic field. Precisely: F = B I L, where B is the field, I the current and L the wire length, with the force at right angles to both.
Back-EMF (Eb)
A voltage generated by the spinning armature that opposes the supply voltage. "EMF" stands for electromotive force, which here means voltage. Precisely: Eb = Ke ω.
Torque constant (Kt)
The torque produced per ampere of armature current. Precisely: T = Kt Ia.
Back-EMF constant (Ke)
The back-EMF per unit of speed. In SI units, Ke has the same numerical value as Kt.
Stall torque
The torque when the shaft is held still, so ω = 0.
No-load speed
The speed when the motor turns freely with no load, so the torque is almost zero.
Copper loss
Heat wasted in the winding resistance. Precisely: Ia2 Ra, in watts.
Efficiency (η)
The fraction of electrical input power that becomes mechanical output. Precisely: η = mechanical output / electrical input.

How a DC motor makes torque

Step 1: a wire in a magnetic field feels a force

Take a magnet and place a wire carrying current across its field. The wire is pushed sideways. This is the basic motor effect. The size of the push is given by the Lorentz force law:

F = B × I × L

Here F is the force in newtons, B is the magnetic flux density in tesla, I is the current in amperes, and L is the length of the wire in metres that sits inside the field. The force is at right angles to both the field and the wire.

In a motor, many turns of wire sit on a rotor. Each side of each turn is pushed in opposite directions, and the pushes make a turning effect. A commutator, a split ring with brushes, reverses the current as the coil turns, so that the push always acts to keep the rotor spinning the same way.

Step 2: torque is proportional to current

Add up all the forces on all the wires, multiply by the radius of the rotor, and you get the torque. For a fixed magnet, the torque is proportional to the armature current. We write this as:

T = Kt × Ia

Here T is the torque in N·m, Kt is the torque constant in N·m per ampere, and Ia is the armature current in amperes. Double the current and the torque doubles.

Example: a motor has Kt = 0.1 N·m/A. When the current is 4 A, the torque is 0.1 × 4 = 0.4 N·m.

Key ideaThe motor makes torque from current, and the current comes from the supply voltage minus the back-EMF. Everything else follows from that one loop.

Question: a motor has Kt = 0.05 N·m/A. What current gives 0.3 N·m?

Ia = T / Kt = 0.3 / 0.05 = 6 A.

Question: what happens to the torque if the magnet is made weaker?

Kt depends on the magnetic flux. A weaker field gives a smaller Kt, so the same current gives less torque.

Back-EMF and the speed equation

Why a spinning motor makes its own voltage

Here is a surprise. When the rotor spins, its wires cut across the magnetic field, and a voltage is induced in them. This is the same effect as a generator. The induced voltage pushes against the supply, so it is called back-EMF. The faster the motor spins, the bigger the back-EMF:

Eb = Ke × ω

Here Eb is the back-EMF in volts, Ke is the back-EMF constant in V·s/rad, and ω is the angular speed in rad/s.

Everyday analogy. Think of a tap of water pushing against a water wheel. The faster the wheel turns, the more it pushes back against the flow. The back-EMF is that push-back.

The armature circuit

The armature is a loop with a supply, a resistance and the back-EMF in series. Kirchhoff's voltage law says the supply voltage is shared between the resistance drop and the back-EMF:

V = Eb + Ia × Ra

Solve this for the armature current and you get the key equation:

Ia = (V - Ke ω) / Ra

When the motor starts from rest, ω = 0, so the back-EMF is zero and the current is V / Ra. This is a large current, which is why a motor draws a big surge when it starts.

When the motor is spinning fast, the back-EMF is almost equal to V, so the current is small. The motor settles at the speed where the current gives just enough torque for the load.

Worked example: speed from voltage and current

A motor has V = 24 V, Ra = 2 Ω, Ke = Kt = 0.1 (SI units). It carries Ia = 4 A.

  1. Back-EMF: Eb = V - Ia Ra = 24 - 4 × 2 = 24 - 8 = 16 V.
  2. Speed: ω = Eb / Ke = 16 / 0.1 = 160 rad/s.
  3. Convert to rpm: N = 60ω / (2π) = 60 × 160 / 6.2832, which is about 1527.9 rpm.
  4. Torque: T = Kt Ia = 0.1 × 4 = 0.4 N·m.
  5. Mechanical power: T × ω = 0.4 × 160 = 64 W.
  6. Electrical input: V × Ia = 24 × 4 = 96 W.

TrapSpeed in the back-EMF formula must be in rad/s, not rpm. If a question gives rpm, convert first with ω = 2πN/60. Using rpm directly gives a wrong answer by a factor of about 9.55.

Question: a motor has Ke = 0.2 V·s/rad and runs at 100 rad/s. What is its back-EMF?

Eb = Ke ω = 0.2 × 100 = 20 V.

Question: why does a motor draw a large current when it first switches on?

At start, ω = 0, so Eb = 0 and the current is V / Ra. The current drops as the motor speeds up and the back-EMF grows.

Torque-speed characteristic

Combining the two equations

Put T = Kt Ia into Ia = (V - Ke ω) / Ra. You get the relationship between torque and speed for a fixed supply voltage:

T = (Kt V / Ra) - (Kt Ke / Ra) × ω

This is a straight line. Its two important points are:

  • Stall torque (ω = 0): Tstall = Kt V / Ra. This is the largest torque the motor can give.
  • No-load speed (T = 0): ω0 = V / Ke. This is the fastest the motor can spin.

The line runs from the stall torque at zero speed down to zero torque at the no-load speed. So the torque decreases as the speed increases. This is the key picture to remember.

Worked example: the torque-speed line

Use V = 24 V, Ra = 2 Ω and Kt = Ke = 0.1. Find the stall torque and the no-load speed in rad/s.

  1. Stall torque: Tstall = 0.1 × 24 / 2 = 1.2 N·m.
  2. No-load speed: ω0 = 24 / 0.1 = 240 rad/s.
  3. Check with a point on the line: at ω = 120 rad/s, T = 1.2 - (0.1 × 0.1 / 2) × 120 = 1.2 - 0.6 = 0.6 N·m. Halfway in speed gives halfway in torque.
V = 24.0
Ra = 2.0
Kt = 0.1
Ke = 0.1

T_stall = Kt * V / Ra
w0 = V / Ke
print("Stall torque = %.1f N-m" % T_stall)
print("No-load speed = %.0f rad/s" % w0)
for w in (0, 60, 120, 180, 240):
    T = Kt * (V - Ke * w) / Ra
    print("At %3d rad/s, torque = %.2f N-m" % (w, T))

No-load speed = 240 rad/s
At   0 rad/s, torque = 1.20 N-m
At  60 rad/s, torque = 0.90 N-m
At 120 rad/s, torque = 0.60 N-m
At 180 rad/s, torque = 0.30 N-m
At 240 rad/s, torque = 0.00 N-m

GATE loves thisThe torque-speed line always slopes down, and it always starts at the stall torque on the torque axis and ends at the no-load speed on the speed axis. If a question gives you two points on the line, you can find the whole line and then read off any value you need.

Question: at what speed does the torque from the example above equal half the stall torque?

Half of 1.2 N·m is 0.6 N·m. From the table, 0.6 N·m occurs at 120 rad/s, which is half the no-load speed of 240 rad/s.

Question: does the stall torque depend on the armature resistance?

Yes. Tstall = Kt V / Ra, so a larger Ra gives a smaller stall torque. The no-load speed V / Ke does not depend on Ra.

Motor types

The equations above hold for every DC motor. What changes from type to type is how the magnetic field is made. That choice decides the shape of the torque-speed curve.

Permanent magnet DC (PMDC)

The field comes from permanent magnets fixed to the stator. The flux is fixed, so Kt and Ke are constant. The torque-speed line is straight, exactly as derived above. PMDC motors are small, cheap and common in robot joints, toys and fans.

      PMDC motor (cross section, simplified)

        N  +--------+  S
           |  rotor |          The stator magnets (N and S) set
           |   ( )  |          the field. The rotor carries the
        S  +--------+  N       armature coil and the commutator.

Series wound

The field winding is in series with the armature. The same current flows through both. When the load rises, the current rises, and the field gets stronger. The torque then grows faster than the current, and the speed falls a lot under load.

Series motors have very high starting torque, which makes them good for cranes and traction. A series motor must never run with no load, because the field becomes weak, the speed rises sharply, and the motor can destroy itself.

Shunt wound

The field winding is in parallel with the armature, across the supply. The field current is almost constant, so the flux is almost constant. A shunt motor therefore behaves like a PMDC motor. Its speed stays nearly constant as the load changes.

Separately excited

The field has its own supply, so the field current can be set independently of the armature. This makes it easy to control speed by changing the field, which is the topic of the next unit.

Question: which DC motor type should you avoid running with no load?

The series motor. With no load its current is small, so its field is weak, and its speed can rise very high.

Question: which type has the most constant speed as the load changes?

The PMDC or shunt motor, because their field flux stays almost constant.

Speed control and efficiency

Method 1: armature voltage control

Lower the supply voltage V and the no-load speed V / Ke falls. The torque-speed line moves down and to the left, parallel to itself, because its slope is unchanged. The torque the motor can give below a certain speed stays about the same. Engineers often make the voltage with pulse width modulation (PWM), which switches the supply on and off quickly.

With PWM, the average voltage is the duty cycle times the supply voltage. A 24 V supply switched on for half of each cycle gives an average of 0.5 × 24 = 12 V.

Method 2: field control

Weaken the field flux and Ke falls. For the same supply voltage, the no-load speed V / Ke rises. So the motor spins faster, but each ampere gives less torque, because Kt also falls. This method is used above the normal (base) speed of the motor, where the power stays roughly constant.

Method 3: armature resistance control

Add a resistor in series with the armature. The current falls and the speed falls. The extra resistor turns energy into heat, so this method wastes power. It is rarely used in modern drives.

TrapField control raises the speed, while armature voltage control lowers it. Students often mix up the two. Remember: weaker field means faster, lower voltage means slower.

Efficiency and power losses

The electrical power going into the motor is V × Ia. Part of it is wasted as copper loss in the armature resistance, and the rest becomes mechanical output power T × ω. Energy must balance:

V × Ia = T × ω + Ia2 × Ra

Efficiency is the useful output divided by the electrical input: η = T × ω / (V × Ia). Write it as a percentage by multiplying by 100.

Worked example: efficiency

The same motor (V = 24 V, Ra = 2 Ω, Ke = Kt = 0.1) now carries Ia = 3 A.

  1. Electrical input: 24 × 3 = 72 W.
  2. Copper loss: 32 × 2 = 9 × 2 = 18 W.
  3. Back-EMF: 24 - 3 × 2 = 18 V, so the speed is 18 / 0.1 = 180 rad/s.
  4. Torque: 0.1 × 3 = 0.3 N·m. Mechanical output: 0.3 × 180 = 54 W.
  5. Balance check: 54 + 18 = 72 W, which equals the input. Good.
  6. Efficiency: 54 / 72 = 0.75, which is 75 percent.

Question: a motor has input 100 W and copper loss 16 W. What is its output power and efficiency?

Output = 100 - 16 = 84 W, so the efficiency is 84 / 100 = 84 percent.

Question: why does efficiency fall when a motor is loaded very lightly or very heavily?

At light load the output is small, but copper and friction losses remain. At very heavy load the current is large, and the copper loss grows with the square of the current, so the efficiency drops again.

Solved GATE-style problems

No-load speed

A DC motor runs on 12 V. Its back-EMF constant is Ke = 0.05 V·s/rad. Find its no-load speed in rad/s.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The no-load speed in rad/s.
  2. Which idea applies. At no load the torque is almost zero, so the back-EMF nearly equals the supply voltage. The no-load speed is ω0 = V / Ke.
  3. Work it out. ω0 = 12 / 0.05 = 240 rad/s.
  4. Check. Multiply back: 0.05 × 240 = 12 V, which equals the supply. Good.
V = 12.0
Ke = 0.05
print("No-load speed = %.0f rad/s" % (V / Ke))
No-load speed = 240 rad/s

Answer: 240 rad/s.

Back-EMF and current at a given speed

A motor has Ke = 0.1 V·s/rad and Ra = 1 Ω. It runs at 100 rad/s from a 12 V supply. Find (a) the back-EMF and (b) the armature current.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Two numbers: back-EMF in volts and current in amperes.
  2. Which idea applies. Eb = Ke ω, and V = Eb + Ia Ra.
  3. Work it out. (a) Eb = 0.1 × 100 = 10 V. (b) Ia = (12 - 10) / 1 = 2 A.
  4. Check. The voltage across the resistance is 2 × 1 = 2 V, and 10 + 2 = 12 V. Good.

Ra = 1.0
V = 12.0
w = 100.0
Eb = Ke * w

print("Current = %.1f A" % Ia)

Current = 2.0 A

Answer: (a) 10 V. (b) 2 A.

Which statements about DC motors are true?

Consider the following statements about DC motors. Select all that apply.

(A) For a PMDC motor at fixed voltage, torque falls in a straight line as speed rises.
(B) Changing the armature resistance changes the no-load speed.
(C) A series motor gives a very high starting torque.
(D) Weakening the field, with the armature voltage fixed, makes the motor speed up.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which of the four statements are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. The torque-speed line, the no-load speed V / Ke, the motor types and field control.
  3. Work it out. (A) True, the line T = (Kt/Ra)(V - Ke ω) is straight. (B) False, the no-load speed V / Ke does not contain Ra. (C) True, series motors are used for high starting torque. (D) True, a weaker field lowers Ke, so the speed rises.
  4. Check. The only statement that mentions Ra and no-load speed together is (B), and the formula shows it is wrong.

Answer: (A), (C) and (D). Statement (B) is false.

Gearbox and speed conversion

A motor shaft turns at 1200 rpm and gives a torque of 0.5 N·m. It drives a wheel through an ideal gearbox with ratio 10:1 (the wheel turns 10 times slower and gives 10 times the torque). Find the wheel speed in rad/s, correct to two decimals.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The wheel speed in rad/s.
  2. Which idea applies. Convert rpm to rad/s with ω = 2πN/60, then divide by the gear ratio.
  3. Work it out. Motor speed: 2π × 1200 / 60 = 125.66 rad/s. Wheel speed: 125.66 / 10 = 12.57 rad/s.
  4. Check. The wheel turns 10 times slower, so its speed should be one tenth of the motor speed. 125.66 / 10 = 12.57. Good.

Torque and power at a given speed

A motor has V = 24 V, Ra = 2 Ω and Ke = Kt = 0.1 (SI units). It runs at 100 rad/s. Find the output torque in N·m and the mechanical output power in W.

Speed control statements

A DC motor is driven from a fixed supply. Consider the following statements about speed control. Select all that apply.

(A) Lowering the armature voltage moves the torque-speed line parallel to itself, towards lower speeds.
(B) With the armature voltage fixed, a weaker field raises the no-load speed.
(C) Armature resistance control is more efficient than armature voltage control.
(D) Armature voltage control can give roughly constant torque below the base speed.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which of the four statements are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. The torque-speed line T = (Kt/Ra)(V - Ke ω), the no-load speed V / Ke, and where power is lost in each method.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. Changing V changes the intercepts but not the slope, so the line shifts in parallel. (B) True. A weaker field lowers Ke, and V / Ke rises. (C) False. Armature resistance control wastes power as heat in the added resistor, so it is less efficient. (D) True. Below base speed the flux is kept at its normal value, so Kt is fixed and the torque capability is about constant.
  4. Check. Statement (C) is the only one that claims a method wastes less energy, and the resistor argument shows that is wrong.

Answer: (A), (B) and (D). Statement (C) is false.

Speed under PWM with a fixed load

A 24 V motor (Ke = Kt = 0.1, Ra = 2 Ω) is driven by PWM with a duty cycle of 50 percent. The average voltage is therefore 12 V. The load needs a torque of 0.2 N·m. Ignore switching losses. Find the speed in rad/s and the efficiency.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The steady speed in rad/s and the efficiency, with the supply taken as the average voltage.
  2. Which idea applies. Average voltage = duty cycle × V. Then the current from the torque, the back-EMF from the voltage equation, and the speed from Eb = Ke ω.
  3. Work it out. Ia = 0.2 / 0.1 = 2 A. Eb = 12 - 2 × 2 = 8 V. ω = 8 / 0.1 = 80 rad/s. Output = 0.2 × 80 = 16 W. Input = 12 × 2 = 24 W. Efficiency = 16 / 24 = 66.7 percent.
  4. Check. The copper loss is 22 × 2 = 8 W, and 16 + 8 = 24 W, which equals the input. Good.

Exam-level 3: Motor characteristics (MSQ, 2 marks)

Which are TRUE? (A) A series motor has high starting torque. (B) A series motor should not run without load. (C) A shunt motor has nearly constant speed. (D) Weakening the field of a shunt motor lowers its speed.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Standard DC motor behaviour.
  2. Which idea applies. N ∝ E/φ.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) It can overspeed dangerously. True. (C) True. (D) Lower φ raises speed. False.
  4. Check. Field weakening is the usual way to run above base speed.

Answer: (A), (B) and (C).

Summary

Each line gives the plain idea first, then the short definition.

Armature
The rotating winding that carries the current.
Lorentz force
Force on a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field, F = B I L.
Torque constant
Kt, the torque per ampere. T = Kt Ia.
Back-EMF
The voltage a spinning motor generates against the supply, Eb = Ke ω.
Back-EMF constant
Ke, the back-EMF per rad/s. In SI units equal to Kt.
Armature circuit
V = Eb + Ia Ra, the voltage balance of the armature loop.
rpm to rad/s
ω = 2πN / 60.
Stall torque
Torque at zero speed, Kt V / Ra.
No-load speed
V / Ke, the fastest speed with no load.
Torque-speed line
A straight line that falls from stall torque to zero torque at no-load speed.
PMDC motor
A DC motor with permanent magnets, so its flux and constants are fixed.
Series motor
Field in series with the armature. High starting torque, but dangerous with no load.
Shunt motor
Field in parallel with the supply. Nearly constant speed under load.
Armature voltage control
Changing V shifts the torque-speed line in parallel.
Field control
A weaker field raises the speed and lowers the torque per ampere.
Copper loss
Ia2 Ra, the heat lost in the armature winding.
Efficiency
Mechanical output divided by electrical input.

Remember these

  1. Torque comes from current, and back-EMF comes from speed.
  2. Use ω in rad/s in every back-EMF formula, and convert rpm first.
  3. Stall torque is the maximum torque, and the torque falls in a straight line as speed rises.
  4. Electrical power in equals mechanical power out plus copper loss.
  5. Weaker field means faster speed, and lower armature voltage means slower speed.
  6. Series motors have high starting torque but must never run unloaded.

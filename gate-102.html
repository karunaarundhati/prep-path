Linear Discriminant Analysis

Linear Discriminant Analysis, or LDA, finds the single direction in which the classes are best separated. It is a supervised method that uses the labels, unlike PCA. You will learn projections, Fisher's criterion, the scatter matrices, how to compute the LDA direction for two classes, and how LDA differs from PCA.

The idea in plain words

Imagine a box of red and blue beads spread across a table. You want to shine a torch and cast the shadow of the beads onto a wall, so that the red and blue shadows are as far apart as possible and each colour stays bunched together. The angle of the torch matters a great deal. Some angles mix the colours into one smudge. Other angles separate them cleanly. LDA is the search for the best angle.

In data language, the torch angle is a direction, and the shadow is a projection. Each data point with two or more features becomes a single number: how far along that direction it lies. LDA picks the direction so that the projected classes are far apart in the middle and tight inside.

The method is supervised, meaning it uses the class labels to decide the direction. This is what separates it from principal component analysis (PCA), which ignores labels and only looks for the direction of largest spread. For GATE, LDA appears in the Machine Learning part of the Data Science and AI paper (DA). Questions ask you to compute scatter matrices, find the LDA direction for two classes, compare LDA with PCA, and state the number of useful directions. You need matrix arithmetic on 2 by 2 matrices and some care with signs.

Key terms

Read these first. Each term is used again on the page.

Class
A group of data points that share a label, such as red beads or blue beads.
Direction w
A column of numbers that points one way. Only its direction matters, so w and 2w give the same result for ranking.
Projection
The shadow of a point on a direction. Precisely: the dot product wTx, a single number.
Dot product
Multiply matching entries and add. For w = (3, 2) and x = (1, 4), the dot product is 3×1 + 2×4 = 11.
Class mean μ
The average point of one class. Precisely: the sum of its points divided by how many there are.
Within-class scatter SW
How spread out each class is around its own mean, added over classes.
Between-class scatter SB
How far apart the class means are from each other.
Scatter matrix
A 2 by 2 (or n by n) table that records spreads and co-spreads of the features.
Fisher's criterion J(w)
The ratio of between-class to within-class spread after projecting onto w. Bigger is better separation.
Inverse matrix
The matrix that undoes another, like dividing by a number. The inverse of M is written M-1.
Principal component analysis PCA
An unsupervised method that finds the directions of largest total variance, ignoring labels.
Covariance
How two features vary together. Positive means they tend to rise together.
Shared covariance
The assumption that all classes have the same spread pattern, so their scatter can be pooled.

Projecting data onto a direction

From a point to a number

A point with two features, such as (2, 1), lies somewhere on a flat plane. A direction is an arrow from the origin, such as w = (3, 2). Ask: how far does the point go along the arrow? The answer is the projection, computed as a dot product.

projection of x on w = wTx = w1x1 + w2x2

Read it. wT means w written as a row. x is the point written as a column. The result is one number for each point. Think of a long line of coats hung on a single rail. Each coat has one position along the rail. The rail is the direction, and the position is the projection.

Worked example

Take w = (3, 2) and the point x = (2, 1).

  1. Multiply the first pair. 3 × 2 = 6.
  2. Multiply the second pair. 2 × 1 = 2.
  3. Add. 6 + 2 = 8.
  4. Read. The point projects to 8 along the direction (3, 2).

Why the length of w does not matter

If we double w to (6, 4), every projection doubles. The ranking of points stays the same, and so does the separation measured in relative terms. For this reason we often write the direction with its length scaled to suit us. The direction is what matters.

TipWhenever LDA gives you a direction, you may multiply it by any non-zero number and still have a valid answer. A question that asks for the direction usually wants a clean version such as (1, 0.5) or (3, 2) that points the same way.

Question: what is the projection of (1, 4) on (3, 2)?

3 × 1 + 2 × 4 = 3 + 8 = 11.

Question: if the projection of a point is 0, where is the point?

It lies on the line through the origin that is perpendicular to w. Such points have no component along the direction.

Fisher's criterion and the scatter matrices

What makes a good direction

Think again of the beads. Two things make the shadows easy to separate. First, the two shadow centres should be far apart. Second, each colour's shadow should be narrow. Ronald Fisher turned this idea into a single number. Let the projected class means be m1 and m2, and the projected spreads be s1² and s2². Fisher's criterion is:

J(w) = (m1 minus m2)² / (s1² + s2²)

Read it. The top is the squared gap between the projected means, which is the between-class separation. The bottom adds the two projected spreads, the within-class scatter. A big gap and small spreads give a big J. LDA chooses the w that makes J as large as possible.

The scatter matrices

We can write these quantities without projecting first, using matrices. Define the within-class scatter SW as the total spread of each class around its own mean, added over both classes. For one class with points x, the contribution is the sum of (x minus μ)(x minus μ)T. A product of a column with its own transpose is a 2 by 2 matrix that records the spread in each feature and how the features vary together.

SW = Σclasses Σx in class (x minus μclass)(x minus μclass)T

The between-class scatter for two classes is

SB = (μ1 minus μ2)(μ1 minus μ2)T

With these, the spread of the projection onto w is simply wTSWw, and the squared gap between the projected means is (wT(μ1 minus μ2))² = wTSBw. So Fisher's criterion becomes:

J(w) = (wTSBw) / (wTSWw)

Key ideaLDA is the direction that maximises the ratio of between-class to within-class scatter. Everything else in the method is arithmetic that finds this direction.

Worked example of the scatter matrix

Take a single class with points (1, 1), (3, 1), (2, 2) and (2, 0). Its mean is ((1+3+2+2)/4, (1+1+2+0)/4) = (2, 1). The deviations from the mean are (minus 1, 0), (1, 0), (0, 1) and (0, minus 1).

  1. First point. (minus 1, 0) times its transpose gives [[1, 0], [0, 0]].
  2. Second point. (1, 0) gives [[1, 0], [0, 0]].
  3. Third point. (0, 1) gives [[0, 0], [0, 1]].
  4. Fourth point. (0, minus 1) gives [[0, 0], [0, 1]].
  5. Add. The sum is [[2, 0], [0, 2]]. This is the scatter matrix for this class.

The diagonal entries are the total squared spread in each feature, and the off-diagonal entries show how the features vary together. Here they are zero, so the features are uncorrelated within the class.

Question: why is the scatter matrix built from (x minus μ)(x minus μ)T and not from the plain values of x?

Subtracting the mean measures spread around the centre. Without the subtraction, the matrix would mostly measure where the class sits, not how wide it is.

Question: what does a large SB compared with SW mean for the classes?

The class centres are far apart compared with how spread each class is, so the classes are likely to be well separated along that direction.

Computing the LDA direction

The answer for two classes

For two classes, the direction that maximises Fisher's ratio has a neat closed form:

w ∝ SW-1 (μ2 minus μ1)

The symbol ∝ means "points in the same direction as", so any positive or negative multiple is equally good. Read the parts. SW-1 is the inverse of the within-class scatter. It shrinks directions where the classes are already spread out and boosts directions where they are tight. μ2 minus μ1 is the direction from the first mean to the second. The answer combines the two: follow the gap between the means, but correct it for the spread.

Key ideaThe inverse of SW is what turns "the means are far apart" into "the means are far apart relative to how spread each class is".

The inverse of a 2 by 2 matrix

For a matrix [[a, b], [c, d]], the inverse is a fraction times [[d, minus b], [minus c, a]]. The fraction is 1 divided by the determinant ad minus bc. If the determinant is zero, there is no inverse.

[[a, b], [c, d]]-1 = (1/(ad minus bc)) × [[d, minus b], [minus c, a]]

Worked example

Class 1 has points (1, 1), (3, 1), (2, 2) and (2, 0). Its mean is μ1 = (2, 1), and its scatter matrix is [[2, 0], [0, 2]], as computed above. Class 2 has points (4, 3), (6, 3), (5, 4) and (5, 2). Its mean is μ2 = (5, 3), and by the same steps its scatter matrix is also [[2, 0], [0, 2]].

  1. Add the scatters. SW = [[2, 0], [0, 2]] + [[2, 0], [0, 2]] = [[4, 0], [0, 4]].
  2. Find the gap between means. μ2 minus μ1 = (5 minus 2, 3 minus 1) = (3, 2).
  3. Invert SW. The determinant is 4 × 4 minus 0 × 0 = 16. The inverse is (1/16) × [[4, 0], [0, 4]] = [[1/4, 0], [0, 1/4]].
  4. Multiply. SW-1(3, 2) = (3/4, 2/4) = (3/4, 1/2).
  5. Choose a clean scale. Multiply by 4 to get w = (3, 2). This is the LDA direction.

The direction (3, 2) leans more toward the first feature than toward the second, because the gap between the means is (3, 2) and the classes are equally spread in both features.

Checking the separation

Project every point onto w = (3, 2). Class 1 gives 5, 11, 10 and 6. Class 2 gives 18, 24, 23 and 19. The projected means are 8 and 21. The threshold halfway between them is 14.5. Every class 1 value is below 14.5 and every class 2 value is above it, so the two classes are perfectly separated along this line.

The code below verifies the scatter matrices, the direction and the projections with exact fractions.

from fractions import Fraction as F

C1 = [(1, 1), (3, 1), (2, 2), (2, 0)]
C2 = [(4, 3), (6, 3), (5, 4), (5, 2)]

def mean(points):
    n = len(points)
    return (F(sum(p[0] for p in points), n), F(sum(p[1] for p in points), n))

def scatter(points, m):
    a = b = c = F(0)
    for x, y in points:
        dx, dy = x - m[0], y - m[1]
        a += dx * dx
        b += dx * dy
        c += dy * dy
    return [[a, b], [b, c]]

m1, m2 = mean(C1), mean(C2)
S1, S2 = scatter(C1, m1), scatter(C2, m2)
SW = [[S1[i][j] + S2[i][j] for j in range(2)] for i in range(2)]
print("mean 1 =", [str(v) for v in m1], " mean 2 =", [str(v) for v in m2])
print("S_W =", [[str(v) for v in row] for row in SW])

det = SW[0][0] * SW[1][1] - SW[0][1] * SW[1][0]
inv = [[SW[1][1] / det, -SW[0][1] / det], [-SW[1][0] / det, SW[0][0] / det]]
diff = (m2[0] - m1[0], m2[1] - m1[1])
w_exact = [inv[0][0] * diff[0] + inv[0][1] * diff[1], inv[1][0] * diff[0] + inv[1][1] * diff[1]]
print("S_W^-1 (m2 - m1) =", [str(v) for v in w_exact])

w = (3, 2)
proj1 = [w[0] * x + w[1] * y for x, y in C1]
proj2 = [w[0] * x + w[1] * y for x, y in C2]
print("projections class 1 =", proj1, " class 2 =", proj2)
print("projected means =", str(F(sum(proj1), 4)), str(F(sum(proj2), 4)))

d = (m1[0] - m2[0], m1[1] - m2[1])
num = (w[0] * d[0] + w[1] * d[1]) ** 2
den = (w[0] * (SW[0][0] * w[0] + SW[0][1] * w[1]) + w[1] * (SW[1][0] * w[0] + SW[1][1] * w[1]))
print("Fisher ratio J =", str(num / den), "=", round(float(num / den), 2))
mean 1 = ['2', '1']  mean 2 = ['5', '3']
S_W = [['4', '0'], ['0', '4']]
S_W^-1 (m2 - m1) = ['3/4', '1/2']
projections class 1 = [5, 11, 10, 6]  class 2 = [18, 24, 23, 19]
projected means = 8 21
Fisher ratio J = 13/4 = 3.25
Question: what would happen to w if the two classes were equally spread in one feature but very spread in the other?

The inverse of SW would down-weight the widely spread feature. The direction would then favour the feature in which the classes are tight, because that is where the separation is most reliable.

Question: why is the solution unique up to scale?

Fisher's ratio does not change when w is multiplied by a non-zero number, since the same factor appears on top and bottom. So only the direction is determined.

Using LDA as a classifier

From a direction to a decision

Once we have w, each point becomes one number, its projection. To classify a new point, compute its projection and compare it with a threshold. A natural choice when the classes are of similar size is the midpoint between the two projected means.

threshold = (wTμ1 + wTμ2) / 2

Using the example above, wTμ1 = 8 and wTμ2 = 21, so the threshold is 14.5. A new point with projection 15 is assigned to class 2, because it lies above the threshold.

The boundary is a line

The points that project exactly onto the threshold form the decision boundary. In two features this is a straight line perpendicular to w. Here the boundary satisfies 3x1 + 2x2 = 14.5. Every point on the far side of the line is class 2, and every point on the near side is class 1.

TrapThe threshold at the midpoint assumes the two classes have about equal size and equal spread along w. If the classes are unbalanced, the best threshold moves toward the smaller class. Always check the priors before trusting the midpoint.

Why this is a good classifier

LDA assumes each class follows a Gaussian distribution, and that all classes share the same covariance matrix. Under those assumptions, the best possible decision boundary between two classes is linear, and LDA estimates exactly that boundary from the data. When the assumptions hold roughly, LDA is simple, fast, and hard to beat. When one class has a very different spread, a quadratic method may be better.

Question: a new point projects to 14.5 exactly. What is the prediction?

It lies on the decision boundary, so the classes are equally likely with the midpoint rule. In practice we pick either class by a fixed convention and note the tie.

Question: what assumption makes the LDA boundary linear?

The shared covariance assumption. If the classes had different covariances, the boundary would curve, which is the quadratic discriminant case.

LDA compared with PCA

Two directions, two purposes

PCA looks at the data as one cloud and asks: where is the spread largest? It finds the direction with the largest total variance. It never looks at labels. LDA asks a different question: where are the classes most separated? It uses the labels to answer.

Here is a picture in words. Suppose the red points lie on the line y = 1 and the blue points on y = minus 1, and both groups stretch from x = minus 5 to x = 5. The overall cloud is wide in x and narrow in y. PCA picks the x-axis, the direction of largest spread, which mixes the two colours completely. LDA picks the y-axis, because the classes differ there and each class is narrow in y. LDA gives the useful direction for classification and PCA does not.

FeaturePCALDA
Uses class labelsNo (unsupervised)Yes (supervised)
ObjectiveMaximum total varianceMaximum between-class to within-class ratio
Maximum useful directionsUp to the number of featuresAt most K minus 1, where K is the number of classes
Main purposeCompression and noise reductionClassification and separation
Key matricesCovariance matrix of all dataSW and SB

GATE loves thisThe number of useful LDA directions is at most K minus 1. With two classes there is exactly one direction. With five classes there are at most four, no matter how many features there are.

Question: can PCA ever pick the same direction as LDA?

Yes, if the direction of largest total spread also happens to separate the classes. That is a coincidence, not a rule.

Question: with 10 features and 4 classes, how many LDA directions are useful?

At most 4 minus 1 = 3, even though there are 10 features.

Counting LDA directions (NAT)

A dataset has 10 features and 5 classes. What is the maximum number of useful LDA projection directions? (NAT)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The largest number of LDA directions that can be useful.
  2. Which idea applies. LDA gives at most K minus 1 useful directions, where K is the number of classes. The number of features does not raise this limit.
  3. Work it out. K = 5, so K minus 1 = 4. The 10 features do not change the answer, though they can limit it in other cases.
  4. Check. With 2 classes the rule gives 1 direction, which matches the two-class formula.

Answer: 4

Facts about LDA and PCA (MSQ)

Which statements are true? Select all that apply.

(A) LDA is a supervised method.

(B) PCA uses the class labels to choose its directions.

(C) LDA assumes the classes have a shared covariance.

(D) LDA gives at most K minus 1 useful directions.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements about LDA and PCA are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. Supervised means it uses labels. Shared covariance is the standard LDA assumption. The K minus 1 limit is a key fact.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true. (B) is false, PCA ignores labels. (C) is true. (D) is true.
  4. Check. The false statement is the one that gives PCA the labels, which is the defining difference between the two.

Answer: (A), (C) and (D)

Fisher ratio for a diagonal case (NAT)

Within-class scatter is SW = [[4, 0], [0, 1]]. The difference of the class means is μ1 minus μ2 = (0, 2). Let SB = (μ1 minus μ2)(μ1 minus μ2)T. Compute Fisher's ratio J(w) for w = (0, 2). (NAT)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A single value of J at a given direction.
  2. Which idea applies. J = (wTSBw)/(wTSWw). With SB = ddT, wTSBw = (wTd)².
  3. Work it out. d = (0, 2) and w = (0, 2). wTd = 4, so the numerator is 16. For the denominator, wTSWw = 0 × 4 × 0 + 2 × 1 × 2 = 4. So J = 16/4 = 4.
  4. Check. Along this direction the means are 4 units of projection apart, while the spread is 2 units of standard deviation in total, which fits a ratio of 4 when squared.

Answer: 4

Scaling the LDA direction (NAT)

Within-class scatter is SW = [[4, 0], [0, 1]] and the difference of the means is μ2 minus μ1 = (2, 2). The LDA direction is w ∝ SW-1(μ2 minus μ1). Scale w so that its second entry is 1. What is the first entry? (NAT)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The first entry of w after scaling its second entry to 1.
  2. Which idea applies. The inverse of a diagonal matrix takes reciprocals of the diagonal.
  3. Work it out. SW-1 = [[1/4, 0], [0, 1]]. Multiply by (2, 2): (2/4, 2) = (1/2, 2). Divide both entries by 2 so the second is 1: (1/4, 1). The first entry is 1/4 = 0.25.
  4. Check. The class difference is equal in both features, but the first feature is four times more spread, so its weight is reduced by a factor of 4 relative to the second.

Answer: 0.25

Which direction does each method choose? (MCQ)

Red points lie on the line y = 1 and blue points on y = minus 1. Both colours spread from x = minus 5 to x = 5. Which direction does LDA choose? Select the single correct option.

(A) the x-axis (B) the y-axis (C) the line y = x

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The direction that best separates the two colours.
  2. Which idea applies. LDA maximises between-class separation divided by within-class spread.
  3. Work it out. Along the x-axis the two classes overlap completely, because both spread from minus 5 to 5. Along the y-axis the means are 2 apart and each class has no spread at all, so the separation is large relative to the spread.
  4. Check. PCA would pick the x-axis, since that is the largest spread. LDA picks the y-axis, which is the classification-friendly answer.

Answer: (B) the y-axis

A non-diagonal within-class scatter (NAT)

Within-class scatter is SW = [[2, 1], [1, 2]]. The difference of the class means is μ2 minus μ1 = (3, 0). The LDA direction is w ∝ SW-1(μ2 minus μ1). Find the ratio w2/w1. (NAT)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The slope of the LDA direction, as a ratio.
  2. Which idea applies. Use the 2 by 2 inverse formula, then multiply.
  3. Work it out. The determinant is 2 × 2 minus 1 × 1 = 3. So SW-1 = (1/3)[[2, minus 1], [minus 1, 2]]. Multiply by (3, 0): the first entry is (1/3)(2 × 3 + (minus 1) × 0) = 2, and the second is (1/3)((minus 1) × 3 + 2 × 0) = minus 1. So w is proportional to (2, minus 1), and the ratio is minus 1/2 = minus 0.5.
  4. Check. Multiply back: SW(2, minus 1) = (2 × 2 + 1 × (minus 1), 1 × 2 + 2 × (minus 1)) = (3, 0), which is the mean difference. The answer is confirmed.

Answer: minus 0.5

Which statements about Fisher's ratio hold? (MSQ)

Select all true statements.

(A) Multiplying w by any non-zero number leaves Fisher's ratio J(w) unchanged.

(B) The LDA derivation assumes the two classes have the same covariance.

(C) If the class means differ, LDA always separates the classes perfectly.

(D) For two classes, the LDA direction is proportional to SW-1(μ1 minus μ2), up to sign.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which claims about J and LDA are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. A scale factor appears on top and bottom of J. LDA assumes shared covariance. Overlapping classes cannot always be separated. The two-class formula can use either order of means if the sign is ignored.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true, the factor cancels in the ratio. (B) is true, that is the shared-covariance assumption. (C) is false, the spreads may overlap so much that no line separates them perfectly. (D) is true, the two orders differ only by a sign, and a sign does not change the direction up to scale.
  4. Check. The false claim uses the word "always", which is a warning sign in exam questions.

Answer: (A), (B) and (D)

Exam-level 1: Fisher direction and a projection (NAT, 2 marks)

Two classes in two dimensions have class means m1 = (1, 2) and m2 = (3, 0). The within class scatter matrix is SW = [[4, 0], [0, 2]]. Fisher LDA chooses the direction w = SW inverse times (m1 minus m2). Find the projection w dot x of the point x = (4, 1) onto this direction. (NAT)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. One number: the projection of a point onto the Fisher direction.
  2. Which idea applies. Compute the difference of the means, multiply by the inverse of SW, then take a dot product with the point.
  3. Work it out. m1 minus m2 = (1 minus 3, 2 minus 0) = (minus 2, 2). The inverse of a diagonal matrix divides each entry by its diagonal value, so w = (minus 2/4, 2/2) = (minus 1/2, 1). The projection is (minus 1/2) times 4 plus 1 times 1 = minus 2 plus 1 = minus 1.
  4. Check. The direction w points from class 2 toward class 1, since w times the mean difference should be positive. Here w dot (m1 minus m2) = (minus 1/2) times (minus 2) + 1 times 2 = 1 + 2 = 3, which is positive as expected.

Answer: minus 1 (the projection w dot x = -1, with w = (-1/2, 1))

A student compares linear discriminant analysis (LDA) with principal component analysis (PCA). Which of the following statements are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) PCA ignores the class labels, while LDA uses them.

(B) For C classes, LDA gives at most C minus 1 useful discriminant directions.

(C) PCA keeps the directions of largest variance, and these directions can mix the classes together.

(D) LDA assumes that each class has its own separate covariance matrix.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which of four comparisons between LDA and PCA are true.
  2. Which idea applies. PCA is unsupervised and maximises variance. LDA is supervised, uses the between class and within class scatter, and has a rank limit from the number of classes.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true. (B) is true, because the between class scatter has rank at most C minus 1. (C) is true, because the top variance direction can separate nothing if the classes overlap along it. (D) is false: standard LDA assumes one shared covariance matrix for all classes, and quadratic discriminant analysis is the version that allows separate ones.
  4. Check. The code below shows the rank limit for three classes in five dimensions. It gives 2 useful LDA directions, while PCA can offer up to 5.

Exam-level 3: Trace of the between class scatter (NAT, 2 marks)

Two classes have n1 = 4 and n2 = 6 training points, with class means (1, 2) and (3, 0). The between class scatter matrix is SB = (n1 n2 / n) times (m1 minus m2)(m1 minus m2) transpose, where n = n1 + n2. Find the trace of SB, which is the sum of its diagonal entries. Give the exact value. (NAT)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The trace of SB.
  2. Which idea applies. Compute the scaling factor, the outer product of the mean difference, then add the two diagonal entries.
  3. Work it out. n = 10, so the factor is 4 times 6 divided by 10 = 2.4. The difference is (minus 2, 2). The outer product has diagonal entries 4 and 4. So the diagonal of SB is 2.4 times 4 = 9.6 and 9.6. The trace is 9.6 plus 9.6 = 19.2.
  4. Check. The trace of an outer product d d transpose equals the squared length of d, which is 4 plus 4 = 8. Multiply by the factor 2.4 to get 19.2.

Answer: 19.2

Exam-level 4: Threshold and distance in the projected space (NAT, 2 marks)

A two-class LDA model uses the direction w = (minus 1/2, 1). The class means are (1, 2) for class 1 and (3, 0) for class 2. The classes have equal priors, so the decision threshold is the midpoint of the two projected means. A new point is x = (minus 2, 3). Project each mean and the new point onto w, find the threshold, and then find the distance between the projected value of x and the threshold. (NAT)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A distance on the projected line, not in the original plane.
  2. Which idea applies. Project everything with the same dot product. The threshold is the average of the projected means when priors are equal.
  3. Work it out. Projected mean of class 1: (minus 1/2) times 1 + 1 times 2 = 3/2. Projected mean of class 2: (minus 1/2) times 3 + 0 = minus 3/2. The threshold is (3/2 plus minus 3/2)/2 = 0. The new point projects to (minus 1/2) times (minus 2) + 1 times 3 = 1 + 3 = 4. The distance to the threshold is |4 minus 0| = 4.
  4. Check. The projected value 4 lies on class 1's side, since it is closer to 3/2 (distance 5/2) than to minus 3/2 (distance 11/2). That agrees with the threshold being at 0.

Answer: 4

Summary

Here is every key term in one line each.

Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA)
A supervised method that finds the direction that best separates classes.
Direction w
An arrow whose length does not matter, only its direction.
Projection
The dot product wTx, one number per point.
Class mean
The average point of one class.
Within-class scatter SW
The total spread of each class around its own mean.
Between-class scatter SB
The outer product of the difference between class means.
Fisher's criterion
The ratio of projected between-class to within-class scatter, to be maximised.
LDA direction
For two classes, w is proportional to SW-1(μ2 minus μ1).
Inverse matrix
The matrix that undoes another, found for 2 by 2 matrices by the swap-and-divide rule.
Decision threshold
The midpoint of the two projected class means, for classes of similar size.
Decision boundary
The line of points that project onto the threshold.
Shared covariance
The assumption that all classes have the same spread pattern, which makes the boundary linear.
Principal component analysis (PCA)
An unsupervised method that finds the direction of largest total variance.
Maximum LDA directions
K minus 1, where K is the number of classes.

Remember these

  • LDA uses the labels. PCA does not.
  • Fisher's ratio is between-class separation divided by within-class spread.
  • For two classes, the LDA direction is SW-1 times the difference of the means.
  • The scale of w does not matter, only its direction.
  • LDA gives at most K minus 1 useful directions.
  • The LDA boundary is linear under shared covariance and roughly equal class sizes.

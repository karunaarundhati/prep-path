Binomial, Poisson, Normal distributions (recap)

Binomial, Poisson and Normal are the three distributions that GATE RA questions use most often. This recap gives you the formulas, what each symbol means, and when one distribution can stand in for another. It is for fast revision, so the derivations are not repeated here.

The idea in plain words

A random variable is a number that depends on chance, such as the number of heads in five coin tosses. Each possible value has a probability. The full list of these probabilities is the distribution, and it tells you how the random number behaves.

Three distributions cover most of what GATE RA asks:

  • Binomial. Count the successes in a fixed number of yes or no trials.
  • Poisson. Count random events that arrive at a steady average rate over a time or space.
  • Normal. A smooth bell curve for measurements that cluster around an average, such as sensor noise.

Everyday picture: a binomial is flipping a coin a fixed number of times and counting heads. A Poisson is counting buses at a stop in an hour when you know the average. A normal is weighing many apples and drawing the bell curve of their weights.

Key terms

Random variable (X)
Plain meaning: a number that depends on chance. Precise definition: a rule that assigns a number to each outcome of a random experiment.
Probability mass function (PMF)
Plain meaning: the probability for each whole-number value. Precise definition: P(X = k) for a discrete variable.
Success probability (p)
Plain meaning: the chance that one trial succeeds. Precise definition: a number between 0 and 1 giving P(success) on one trial.
Combination C(n, k)
Plain meaning: the number of ways to pick k items from n when order does not matter. Precise definition: n!/(k!(n − k)!).
Rate λ (lambda)
Plain meaning: the average number of events in the interval. Precise definition: the one parameter of a Poisson distribution, equal to its mean and variance.
Mean (μ)
Plain meaning: the centre, or long-run average. Precise definition: the expected value E[X].
Variance (σ²)
Plain meaning: how spread out the values are, in squared units. Precise definition: E[(X − μ)²].
Standard deviation (σ)
Plain meaning: the typical distance from the mean. Precise definition: the square root of the variance.
Z-score
Plain meaning: how many standard deviations a value sits from the mean. Precise definition: Z = (X − μ)/σ.
Φ(z)
Plain meaning: the chance that a standard normal value is at most z. Precise definition: the cumulative distribution function of the normal with mean 0 and standard deviation 1.

Part 1: The binomial distribution

Use the binomial when there is a fixed number of trials n, each trial has two outcomes, the success probability p is the same every time, and the trials are independent.

P(X = k) = C(n, k) × pk × (1 − p)n − k
  • X is the number of successes and k is the value we want.
  • pk is the chance of k successes in one specific order.
  • (1 − p)n − k is the chance that the other trials fail.
  • C(n, k) counts the orders that give exactly k successes.
Mean = np and Variance = np(1 − p)

A worked example, every step shown

Each item from a machine is defective with probability 0.3. Ten items are checked. Find P(exactly 3 defective).

Step 1: Identify. n = 10, p = 0.3, k = 3.

Step 2: Count the orders. C(10, 3) = (10 × 9 × 8)/(3 × 2 × 1) = 720/6 = 120.

Step 3: Success part. 0.33 = 0.027.

Step 4: Failure part. 0.77 ≈ 0.0823543.

Step 5: Multiply. 120 × 0.027 × 0.0823543 ≈ 0.2668.

from math import comb

n, p, k = 10, 0.3, 3
prob = comb(n, k) * p**k * (1 - p)**(n - k)
print("C(10, 3) =", comb(n, k))
print("P(X = 3) =", round(prob, 4))
print("Mean =", round(n * p, 2))
print("Variance =", round(n * p * (1 - p), 2))
C(10, 3) = 120
P(X = 3) = 0.2668
Mean = 3.0
Variance = 2.1

TrapForgetting C(n, k) is the most common error. It counts all the orders in which the successes can appear.

A coin is tossed 4 times. What is the mean number of heads?

Mean = np = 4 × 0.5 = 2.

Part 2: The Poisson distribution

Use the Poisson for random events that occur one at a time, independently, at a steady average rate λ over a fixed interval.

P(X = k) = e−λ × λk / k!
  • λ is the average number of events in the interval.
  • e−λ is the chance of zero events, with e about 2.71828.
  • k! is k factorial, the product 1 × 2 × ... × k, with 0! = 1.
Mean = λ and Variance = λ

The mean and variance are equal. If a data set has variance close to its mean, a Poisson model is a sensible first guess.

A worked example, every step shown

Calls arrive at a help desk at an average of 3 per minute. Find P(exactly 2 calls in the next minute).

Step 1: Identify. λ = 3, k = 2.

Step 2: Formula. P(X = 2) = e−3 × 32/2!.

Step 3: Pieces. 32 = 9, 2! = 2, so 9/2 = 4.5. And e−3 ≈ 0.04979.

Step 4: Multiply. 4.5 × 0.04979 ≈ 0.2240.

import math

lam, k = 3, 2
prob = math.exp(-lam) * lam**k / math.factorial(k)
print("P(X = 2) =", round(prob, 4))
print("Chance of zero calls =", round(math.exp(-lam), 4))
P(X = 2) = 0.224
Chance of zero calls = 0.0498
What is the variance of a Poisson variable with mean 4?

It is 4, because for a Poisson the variance equals the mean.

Part 3: When Poisson approximates Binomial

When n is large and p is small, a binomial count behaves almost like a Poisson count with λ = np. The usual rule is n at least 20 and p at most 0.05. Use λ = np, the binomial mean.

import math

n, p = 1000, 0.002
lam = n * p
exact = (1 - p) ** n            # binomial P(X = 0)
approx = math.exp(-lam)         # Poisson P(X = 0) with lambda = np
print("lambda = np =", round(lam, 2))
print("Binomial P(X = 0) =", round(exact, 5))
print("Poisson P(X = 0) =", round(approx, 5))
lambda = np = 2.0
Binomial P(X = 0) = 0.13506
Poisson P(X = 0) = 0.13534

The two answers agree to about three decimal places, which is why the approximation is popular in exam questions.

TrapUse λ = np, not λ = n. And do not approximate when p is large, because then the approximation is poor even for large n.

Part 4: The normal distribution and Z-scores

The normal curve is symmetric about its mean μ. About 68 percent of values lie within one standard deviation of the mean, about 95 percent within two, and about 99.7 percent within three.

Z = (X − μ) / σ

Standardising turns any normal variable into the standard normal, so one table of Φ(z) answers every question.

A worked example, every step shown

Exam scores are normal with mean 50 and standard deviation 10. Find P(X ≤ 60) and P(X ≥ 65).

Step 1: Standardise 60. Z = (60 − 50)/10 = 1. P(X ≤ 60) = Φ(1) = 0.8413.

Step 2: Standardise 65. Z = (65 − 50)/10 = 1.5. P(X ≥ 65) = 1 − Φ(1.5) = 1 − 0.9332 = 0.0668.

import math

def Phi(z):
    # standard normal cumulative probability
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2)))

mu, sigma = 50, 10
print("P(X <= 60) =", round(Phi((60 - mu) / sigma), 4))
print("P(X >= 65) =", round(1 - Phi((65 - mu) / sigma), 4))
P(X <= 60) = 0.8413
P(X >= 65) = 0.0668

TrapDivide by σ, not σ². If the variance is 100, then σ is 10.

For μ = 100 and σ = 15, what is Z when X = 115?

Z = (115 − 100)/15 = 1.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original, written in GATE style, and are not past exam questions. Try each one before reading the answer.

Exactly two heads

A fair coin is tossed 5 times. What is the probability of exactly 2 heads? Give the answer as a fraction.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. P(X = 2) for n = 5 and p = 0.5.
  2. Which idea applies. The binomial formula.
  3. Work it out. C(5, 2) = 10. p2 = 1/4 and (1 − p)3 = 1/8. Product = 10 × 1/4 × 1/8 = 10/32 = 5/16.
  4. Check. There are 32 equally likely sequences, and 10 of them have exactly 2 heads.

Answer: 5/16.

No calls in a minute

Calls arrive at a switchboard at an average of 4 per minute, following a Poisson process. Find the probability of no calls in the next minute, to 4 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. P(X = 0) with λ = 4.
  2. Which idea applies. The Poisson formula with k = 0.
  3. Work it out. P(X = 0) = e−4 × 40/0! = e−4. Since e4 ≈ 54.598, e−4 ≈ 1/54.598 ≈ 0.0183.
  4. Check. The answer lies between 0 and 1 and is small, which fits an average of 4 calls.

Answer: 0.0183.

Which statements about the approximation are correct?

A binomial variable has n = 50 and p = 0.04. Select all statements that are correct.

(A) The mean is 2.
(B) The variance is 2.
(C) A Poisson distribution with λ = 2 is a reasonable approximation.
(D) P(X = 0) is exactly e−2.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Test four claims.
  2. Which idea applies. Mean = np, variance = np(1 − p), and the approximation uses λ = np.
  3. Work it out. Mean = 50 × 0.04 = 2, so A is true. Variance = 2 × 0.96 = 1.92, so B is false. n = 50 and p = 0.04 satisfy the rule, so C is true. P(X = 0) = 0.9650 ≈ 0.1300, while e−2 ≈ 0.1353, so D is false.
  4. Check. A Poisson approximation is close but never exact, so D being false is expected.

Answer: (A) and (C).

A NAT question on marks

Marks in a test are normal with mean 100 and standard deviation 15. Find P(X ≤ 115), to 4 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A cumulative probability for a normal variable.
  2. Which idea applies. Standardise, then read Φ(z).
  3. Work it out. Z = (115 − 100)/15 = 1, so P(X ≤ 115) = Φ(1) = 0.8413.
  4. Check. One standard deviation above the mean should give a probability a little above one half, and 0.8413 fits the 68-95-99.7 rule.

Answer: 0.8413.

A Poisson count of defects

Defects on a metal sheet follow a Poisson distribution with λ = 2.5. Find P(X = 1), to 4 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. One Poisson probability with k = 1.
  2. Which idea applies. P(X = k) = e−λ λk/k!.
  3. Work it out. P(X = 1) = 2.5 × e−2.5. With e−2.5 ≈ 0.082085, the product is 0.205213, so 0.2052.
  4. Check. The answer is a probability between 0 and 1 and is close to the value at k = 2, which fits a mean of 2.5.

Answer: 0.2052.

Exact binomial against Poisson

X is binomial with n = 20 and p = 0.1. Find P(X = 2) exactly, to 4 decimal places. Then find the Poisson approximation with λ = np, and the absolute difference between the two, to 4 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The exact value, the approximation and the difference.
  2. Which idea applies. Binomial formula, then Poisson with λ = 2.
  3. Work it out. Exact: C(20, 2) = 190, p2 = 0.01, 0.918 ≈ 0.150095. Product = 1.9 × 0.150095 ≈ 0.285180, so 0.2852. Poisson: e−2 × 22/2! = 2e−2 ≈ 0.270671. Difference ≈ 0.014509, so 0.0145.
  4. Check. Both values lie between 0 and 1, and they differ by less than 0.015, so the approximation is fair here.

Answer: binomial 0.2852, Poisson 0.2707, difference 0.0145.

Working backwards with a normal

X is normal with mean 40 and standard deviation 5. Find the value a such that P(X > a) = 0.9332.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A cut-off a with 93.32 percent of the probability above it.
  2. Which idea applies. Convert to a lower-tail statement, then use Z in reverse.
  3. Work it out. P(X ≤ a) = 1 − 0.9332 = 0.0668. This is Φ(z) = 0.0668 at z = −1.5. So (a − 40)/5 = −1.5, giving a = 40 − 7.5 = 32.5.
  4. Check. Since most of the probability lies above a, a must sit below the mean 40. And 32.5 is below 40, as it should be.

Answer: a = 32.5.

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Poisson approximation (NAT, 2 marks)

A batch has 500 items and each is defective with probability 0.004, independently. Using the Poisson approximation, find P(at most 1 defective), correct to 3 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. P(X ≤ 1) with X approximately Poisson.
  2. Which idea applies. λ = np = 2. P(X ≤ 1) = e−λ(1 + λ).
  3. Work it out. e−2 · 3 = 0.406.
  4. Check. The code compares with the exact binomial value.
import math
n, p = 500, 0.004
exact = sum(math.comb(n, k) * p ** k * (1 - p) ** (n - k) for k in range(2))
print("poisson = %.3f  binomial = %.3f" % (math.exp(-2) * 3, exact))

poisson = 0.406  binomial = 0.405

Answer: about 0.406.

Exam-level 2: Normal standardisation (NAT, 2 marks)

X is normal with mean 50 and standard deviation 10. Find P(40 ≤ X ≤ 70), correct to 3 decimal places. Use Φ(1) = 0.8413 and Φ(2) = 0.9772.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The probability of an interval for a normal variable.
  2. Which idea applies. Standardise: Z = (X − 50)/10, so the interval becomes −1 ≤ Z ≤ 2.
  3. Work it out. Φ(2) − Φ(−1) = 0.9772 − (1 − 0.8413) = 0.8185.
  4. Check. The code uses the error function.
import math
Phi = lambda z: 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2)))
print("%.4f" % (Phi(2) - Phi(-1)))

0.8186

Answer: 0.819 (0.8185).

Exam-level 3: Distribution facts (MSQ, 2 marks)

Which are TRUE? (A) For Binomial(n, p), variance ≤ mean. (B) For Poisson(λ), mean = variance. (C) The sum of two independent Poisson variables is Poisson. (D) If X is normal, 2X + 3 is normal.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Standard properties of the three distributions.
  2. Which idea applies. Binomial variance np(1 − p) and mean np. Poisson rates add. Linear maps keep normality.
  3. Work it out. (A) np(1 − p) ≤ np. True. (B) True. (C) True, with rate λ1 + λ2. (D) True.
  4. Check. All four are standard results that GATE often mixes with a false statement, so read carefully.

Answer: (A), (B), (C) and (D).

Summary

Binomial distribution
Counts successes in n independent trials, each with success probability p.
Binomial mean and variance
Mean np and variance np(1 − p).
Poisson distribution
Counts random events at a steady average rate λ.
Poisson mean and variance
Both equal λ.
Poisson approximation
Binomial(n, p) is close to Poisson(np) when n is large and p is small.
Normal distribution
A symmetric bell curve with mean μ and standard deviation σ.
Z-score
Z = (X − μ)/σ, the number of standard deviations from the mean.
Φ(z)
The probability that a standard normal value is at most z.
Combination C(n, k)
The number of ways to choose k items from n, order ignored.
Factorial k!
The product of the whole numbers from 1 to k, with 0! = 1.
68-95-99.7 rule
About 68, 95 and 99.7 percent of normal values lie within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations of the mean.

Remember these

  1. Binomial needs fixed trials, two outcomes, a constant p, and independence.
  2. Binomial mean is np and variance is np(1 − p).
  3. Poisson mean and variance are both λ.
  4. Use λ = np when a binomial with large n and small p is approximated by a Poisson.
  5. Standardise a normal value with Z = (X − μ)/σ, dividing by σ and not σ².
  6. For an upper-tail probability, use 1 − Φ(z).

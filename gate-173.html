Strain gauges and rosettes

A strain gauge is a tiny wire or foil that changes its electrical resistance when it is stretched, and a Wheatstone bridge turns that tiny change into a voltage you can measure. A rosette uses three gauges at different angles to find the largest and smallest strains at a point. These ideas are core measurement topics for GATE Robotics and Automation.

The idea in plain words

Welcome. Suppose you want to know how much a machine part is stretching while it works. You cannot see the stretch with your eyes, because it is far too small. A few micrometres along a metre is typical. So engineers stick a tiny sensor onto the part. The sensor changes its electrical resistance when the part stretches, and a simple circuit turns that resistance change into a voltage. That is the whole idea of a strain gauge.

Here is an everyday picture. Think of a long, thin wire. If you pull it, it gets longer and thinner. A thinner wire has more resistance to electricity, just as a narrow garden hose lets less water through than a wide one. A strain gauge is a wire folded into a zigzag on a thin backing, so that a small stretch of the part makes a useful change of resistance.

But one gauge is not always enough. If you only glue one gauge to a surface, you measure the strain in one direction only. Real points in a loaded part can have strain in several directions at once. A rosette is a small group of three gauges glued at different angles on the same spot. With the three readings, you can calculate the largest and smallest strains at that point, and then the stresses.

Why does this matter for GATE? In Robotics and Automation (RA) papers, strain gauges are how robot arms, force sensors and load cells are built. A robot wrist force sensor is often a cross of strain gauges on a metal flexure. GATE questions typically ask for the gauge factor, the bridge output voltage for a given strain, or the principal strains from a rectangular rosette. The arithmetic is short, once the formulas are clear. This page builds each one slowly.

The plan: first the gauge and its resistance change. Second the gauge factor. Third the Wheatstone bridge that reads the change. Fourth the rosette, which gives the full strain picture at a point. Fifth the step from the rosette readings to principal stresses.

Key terms

Read this list now. Come back whenever a word feels unfamiliar. Each entry gives the plain meaning first, then the precise definition.

Strain (epsilon)
The fractional change in length of a material. Plain meaning: how much a part has stretched, as a fraction of its length. Precise definition: epsilon = change in length / original length, a number with no unit. Very small values are written in microstrain, where 1 microstrain = 10-6.
Microstrain (µε)
One millionth of a strain. Plain meaning: a convenient way to write very small strains. Precise definition: 1 µε = 10-6 strain, so 500 µε = 0.0005.
Strain gauge
A sensor whose electrical resistance changes when it is strained. Plain meaning: a tiny wire that reports stretching as a change in resistance. Precise definition: a resistive element bonded to a surface so that it deforms with the surface.
Resistance (R)
How strongly a material opposes the flow of electric current. Plain meaning: how hard it is for current to get through. Precise definition: R = ρL / A, in ohms, where ρ is resistivity, L is length and A is cross-sectional area.
Gauge factor (GF)
The sensitivity of a strain gauge. Plain meaning: how big a resistance change you get for each unit of strain. Precise definition: GF = (ΔR / R) / epsilon, a dimensionless ratio.
Wheatstone bridge
A four-arm circuit that compares resistances and gives a small output voltage when they are unbalanced. Plain meaning: a balanced pair of voltage dividers. Precise definition: a circuit of four resistors connected in a diamond, fed by a supply voltage, whose output is the voltage difference between two midpoints.
Excitation voltage (Vex)
The supply voltage applied across the bridge. Plain meaning: the voltage that powers the bridge. Precise definition: the voltage applied between the two input terminals of the bridge.
Quarter bridge
A bridge with one active gauge and three fixed resistors. Plain meaning: one gauge in one arm. Precise definition: a bridge in which only one arm changes with strain.
Half bridge
A bridge with two active gauges. Plain meaning: two gauges in two arms. Precise definition: a bridge in which two arms change with strain in a way that adds to the output.
Full bridge
A bridge with four active gauges. Plain meaning: all four arms are gauges. Precise definition: a bridge in which all four arms change with strain.
Rosette
A group of gauges in one package, at fixed angles to each other. Plain meaning: three gauges glued at a point, pointing in different directions. Precise definition: a set of strain gauges mounted at known angles to measure the strain state at a point.
Rectangular rosette
A rosette whose three gauges are at 0°, 45° and 90°. Plain meaning: gauges set at the corners of a square. Precise definition: a three-gauge rosette with its grids at 0, 45 and 90 degrees to a reference axis.
Strain state at a point
The full set of strains at a point, in every direction. Plain meaning: the complete strain picture at one spot. Precise definition: the strain components epsilonx, epsilony and gammaxy at a point.
Shear strain (gamma)
The change in angle between two lines that were at right angles. Plain meaning: how much a square turns into a parallelogram. Precise definition: gammaxy is the decrease in the right angle between the x and y lines.
Principal strain
The largest and smallest normal strains at a point, with no shear strain on those directions. Plain meaning: the strains in the two special directions. Precise definition: the eigenvalues of the strain state, epsilon1 and epsilon2.
Principal stress
The largest and smallest normal stresses at a point, with no shear stress on those planes. Plain meaning: the stresses in the two special directions. Precise definition: the normal stresses on the planes where shear stress is zero.
Poisson's ratio (nu)
The ratio of the sideways contraction to the stretch along the length of a material. Plain meaning: how much a rubber band thins when pulled. Precise definition: nu = -(lateral strain) / (axial strain); for steel about 0.3.
Plane stress
A state in which the stress perpendicular to a surface is zero. Plain meaning: a thin plate with no pressure across its face. Precise definition: sigmaz = 0, which applies to a gauged surface of a thin part.

Concept 1: How a strain gauge works

Start with the basic law of resistance. For a wire of length L, cross-sectional area A and resistivity rho (a property of the material), the resistance is:

R = rho × L / A

Each symbol has a plain meaning. R is the resistance, in ohms. rho is the resistivity, which depends only on the material. L is the length of the wire, in metres. A is the cross-sectional area, in square metres. A longer wire has more resistance. A thicker wire has less resistance.

Now stretch the wire. Its length grows, and its area shrinks, because the wire gets thinner. Both changes push the resistance up. A longer wire and a thinner wire each give more resistance. So a stretched wire always has a higher resistance than an unstretched one, and the change in resistance tells us how much it was stretched.

A strain gauge is a practical version of this wire. The wire is folded back and forth into a grid, and the grid is bonded to a thin backing. The backing sticks to the surface of the machine part. When the part stretches, the backing stretches too, and the grid stretches with it. The grid gives a reasonably large resistance change for a small strain, because the wire is long in a small space.

     ___________________
    |  _     _     _    |     the zigzag grid of thin metal foil
    | | |___| |___| |   |     sits on a thin backing sheet
    | |_______________|  |
    |___________________|
         | lead 1      | lead 2

Key ideaA strain gauge changes resistance because the stretch makes the wire longer and thinner at the same time. Both effects raise the resistance.

Question 1: A wire is stretched. What happens to its resistance?

It increases. The length grows and the cross-sectional area falls, and R = rho L / A rises for both reasons.

Question 2: Why is the zigzag grid used instead of a straight wire?

A zigzag puts a long wire into a small area, which gives a useful resistance change even for a small strain.

Concept 2: The gauge factor

The sensitivity of a gauge is measured by its gauge factor, GF. It is defined as the fractional change in resistance divided by the strain:

GF = (ΔR / R) / epsilon, so ΔR = GF × epsilon × R

Let us say what each symbol means. ΔR is the change in resistance, in ohms, caused by the strain. R is the unstrained resistance, in ohms. epsilon is the strain, a number with no unit. GF is the gauge factor, a number with no unit. For most metal foil gauges it is close to 2.

The gauge factor tells you how much the gauge responds to strain. A gauge with GF = 2 changes its resistance by 2 times the strain. So a strain of 500 microstrain gives a fractional change of 2 × 0.0005 = 0.001, which is 0.1 percent of the resistance.

Worked example. A gauge has R = 120 ohms and GF = 2. It is strained by 500 microstrain. Find the change in resistance.

  1. Convert the strain: 500 microstrain = 500 × 10-6 = 0.0005.
  2. Use ΔR = GF × epsilon × R = 2 × 0.0005 × 120.
  3. Multiply 2 × 0.0005 = 0.001.
  4. Multiply 0.001 × 120 = 0.12 ohms.
  5. So the resistance rises from 120 ohms to 120.12 ohms.
R0 = 120     # ohms, unstrained resistance
GF = 2       # gauge factor
eps = 500e-6 # strain
dR = GF * eps * R0
print("delta R =", round(dR, 4), "ohm")
print("delta R / R0 =", round(dR / R0, 6))

delta R = 0.12 ohm
delta R / R0 = 0.001

TrapUsing the strain in microstrain directly in the formula. Microstrain must first be converted, so 500 microstrain becomes 0.0005. Forgetting this gives an answer a million times too large.

TrapA resistance change of 0.12 ohms is tiny compared with 120 ohms. That is why a bridge circuit is needed: a plain ohmmeter cannot reliably see such a small change. The bridge is the subject of the next concept.

Question 3: A gauge with GF = 2 and R = 350 ohms is strained by 1000 microstrain. What is the new resistance?

ΔR = 2 × 0.001 × 350 = 0.7 ohms. The new resistance is 350.7 ohms.

Question 4: Why is the gauge factor a pure number?

Because it is a ratio of two dimensionless quantities: the fractional change in resistance, ΔR / R, divided by the strain, which is also dimensionless.

Concept 3: The Wheatstone bridge reads the small change

A change of 0.12 ohms out of 120 ohms is far too small to read directly. The Wheatstone bridge solves this. It compares the gauge against fixed resistors, and it gives a voltage output that is proportional to the change, which an amplifier can measure.

Here is the layout. Four resistors form a diamond. A supply voltage Vex is applied across the top and bottom corners. The output voltage Vout is measured between the two side corners.

                 V_ex (supply, top corner)
                    |
          R1 -------+------- R2
           |                 |
           |   (out +)  Vout |     Vout is the voltage between the two
           +-----------------+     side corners (the middle of the diamond)
           |                 |
          R4 -------+------- R3
                    |
                  ground (bottom corner)

  Balanced bridge: R1/R2 = R4/R3, so Vout = 0.
  Strain gauge in one arm: its resistance changes, so Vout is no longer zero.

The formula for the output voltage is:

Vout = Vex × [ R3/(R3 + R4) − R2/(R1 + R2) ]

This formula says: each half of the bridge is a voltage divider. The output is the difference between the two divider voltages. When the two ratios are equal, the output is zero. That is the balanced state.

For a quarter bridge with one active gauge, suppose the gauge is R1 = R(1 + x), where x = ΔR / R = GF × epsilon, and the other three arms are fixed at R. Then the output becomes:

Vout = Vex × x / (2(2 + x))

When x is small, which is always true for gauges, the bottom term 2(2 + x) is close to 4. So Vout ≈ Vex × x / 4 = Vex × GF × epsilon / 4. This is the quarter bridge rule that GATE uses.

Worked example. The supply is Vex = 5 V, GF = 2, and the strain is 500 microstrain, in a quarter bridge. Find Vout.

  1. Find x: x = GF × epsilon = 2 × 0.0005 = 0.001.
  2. Use the approximate rule: Vout = 5 × 0.001 / 4 = 0.00125 V.
  3. So Vout is 1.25 mV. This is a small voltage, which an instrumentation amplifier can then boost.

The next Python check shows how close the exact formula is to the approximate one, so you can see the approximation is safe.

Vex = 5
GF = 2
eps = 500e-6
x = GF * eps
exact = Vex * ((1 + x) / (2 + x) - 0.5)
approx = Vex * GF * eps / 4
print("exact Vout =", round(exact * 1000, 4), "mV")
print("approx Vout =", round(approx * 1000, 4), "mV")

exact Vout = 1.2494 mV
approx Vout = 1.25 mV

Bridge types. The same idea extends to more active arms. In each case the output is a multiple of Vex × GF × epsilon / 4:

  • Quarter bridge (one active gauge): Vout = Vex × GF × epsilon / 4.
  • Half bridge (two active gauges in opposite arms, for example one in tension and one in compression): Vout = Vex × GF × epsilon / 2.
  • Full bridge (four active gauges arranged so that their changes add): Vout = Vex × GF × epsilon.

GATE loves thisAlways decide how many arms are active before you use a bridge formula. Each extra active arm that adds to the output doubles the sensitivity. The quarter, half and full bridges give factors of 1, 2 and 4 relative to each other, times Vex × GF × epsilon / 4.

TrapUsing the quarter bridge rule when the question says two gauges are active. Read the set-up carefully and count the active arms first.

Question 5: In a balanced bridge, what is Vout?

Zero. The two voltage dividers give the same voltage, so the difference is zero.

Question 6: A half bridge with Vex = 10 V, GF = 2 and epsilon = 300 microstrain has Vout = ? Give the answer in mV.

Vout = 10 × 2 × 0.0003 / 2 = 0.006 V = 6 mV.

Concept 4: Strain gauge rosettes and the strain state

A single gauge tells you the strain only in its own direction. But a point on a loaded part can have strain in several directions at once, and it can also have shear strain. To capture the whole picture, we use a rosette: three gauges bonded at the same point, at known angles to each other.

The most common type in GATE is the rectangular rosette. Its three gauges are at 0°, 45° and 90° to a reference axis, like the corners of a square. Call the readings epsilona (at 0°), epsilonb (at 45°) and epsilonc (at 90°).

        Gauge B at 45 degrees
               \
                \
   Gauge A ------+------ (reference x axis, 0 degrees)
   at 0 degrees  |
                 |
          Gauge C at 90 degrees
                 (the y axis)

   All three gauges sit at the same point.

A gauge at angle theta measures a strain given by the strain transformation rule, which relates the strains in the x and y directions to the strain at any angle:

epsilon(theta) = (epsilonx + epsilony)/2 + (epsilonx − epsilony)/2 × cos 2theta + (gammaxy/2) × sin 2theta

Let us check it on the three gauges of the rectangular rosette. Put theta = 0 for gauge A, which gives epsilona = epsilonx. Put theta = 90° for gauge C, which gives epsilonc = epsilony. Put theta = 45° for gauge B. Then cos 90° = 0 and sin 90° = 1, so epsilonb = (epsilonx + epsilony)/2 + gammaxy/2. Solving for the shear strain:

gammaxy = 2 epsilonb − epsilona − epsilonc

So from the three readings we get all three components of the strain state: epsilonx = epsilona, epsilony = epsilonc, and gammaxy = 2 epsilonb − epsilona − epsilonc.

Worked example. A rectangular rosette gives epsilona = 400 microstrain, epsilonb = 300 microstrain and epsilonc = 100 microstrain. Find the shear strain.

  1. Write the formula: gammaxy = 2 epsilonb − epsilona − epsilonc.
  2. Substitute: gammaxy = 2 × 300 − 400 − 100.
  3. Compute: 600 − 400 − 100 = 100 microstrain.
  4. So gammaxy = 100 microstrain.

TrapForgetting the factor 2 on the 45° reading. The formula has 2 epsilonb, not epsilonb. Many GATE wrong answers come from this single slip.

Question 7: Why is the 45° gauge used to get the shear strain?

At 45°, the cos 2theta term vanishes and the sin 2theta term is at its maximum, so the reading picks up the shear strain most clearly.

Question 8: What do the three rosette readings give you?

The full strain state at the point: epsilonx, epsilony and gammaxy. From these, the principal strains and stresses can be found.

Concept 5: From rosette strains to principal strains and stresses

The principal strains are the largest and smallest normal strains at the point. In those two special directions, the shear strain is zero. They are the most useful numbers for a designer, because a material often fails when its largest principal stress reaches a limit.

For a rectangular rosette, the principal strains have a neat form in terms of the three readings:

epsilon1,2 = (epsilona + epsilonc)/2 ± (1 / square root of 2) × square root of ((epsilona − epsilonb)² + (epsilonb − epsilonc)²)

The angle of the major principal strain, measured from gauge A, is found from:

tan 2thetap = gammaxy / (epsilona − epsilonc)

Once the principal strains are known, the principal stresses follow from Hooke's law for plane stress. With Young's modulus E and Poisson's ratio nu:

sigma1 = E/(1 − nu²) × (epsilon1 + nu × epsilon2), sigma2 = E/(1 − nu²) × (epsilon2 + nu × epsilon1)

Here E is in MPa, the strains are numbers (not microstrain), and nu is about 0.3 for steel. The factor E/(1 − nu²) is about 1.1 times E, so for steel with E = 200 GPa it is about 219 780 MPa.

Worked example. Using the rosette of Concept 4 with epsilona = 400, epsilonb = 300 and epsilonc = 100 microstrain, and E = 200 GPa, nu = 0.3, find the principal strains, the angle, and the principal stresses.

  1. Mean strain: (400 + 100)/2 = 250 microstrain.
  2. Shear strain: gammaxy = 100 microstrain (from Concept 4).
  3. Radius: (1/√2) × √((400 − 300)² + (300 − 100)²) = (1/√2) × √(10 000 + 40 000) = (1/√2) × √50 000 = 158.11 microstrain.
  4. Principal strains: epsilon1 = 250 + 158.11 = 408.11 microstrain, and epsilon2 = 250 − 158.11 = 91.89 microstrain.
  5. Angle: tan 2thetap = 100 / (400 − 100) = 1/3, so 2thetap = 18.43°, and thetap = 9.22°. This is measured from gauge A towards gauge B.
  6. Principal stresses: E/(1 − nu²) = 200 000 / 0.91 = 219 780 MPa. Then sigma1 = 219 780 × (408.11 + 0.3 × 91.89) × 10-6 = 95.75 MPa, and sigma2 = 219 780 × (91.89 + 0.3 × 408.11) × 10-6 = 47.10 MPa.
import math
ea, eb, ec = 400e-6, 300e-6, 100e-6
gamma = 2 * eb - ea - ec
mean = (ea + ec) / 2
r = math.sqrt((ea - eb)**2 + (eb - ec)**2) / math.sqrt(2)
e1 = mean + r
e2 = mean - r
print("e1 =", round(e1 * 1e6, 2), "microstrain")
print("e2 =", round(e2 * 1e6, 2), "microstrain")
theta_p = 0.5 * math.degrees(math.atan2(gamma, ea - ec))
print("theta_p =", round(theta_p, 2), "degrees")
E = 200000
nu = 0.3
k = E / (1 - nu**2)
s1 = k * (e1 + nu * e2)
s2 = k * (e2 + nu * e1)
print("s1 =", round(s1, 2), "MPa")
print("s2 =", round(s2, 2), "MPa")

e1 = 408.11 microstrain
e2 = 91.89 microstrain
theta_p = 9.22 degrees
s1 = 95.75 MPa
s2 = 47.1 MPa

Key ideaThe principal strains and stresses describe the same point in the directions where shear is zero. The rosette gives you the strain state, and Hooke's law converts it to stress.

TrapUsing the strain values in microstrain inside Hooke's law. Convert to plain numbers first (400 microstrain = 0.0004), or keep the microstrain and divide by 106 at the end, but do not mix the two.

Question 9: If the principal strain epsilon1 is larger than epsilon2, is sigma1 larger than sigma2?

Yes. The difference is sigma1 − sigma2 = E/(1 − nu²) × (1 − nu) × (epsilon1 − epsilon2), which is positive for a positive nu below 1 and epsilon1 larger than epsilon2.

Question 10: Why is the mean of epsilona and epsilonc the centre of the principal strain range?

The average of the normal strains in two perpendicular directions is the same for every pair of perpendicular directions. So it is the centre of the range, and the two principal strains are the centre plus and minus a radius.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original GATE-style questions written for practice. They are not past exam questions. Try each one on paper before reading the steps.

Statements about strain gauges

Which of the following statements about a strain gauge are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) Its resistance increases when it is stretched.
(B) Its gauge factor is a dimensionless number.
(C) A balanced Wheatstone bridge gives a non-zero output.
(D) A rectangular rosette uses gauges at 0, 60 and 120 degrees.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct. This is an MSQ.
  2. Which idea applies. R = rho L / A, the gauge factor definition, the bridge balance, and the rectangular rosette angles.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true, because stretching raises R. (B) is true, because GF = (ΔR/R)/epsilon is a ratio of dimensionless quantities. (C) is false, because a balanced bridge has zero output. (D) is false, because a rectangular rosette uses 0, 45 and 90 degrees.
  4. Check. Two statements are true, and each false one contradicts a definition in this topic.

Answer: (A) and (B) only.

Resistance change of a gauge

A strain gauge with R = 350 ohms and GF = 2 is strained by 1000 microstrain. What is the change in resistance, in ohms? This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. ΔR in ohms.
  2. Which idea applies. ΔR = GF × epsilon × R.
  3. Work it out. Strain = 1000 × 10-6 = 0.001. ΔR = 2 × 0.001 × 350 = 0.7 ohms.
  4. Check. A change of 0.7 ohms out of 350 ohms is 0.2 percent, which is twice the 0.1 percent of 500 microstrain, as expected.

Answer: 0.7 ohms.

Output of a quarter bridge

A quarter bridge with one active gauge (GF = 2) is excited by 10 V. The gauge is strained by 400 microstrain. Using the standard approximate rule, find the output voltage in mV. This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Vout in mV.
  2. Which idea applies. The quarter bridge rule Vout = Vex × GF × epsilon / 4.
  3. Work it out. Strain = 0.0004. Vout = 10 × 2 × 0.0004 / 4 = 0.008 / 4 = 0.002 V = 2 mV.
  4. Check. Half of the 4 mV that a half bridge would give, which matches the ratio 1 to 2 between a quarter and a half bridge.

Answer: 2 mV.

Shear strain from a rosette

A rectangular rosette reads epsilona = 250 microstrain, epsilonb = 400 microstrain and epsilonc = 150 microstrain. Find the shear strain gammaxy in microstrain. This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. gammaxy in microstrain.
  2. Which idea applies. gammaxy = 2 epsilonb − epsilona − epsilonc.
  3. Work it out. 2 × 400 = 800. Then 800 − 250 − 150 = 400 microstrain.
  4. Check. The 45° reading (300) is larger than the average of the other two readings (200) by 200 microstrain, and twice that gap is the shear strain, 400 microstrain.

Answer: 400 microstrain.

Which bridge is more sensitive

Four bridge arrangements, all with the same excitation Vex, gauge factor GF and strain epsilon, are compared. Which statements are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) The full bridge gives a larger output than the half bridge.
(B) The quarter bridge gives the largest output.
(C) The half bridge gives twice the output of the quarter bridge.
(D) The full bridge gives four times the output of the quarter bridge.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements about relative bridge sensitivity are correct. This is an MSQ.
  2. Which idea applies. Quarter bridge: Vex GF epsilon / 4. Half bridge: divide by 2. Full bridge: divide by 1.
  3. Work it out. The full bridge gives Vex GF epsilon and the half bridge gives Vex GF epsilon / 2, so (A) is true. The quarter bridge gives the smallest output, so (B) is false. The half bridge is twice the quarter, so (C) is true. The full bridge is four times the quarter, so (D) is true.
  4. Check. Ratios: full to quarter is 4 to 1, half to quarter is 2 to 1, and full to half is 2 to 1. All consistent.

Answer: (A), (C) and (D) only.

Principal strain from a rectangular rosette

A rectangular rosette on a steel part reads epsilona = 300 microstrain, epsilonb = 350 microstrain and epsilonc = 100 microstrain. Find the larger principal strain epsilon1 in microstrain. This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. epsilon1 in microstrain.
  2. Which idea applies. epsilon1,2 = (epsilona + epsilonc)/2 ± (1/√2) √((epsilona − epsilonb)² + (epsilonb − epsilonc)²).
  3. Work it out. Mean = (300 + 100)/2 = 200. The differences: 300 − 350 = −50, and 350 − 100 = 250. Squares: 2500 and 62 500, sum 65 000. Square root: √65 000 = 254.95. Divide by √2: 254.95 / 1.41421 = 180.28. So epsilon1 = 200 + 180.28 = 380.28 microstrain.
  4. Check. The same radius can be found from the shear strain: gamma = 2 × 350 − 300 − 100 = 300, so the radius is √(((300 − 100)/2)² + (300/2)²) = √(100² + 150²) = √32 500 = 180.28. This matches the value from the first formula, so the answer is consistent. The Python check below confirms the numbers.

Answer: about 380.28 microstrain.

Statements about rosettes and principal values

Which of the following statements about strain rosettes and principal values are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) A rectangular rosette gives all three strain components at a point.
(B) At a principal direction, the shear strain is zero.
(C) The principal stresses can be found from the principal strains with E and nu.
(D) The principal strain epsilon1 is always equal to epsilona.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct. This is an MSQ.
  2. Which idea applies. The rosette equations, the definition of principal strains, and Hooke's law for plane stress.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true: epsilonx, epsilony and gammaxy come from the three readings. (B) is true by the definition of principal directions. (C) is true by the plane stress formulas. (D) is false, because epsilon1 is the larger principal strain and does not have to be the reading at gauge A.
  4. Check. Three statements match the definitions, and (D) confuses the reading at one gauge with a principal value.

Answer: (A), (B) and (C) only.

Bridge output in a balanced state

A Wheatstone bridge is balanced, so the four arms have equal resistances, and then one arm is strained by a small amount. Which statement best describes the output? Select one.

(A) The output is exactly zero for any strain.
(B) The output is proportional to the strain for small strains.
(C) The output is proportional to the square of the strain.
(D) The output does not depend on the excitation voltage.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The best description of the output in a bridge with one strained arm.
  2. Which idea applies. The quarter bridge formula, Vout ≈ Vex × GF × epsilon / 4.
  3. Work it out. Strain makes one arm differ, so the output is no longer zero: (A) is wrong. The rule shows the output is linear in epsilon, so (B) is right. It is not quadratic, so (C) is wrong. It is proportional to Vex, so (D) is wrong.
  4. Check. Doubling the strain doubles the output, which is what (B) says.

Answer: (B).

import math
ea, eb, ec = 300e-6, 350e-6, 100e-6
mean = (ea + ec) / 2
r = math.sqrt((ea - eb)**2 + (eb - ec)**2) / math.sqrt(2)
e1 = mean + r
print("e1 =", round(e1 * 1e6, 2), "microstrain")

e1 = 380.28 microstrain

Exam-level 1: Rosette to principal strain (NAT, 2 marks)

A 0°/45°/90° rosette reads ε0 = 500, ε45 = 300, ε90 = −100 (×10−6). Find the major principal strain (×10−6), correct to 1 decimal place.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. ε1.
  2. Which idea applies. γxy = 2ε45 − ε0 − ε90. Then ε1 = (εx + εy)/2 + √(((εx − εy)/2)² + (γ/2)²).
  3. Work it out. γ = 600 − 400 = 200. Centre 200, radius √(300² + 100²) = 316.2. ε1 = 516.2.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
import math
e0, e45, e90 = 500, 300, -100
g = 2 * e45 - e0 - e90
print("%.1f %.1f" % ((e0 + e90) / 2 + math.hypot((e0 - e90) / 2, g / 2), (e0 + e90) / 2 - math.hypot((e0 - e90) / 2, g / 2)))

516.2 -116.2

Answer: about 516.2 × 10−6.

Exam-level 2: Gauge factor (NAT, 1 mark)

A 120 Ω gauge with gauge factor 2.1 is strained to 400 × 10−6. Find the change in resistance in Ω.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. ΔR = GF · ε · R.
  2. Which idea applies. Definition of gauge factor.
  3. Work it out. 2.1 · 400e−6 · 120 = 0.1008 Ω.
  4. Check. Tiny changes like this need a bridge.

Summary

Strain
Fractional change in length. Precise definition: epsilon = ΔL / L.
Microstrain
One millionth of a strain. Precise definition: 1 µε = 10-6.
Strain gauge
Sensor whose resistance changes with strain. Precise definition: a resistive grid bonded to the surface.
Gauge factor
Sensitivity of a gauge. Precise definition: GF = (ΔR/R)/epsilon, usually about 2.
Wheatstone bridge
Four-arm circuit that reads a small resistance change. Precise definition: output is the voltage difference between two divider midpoints.
Quarter bridge
One active arm. Precise definition: Vout ≈ Vex GF epsilon / 4.
Half bridge
Two active arms. Precise definition: output is twice the quarter bridge.
Full bridge
Four active arms. Precise definition: output is four times the quarter bridge.
Rosette
Three gauges at one point. Precise definition: measures the strain state at a point.
Rectangular rosette
Gauges at 0, 45 and 90 degrees. Precise definition: gammaxy = 2 epsilonb − epsilona − epsilonc.
Principal strain
Largest and smallest normal strains. Precise definition: directions with zero shear strain.
Principal stress
Largest and smallest normal stresses. Precise definition: planes with zero shear stress.
Poisson's ratio
Lateral to axial strain ratio. Precise definition: nu = −lateral / axial.
Plane stress
Zero stress perpendicular to a surface. Precise definition: sigmaz = 0.
Shear strain
Change in a right angle. Precise definition: gammaxy.

Remember these

  1. A strain gauge's resistance rises when it is stretched.
  2. ΔR = GF × epsilon × R, with GF about 2.
  3. Convert microstrain to a plain number before using it in a formula.
  4. A bridge gives a small voltage proportional to the strain.
  5. Quarter, half and full bridges give 1, 2 and 4 times Vex GF epsilon / 4.
  6. A rectangular rosette gives gammaxy = 2 epsilonb − epsilona − epsilonc.
  7. Principal strains come from the mean and a radius; principal stresses come from Hooke's law for plane stress.

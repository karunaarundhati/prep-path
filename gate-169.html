Concept of shear centre

The shear centre is the one point in a beam's cross-section where a sideways load can act so the beam bends without also twisting. It matters for GATE Robotics and Automation questions on beam design, because a load applied in the wrong place quietly adds unwanted torsion.

The idea in plain words

Welcome. This topic sounds grand, but the core idea is down to earth. Imagine you hold a plastic ruler by its two ends and push down on its middle. The ruler bends downward. Now imagine you push down on the middle, but a little off to one side. The ruler still bends, and it also starts to rotate, like a propeller. That rotation is called twisting, and it happens because the push was not in the right place.

The shear centre is the answer to a simple question: where exactly must the push act so that the beam bends but does not twist? Engineers call that special point the shear centre. For some cross-sections it is easy to guess. For others it is in a surprising place, sometimes outside the solid material altogether.

Here is an everyday picture. Think of a door held open by a hinge on one side. If you push the door at its middle, it swings, which is a clean, straight motion. If you push it near the edge away from the hinge, the door also turns on its frame. The place where you push decides whether the motion is clean or messy. A beam is the same. Its cross-section has a special spot where a load gives a clean bend.

Why does this matter for GATE? In Robotics and Automation (RA) papers, you meet beams in robot arms, frames and mounting brackets. A bracket made from a channel section is very common. If a designer loads the channel at the wrong point, the bracket twists, and the robot's position drifts. GATE questions on this topic often ask you to find the shear centre of a standard section, to say whether a load causes twist, or to find the twisting moment produced by a load that misses the shear centre. The ideas are short, but the marks come from being careful about where the load acts.

We will build the idea in steps. First we separate bending from twisting. Then we define the shear centre. Then we look at three kinds of section: symmetric, singly symmetric (the channel), and unsymmetric. Each step has a picture, a formula where needed, and a small worked example with every line shown.

Key terms

Read this list now, and come back to it whenever a word feels unfamiliar. Each entry gives the plain meaning first, then the more precise definition.

Beam
A long, straight structural member, such as a bar or girder, that carries loads across its length. Plain meaning: a long plank that holds weight. Precise definition: a member whose length is much greater than its cross-sectional dimensions, loaded mainly transversely.
Cross-section
The shape you see if you cut the beam straight across. Plain meaning: the outline of the beam's end. Precise definition: the plane figure obtained by cutting the beam perpendicular to its longitudinal axis.
Transverse load
A load that pushes across the beam, at right angles to its length. Plain meaning: a sideways push or a weight hanging from the middle. Precise definition: a force component perpendicular to the beam axis.
Bending
The beam curving under load. Plain meaning: the beam goes into a smile or frown shape. Precise definition: deformation in which the axis of the beam changes curvature.
Twisting (torsion)
The beam rotating about its own length, like wringing out a cloth. Plain meaning: each cross-section turns a little around the beam axis. Precise definition: rotation of cross-sections about the longitudinal axis caused by a torque.
Torque (twisting moment)
A turning effect that tries to twist something about an axis. Plain meaning: a push that makes something rotate. Precise definition: the product of a force and its perpendicular distance from the axis of rotation, T = F × d.
Shear centre (S)
The point in the cross-section through which a transverse load must act to produce bending without twisting. Plain meaning: the sweet spot for a sideways push. Precise definition: the point through which the resultant of the transverse shear forces must pass for pure bending, with no twist of the section.
Centroid (C)
The geometric centre of a shape, the average position of its area. Plain meaning: the balance point of the flat shape. Precise definition: the point whose coordinates are the area-weighted averages of the area's coordinates.
Symmetric section
A cross-section that looks the same when you flip it over a line. Plain meaning: it is a mirror image on one side. Precise definition: a section that has an axis of symmetry about which its geometry is mirror-symmetric.
Doubly symmetric section
A section symmetric about both of two perpendicular axes. Plain meaning: a mirror image both left to right and top to bottom. Precise definition: a section with two orthogonal axes of symmetry, such as an I-section, a rectangle or a circle.
Singly symmetric section
A section with exactly one axis of symmetry. Plain meaning: a mirror image in one direction only. Precise definition: a section with one axis of symmetry, such as a channel section or a T-section.
Open section
A thin-walled section that has a gap, so the walls do not form a closed loop. Plain meaning: a shape you could cut open and unroll. Precise definition: a thin-walled section whose wall outline is not a closed curve, such as a channel, an angle or an I-section.
Thin-walled section
A section made of thin plates or walls, where the wall thickness is much smaller than the other dimensions. Plain meaning: a sheet-metal shape. Precise definition: a section whose thickness t is small compared with its width and height, so that stress is treated as acting along the wall midline.
Shear force
The internal force that acts across a cross-section, trying to slide one part past another. Plain meaning: the force that tries to cut the beam. Precise definition: the resultant of the transverse shear stresses over a cross-section.
Shear flow
Shear force per unit length along a thin wall. Plain meaning: how much of the sliding force flows along each part of the wall. Precise definition: the shear stress multiplied by the wall thickness, q = τt.
Channel section
A C-shaped open section with a web and two flanges. Plain meaning: a shape like the letter C. Precise definition: a singly symmetric open section made of a vertical web and two horizontal flanges, all on the same side.
Web
The main vertical plate of a beam section. Plain meaning: the tall middle part. Precise definition: the wall that connects the flanges and carries most of the vertical shear.
Flange
A horizontal plate at the top or bottom of a section. Plain meaning: the wide top or bottom part. Precise definition: a wall that carries most of the bending stress, lying across the web.
Equilibrium
A state where all forces and moments on a body balance. Plain meaning: nothing is moving or spinning unexpectedly. Precise definition: the sum of forces and the sum of moments on a body are both zero.

Concept 1: Bending and twisting are different things

Let us start with the two motions that a beam can make. They look similar at first, so it helps to see them side by side.

Bending is the curving of the beam's length. Its axis (the line that runs along the middle of the beam) becomes curved. If you push the middle of a plank, the plank forms a shallow arch. Each cross-section tilts, but it does not spin.

Twisting is the spinning of each cross-section about the beam's length. Picture a wet towel being wrung out. Each slice of the towel turns a little more than the slice next to it. In a beam, a twist happens when a turning effect, called a torque, acts about the beam's axis.

A transverse load that acts through the right point causes bending only. A transverse load that acts elsewhere causes bending and twisting. The text diagram below shows the two cases for a channel section, seen from its end. The letter P is the load, and S is the shear centre.

    End view of a channel section (C shape, open to the right)

      ________________
     |                  top flange
     |
     |   web
     |
     |________________   bottom flange

    Case A: load P acts through the shear centre S
    S is outside the section, on the far side of the web.

        S  o  |   web
              |
              |  <-- P acts here, through S: bending only

    Case B: load P acts at the web centreline, away from S
    Result: bending AND twisting, because P is not at S

That diagram is busy, so here is the key idea in one line. A transverse load causes pure bending only when it passes through the shear centre. Any other line of action adds a torque about S equal to the load times the distance from S.

Key ideaThe torque produced by a transverse load P acting at distance d from the shear centre is T = P × d. This is the twisting moment that the beam must resist when the load is off the shear centre.

Notice that the twist depends on where the load is, not on how strong the beam is. A very strong beam still twists if the load misses the shear centre. The problem is geometric, not a matter of material strength.

Question 1: What is the twisting moment when a 500 N load acts 20 mm from the shear centre?

T = P × d = 500 N × 20 mm = 10 000 N mm, which is 10 N m.

Question 2: Does a load applied exactly at the centroid of a channel always produce pure bending?

No. For a channel the centroid and the shear centre are at different places, so a load through the centroid creates a torque about S. Only a load through S gives pure bending.

Concept 2: What the shear centre is and why it exists

Now we define the shear centre properly. Think about what happens inside a beam when it carries a sideways load. Each cross-section must carry a shear force, which is an internal force trying to slide one part of the section past the next. In a solid rectangle this force is spread across the whole area. In a thin-walled open section, it is carried along the thin walls as a shear flow, which is the shear force per unit length along the wall.

Here is the everyday analogy. Imagine a group of students carrying a long pole together. Each student pulls with some force, and their pulls add up to one total effect at one point. In a thin-walled section, the shear flows in each wall add up to one resultant force, and that resultant has a line of action. The shear centre is the point where that resultant must pass so that the section balances without any spin.

The formal definition, to remember exactly:

Shear centre S: the point through which the resultant of the transverse shear forces must act so that the beam bends with no twist.

Some useful facts follow directly from this definition.

  • The shear centre depends only on the shape of the cross-section, not on the size of the load.
  • For a load through S, the section has no twist, so the twisting moment is zero.
  • For a load through the centroid of a symmetric section, the shear centre and the centroid coincide, so there is no twist.
  • The shear centre can lie outside the material of the section. That is not a mistake. It is a point in the plane of the section where a force would have to act.

TipSay the definition in your own words before you use it: "a sideways load through this point bends the beam without spinning it." If you can say that, you can answer most GATE conceptual questions on the shear centre.

Question 3: Does the shear centre depend on the magnitude of the load?

No. It depends only on the geometry of the cross-section. The load size changes how much the beam bends, but not where the shear centre is.

Question 4: Can the shear centre lie outside the solid material of a section?

Yes. For an open thin-walled section such as a channel, the shear centre lies in the empty space beside the section.

Concept 3: Symmetric sections put the shear centre at the centroid

Some sections are so balanced that the shear centre is easy to find. These are the doubly symmetric sections: the I-section, the solid rectangle, the hollow square box and the circle. Each one looks the same when you flip it left to right and when you flip it top to bottom.

Why does symmetry help? Imagine that a sideways load acts on an I-section. The shear flows in the top flange and the bottom flange mirror each other, and the shear flows on the left and right of the web mirror each other too. The mirror images cancel any tendency to rotate. The only remaining effect is a clean sliding, and the resultant passes through the centre. For a doubly symmetric section, the centre is also the centroid.

Here is a simple text diagram of an I-section. The letter C marks the centroid, and the shear centre S sits at the same spot.

        ___________________
       |___________________|      top flange
                 | |
                 | |             web
                 | |
                 |C|  <-- centroid C = shear centre S
                 | |
       ___________________
      |___________________|      bottom flange

    Both mirror lines pass through C, so S = C.

Rule of thumb for this section type: for a doubly symmetric section, the shear centre is at the centroid. A load applied at the centre of the cross-section causes pure bending. This is why an I-beam in a building is loaded through its middle without special care.

GATE loves thisA question may show an I-section or a solid rectangle and ask where the shear centre is. The safe answer is "at the centroid, because the section is doubly symmetric". Do not spend time doing a calculation for these two shapes.

TrapSymmetry must be about both axes for the shortcut to hold. A section that is symmetric about only one axis, like a channel, does not have its shear centre at the centroid. The next concept shows why.

Question 5: Where is the shear centre of a solid rectangular section?

At the centroid, which is the centre of the rectangle, because the rectangle is symmetric about two perpendicular axes.

Concept 4: The channel section and its shear centre

The channel is the favourite example in this topic, so we will go through it slowly. A channel is shaped like the letter C. It has a web (the vertical back) and two flanges (the top and bottom arms). It is singly symmetric: it is a mirror image about one horizontal line running through the middle of the web. It is open: its walls do not close into a loop.

Because the channel has only one axis of symmetry, its shear centre must lie somewhere on that axis. It cannot lie on the centroid, because a load through the centroid makes the channel spin. It lies outside the section, beyond the web, on the far side from the open flanges. Here is the text picture. The letter S marks the shear centre, and e is the distance from the web centreline to S.

    Plan view, channel opening to the RIGHT

         S        web centreline
         o   e    |
                  |  ____________ top flange
                  |
                  |  (web, height h)
                  |
                  |____________ bottom flange

    S lies on the horizontal axis of symmetry,
    outside the section, at distance e from the web.

Now the formula. For a thin-walled channel of uniform thickness, with flange width b and web height h, both measured to the middle of the walls, the distance e from the web centreline to the shear centre is:

e = 3b² / (h + 6b)

Let us say what each symbol means:

  • e is the distance from the web centreline to the shear centre, in mm.
  • b is the flange width, from the web centreline to the flange tip, in mm.
  • h is the web height, measured between the flange centrelines, in mm.

Notice that the wall thickness t does not appear in the answer, so long as it is uniform. Let us test the formula on a small example.

Worked example. A channel has flange width b = 30 mm and web height h = 120 mm. Find e.

  1. Write the formula: e = 3b² / (h + 6b).
  2. Find b². We have b = 30, so b² = 30 × 30 = 900.
  3. Find 3b². Multiply 3 by 900, which gives 2700.
  4. Find 6b. Multiply 6 by 30, which gives 180.
  5. Find h + 6b. Add 120 and 180, which gives 300.
  6. Divide: e = 2700 / 300 = 9 mm.

So the shear centre is 9 mm outside the web. Let us confirm the arithmetic with a short Python check.

b = 30   # flange width in mm
h = 120  # web height in mm
e = 3 * b**2 / (h + 6 * b)
print("shear centre distance e =", e, "mm")

shear centre distance e = 9.0 mm

Now see why this matters. Suppose a 2 kN load acts at the web centreline, 9 mm from the shear centre. The torque about S is T = P × e = 2000 N × 9 mm = 18 000 N mm. That is 18 N m of twist that the designer must resist. If the load acts through S, the torque is zero.

P = 2000  # load in N
e = 9     # distance from shear centre in mm
T = P * e
print("torque =", T, "N mm =", T / 1000, "N m")

torque = 18000 N mm = 18.0 N m

TrapStudents often forget that e is measured from the web centreline, and that the shear centre is on the outside of the web. A common wrong answer puts the shear centre inside the flanges. Draw the C shape, mark the web, and then place S beyond the back of the web before you calculate.

Question 6: For a channel with b = 40 mm and h = 160 mm, what is e?

e = 3 × 40² / (160 + 6 × 40) = 3 × 1600 / (160 + 240) = 4800 / 400 = 12 mm.

Question 7: Why is the wall thickness not in the formula?

For a thin-walled channel of uniform thickness, the thickness cancels out when the shear flow is worked through. The result depends only on the centreline dimensions b and h.

Concept 5: Unsymmetric open sections

Some open sections have no axis of symmetry at all. The angle section, shaped like the letter L, is the usual example. Its two legs are different, and there is no mirror line that maps one leg onto the other.

For such a section, there is no shortcut. You cannot say "the shear centre lies on the axis of symmetry", because there is no such axis. Instead, the shear centre is found by a longer method. First you find the shear flow in each wall for a known transverse load. Then you add up the resultant. Finally you find the point where a force must act so that the moment about that point equals the moment of the shear flows. In exam questions, you are usually asked to recognise the situation rather than to do the full calculation.

Here is the text picture of an angle section, with the shear centre drawn in a general position outside the corner.

    Angle section (L shape)

     |
     |
     |    S (somewhere outside the corner, not on a symmetry line)
     |
     |____________


    Two legs of different length. No mirror line exists,
    so S cannot be placed by symmetry alone.

Key ideaThe more symmetry a section has, the more its shear centre is predictable. Doubly symmetric: at the centroid. Singly symmetric: on the symmetry axis. Unsymmetric: must be found by calculation.

For an open section in general, the shear centre is also important because it tells you how far the load must be moved to avoid twist. Designers who cannot place the load at S often brace the section against rotation, or choose a closed section such as a box, which resists torsion much better.

Question 8: Does an angle section have an axis of symmetry?

No. Its two legs are different, so no mirror line maps one leg onto the other. This is why its shear centre cannot be placed by symmetry alone.

Concept 6: Why the shear centre matters in beam design

Now bring the ideas together. Why should a designer care about a point that sits outside the material? The answer is that twisting can be far more damaging than the bending it accompanies.

Think about a robot arm bracket made from a channel. The bracket is bolted at one end and carries a tool at the other. If the tool load acts through the web centreline, the bracket bends and also twists. The twist rotates the tool slightly out of alignment. Repeated over many cycles, this drift reduces accuracy. The fix is simple in principle: place the load through the shear centre, or add bracing that stops the section from rotating.

Here is a short checklist that engineers use. It is also the right order to answer a GATE conceptual question on this topic.

  1. Identify the section. Is it doubly symmetric, singly symmetric or unsymmetric, and is it open or closed?
  2. Locate the shear centre. Use the centroid for doubly symmetric sections, the symmetry axis for singly symmetric sections, and calculation for unsymmetric ones.
  3. Find the line of action of the load. Is it through S, or not?
  4. If not, find the twisting moment T = P × d, where d is the perpendicular distance from S to the line of action.
  5. Decide whether the twist is acceptable, or whether the load should be moved or the section braced.

TipIn a GATE numerical, the question often gives a small section and a load, then asks for the twisting moment. Follow the five steps above and the question becomes a routine calculation.

A last note on closed sections. A closed thin-walled section, such as a rectangular box, resists torsion through a different and much stiffer mechanism. For that reason, the shear centre concept is mostly needed for open sections, where the twist can be large.

Question 9: A 4 kN load acts 9 mm from the shear centre of a channel. What is the twisting moment in N m?

T = 4000 N × 9 mm = 36 000 N mm = 36 N m.

Question 10: What is the first step in finding the shear centre of an unknown section?

Identify the section type: its symmetry (doubly, singly or none) and whether it is open or closed. That tells you which shortcut applies.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original GATE-style questions written for practice. They are not past exam questions. Work each one on paper first, then read the think steps.

Which statements describe the shear centre

Which of the following statements about the shear centre of a cross-section are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) It depends only on the geometry of the cross-section.
(B) A transverse load through it produces bending without twist.
(C) It always lies inside the solid material of the section.
(D) It must lie on the centroid for every section.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true. This is an MSQ, so more than one option may be correct.
  2. Which idea applies. The definition of the shear centre: the point through which a transverse load must pass to bend the section without twisting it.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true, because the location depends only on shape. (B) is true, by the definition. (C) is false, because the channel's shear centre lies outside the section. (D) is false, because the centroid and shear centre differ for a channel.
  4. Check. Two true statements and two false ones, which fits the definition.

Answer: (A) and (B) only.

Shear centre of a solid rectangle

A solid rectangular beam section is loaded transversely. Where must the load act to produce pure bending with no twist? Choose the correct answer.

(A) At the top edge
(B) At the centroid
(C) At the bottom-left corner
(D) Outside the section

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The location of the shear centre for a rectangle.
  2. Which idea applies. A rectangle is doubly symmetric, so its shear centre is at the centroid.
  3. Work it out. The rectangle has two mirror lines, both passing through its centre. So the shear centre is at the centroid, option (B).
  4. Check. The other options are corners or outside points, which would give a twist for a symmetric section.

Answer: (B) At the centroid.

Shear centre distance of a channel

A thin-walled channel of uniform thickness has flange width b = 40 mm and web height h = 160 mm, both measured to the wall centrelines. Find the distance e from the web centreline to the shear centre, in mm. This is a NAT (numerical answer) question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The distance e in mm.
  2. Which idea applies. The channel formula e = 3b² / (h + 6b).
  3. Work it out. b² = 1600, so 3b² = 4800. Next, 6b = 240, so h + 6b = 160 + 240 = 400. Then e = 4800 / 400 = 12.
  4. Check. The shear centre sits outside the web, and 12 mm is a sensible small distance for a section of this size.

Answer: e = 12 mm.

Twisting moment from an off-centre load

A channel has shear centre distance e = 12 mm from its web centreline. A transverse load of 5 kN acts along the web centreline. What is the twisting moment about the shear centre, in N m? This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The torque in N m.
  2. Which idea applies. Torque equals load times perpendicular distance from the shear centre.
  3. Work it out. The load is 5 kN = 5000 N. The distance is 12 mm. So T = 5000 × 12 = 60 000 N mm. Divide by 1000 to convert to N m: 60 N m.
  4. Check. Units: N times mm gives N mm, and dividing by 1000 gives N m. The answer is positive in magnitude.

Answer: 60 N m.

Which section has its shear centre on the centroid

Which of the following sections has its shear centre at the centroid? Select all that apply.

(A) Solid circle
(B) Channel section
(C) I-section with equal flanges
(D) Angle section

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which sections have S at the centroid. This is an MSQ.
  2. Which idea applies. Doubly symmetric sections have their shear centre at the centroid.
  3. Work it out. A solid circle has symmetry about every diameter, so it is doubly symmetric: yes. A channel has only one axis of symmetry: no. An I-section with equal flanges has two axes of symmetry: yes. An angle has no axis of symmetry: no.
  4. Check. Only the doubly symmetric sections, (A) and (C), qualify.

Answer: (A) and (C) only.

Finding the web depth from a target shear centre

A thin-walled channel of uniform thickness has flange width b = 30 mm. The designer wants the shear centre to lie e = 6 mm from the web centreline. Find the web height h, in mm, that achieves this. This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The value of h that makes e = 6 mm.
  2. Which idea applies. Start from e = 3b² / (h + 6b) and rearrange it to solve for h.
  3. Work it out. Multiply both sides by (h + 6b): e(h + 6b) = 3b². Divide by e: h + 6b = 3b² / e. So h = 3b² / e − 6b. Put in the numbers: 3b² = 3 × 900 = 2700. Then 2700 / 6 = 450. Next, 6b = 180. So h = 450 − 180 = 270 mm.
  4. Check. Substitute back: h + 6b = 270 + 180 = 450, and 3b² / 450 = 2700 / 450 = 6. This matches e = 6 mm.

Answer: h = 270 mm.

Torque with a load off the shear centre

A channel has b = 30 mm and h = 120 mm, uniform thickness. A transverse load of 4 kN acts at the tip of the top flange, which lies 30 mm beyond the web centreline on the open side. The shear centre is 9 mm from the web centreline on the far side of the web. Treat the load as acting along a line that is parallel to the web and passes through the flange tip. What is the twisting moment about the shear centre, in N m? This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The torque about S, in N m, when the load line is at the flange tip.
  2. Which idea applies. Torque equals load times perpendicular distance from S to the line of action.
  3. Work it out. Put the web centreline at x = 0, with the open side at positive x. The shear centre is at x = −9 mm. The load line is at x = +30 mm (the flange tip). The perpendicular distance from S to the load line is 30 − (−9) = 39 mm. The torque is T = 4000 × 39 = 156 000 N mm. Divide by 1000: 156 N m.
  4. Check. The distance is larger than 9 mm because the load moved to the far side. Using the earlier value 9 mm would give too small a torque, so the larger distance is expected.

Answer: 156 N m.

Statements about open and closed sections

Which of the following statements about the shear centre are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) A singly symmetric open section has its shear centre on its axis of symmetry.
(B) An unsymmetric open section has its shear centre on the centroid.
(C) Changing the load magnitude moves the shear centre.
(D) A doubly symmetric section has its shear centre at the centroid.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct. This is an MSQ, so test each option separately.
  2. Which idea applies. Symmetry rules for the shear centre, and the fact that it depends only on geometry.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true: a singly symmetric section has S on its symmetry axis. (B) is false, because an unsymmetric section need not have S at the centroid; it is found by calculation. (C) is false, because S depends only on shape. (D) is true, by the doubly symmetric rule.
  4. Check. Two correct options, and each false option breaks a rule stated in this topic.

Numbers for a channel in a bracket

A bracket made from a channel has shear centre distance e = 9 mm. A load of 1.5 kN acts through the web centreline. What is the twisting moment, in N mm? This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The torque in N mm.
  2. Which idea applies. T = P × e.
  3. Work it out. P = 1.5 kN = 1500 N. T = 1500 × 9 = 13 500 N mm.
  4. Check. Dividing 13 500 by 9 gives 1500, which returns the load. Good.

Exam-level 2: Location facts (MSQ, 2 marks)

Which are TRUE? (A) For a doubly symmetric section, the shear centre is at the centroid. (B) For an angle section, it is at the corner where the legs meet. (C) For a T section, it lies at the junction of flange and web centrelines. (D) Loads through the shear centre cause no twisting.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Where the shear centre lies.
  2. Which idea applies. For sections made of thin plates meeting at one point, the shear flows all pass through that point.
  3. Work it out. All four are true.
  4. Check. A channel is the common example where it lies outside the section.

Answer: (A), (B), (C) and (D).

Exam-level 3: Twisting moment (NAT, 2 marks)

A vertical load of 10 kN acts through the web centreline of the channel above. What torque does it cause about the shear centre?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Load times offset.
  2. Which idea applies. Torque = P · e.
  3. Work it out. 10 · 0.0225 = 0.225 kN·m = 225 N·m.
  4. Check. Moving the load to the shear centre removes this twist.

Answer: 225 N·m.

Summary

Bending
The curving of a beam's axis under load. Plain meaning: the beam bows. Precise definition: change in curvature of the beam axis.
Twisting
Rotation of cross-sections about the beam's length. Plain meaning: the beam spins. Precise definition: rotation caused by torque about the axis.
Torque
A turning effect. Precise definition: force times perpendicular distance, T = P × d.
Shear centre
The point where a transverse load gives bending with no twist. Precise definition: the point through which the resultant of transverse shear forces must act.
Centroid
The geometric centre of a shape. Precise definition: area-weighted average position.
Doubly symmetric section
A section with two perpendicular axes of symmetry. Precise definition: its shear centre is at the centroid.
Singly symmetric section
A section with one axis of symmetry. Precise definition: its shear centre lies on that axis.
Unsymmetric section
A section with no axis of symmetry. Precise definition: its shear centre must be found by calculation.
Open section
A thin-walled section with a gap. Precise definition: its walls do not form a closed loop.
Closed section
A thin-walled section whose walls form a loop, such as a box. Precise definition: its wall outline is a closed curve.
Channel section
A C-shaped open section. Precise definition: a web with two flanges on one side.
Web
The main vertical plate of a section. Precise definition: the wall connecting the flanges.
Flange
A horizontal plate at the top or bottom of a section. Precise definition: a wall carrying most bending stress.
Shear flow
Shear force per unit length along a thin wall. Precise definition: q = τt.
Channel formula
e = 3b² / (h + 6b), the shear centre distance for a uniform channel. Precise definition: e is measured from the web centreline.
Thin-walled section
A section whose wall thickness is small compared with its other dimensions. Precise definition: shear stress is treated as acting along the wall midline.

Remember these

  1. The shear centre is where a transverse load must act to bend the section without twisting it.
  2. It depends only on the geometry of the cross-section, not on the size of the load.
  3. Doubly symmetric sections have the shear centre at the centroid.
  4. A channel's shear centre lies on its symmetry axis, outside the section, beyond the web.
  5. For a uniform thin-walled channel, e = 3b² / (h + 6b), measured from the web centreline.
  6. A load off the shear centre produces a twisting moment T = P × d.
  7. Unsymmetric sections have no symmetry shortcut, so their shear centre is found by calculation.

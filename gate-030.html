Counting: permutations & combinations

Counting tells you how many ways something can happen. Permutations count ordered arrangements, combinations count selections where order does not matter, and both are the engine behind most probability questions in GATE DA. Once you can count the outcomes, the probability is just a division.

The idea in plain words

Suppose you have three shirts and two pairs of trousers. How many outfits can you make? For each shirt there are two trousers, so the answer is 3 × 2 = 6. Every choice of shirt pairs with every choice of trousers. That simple multiplication is the whole idea of counting.

Counting problems in GATE usually ask one of two questions. The first is: how many ways can I arrange these things in a line, where the order matters? The second is: how many ways can I choose some of these things, where only the group matters? Arranging is called a permutation. Choosing is called a combination. Confusing the two is the most common mistake in counting, so this topic spends time on how to tell them apart.

Why does this matter for GATE DA? A probability question is a ratio: the number of favourable outcomes divided by the total number of outcomes. Both numbers usually come from counting. If you can count well, many probability questions become short.

Key ideaAsk yourself whether order matters. If it does, you are arranging (permutations). If it does not, you are choosing (combinations). Then count.

Key terms

Fundamental counting principle
If one choice has a options and a second independent choice has b options, the two together have a × b options.
Factorial n!
The product n × (n - 1) × ... × 2 × 1. By convention 0! = 1. Plain meaning: the number of ways to line up n distinct things.
Permutation
An ordered arrangement of objects. The order of the chosen items matters.
nPr (permutations of r from n)
The number of ordered ways to pick and arrange r objects from n distinct objects: nPr = n!/(n - r)!.
Combination
An unordered selection of objects. Only the group matters, not the order.
nCr (binomial coefficient)
The number of ways to choose r objects from n, with no regard to order: nCr = n!/(r!(n - r)!). Also written C(n, r).
Circular permutation
An arrangement around a circle, where rotations of the same arrangement count as one.
Repetition
Allowing the same object to be used more than once, or identical objects in a list.
Pascal's rule
nCr = (n - 1)C(r - 1) + (n - 1)Cr. A rule for building the table of combinations one row at a time.

Step 1: The counting principle and factorials

Multiply independent choices

The fundamental counting principle says: when a process has several steps, and each step's options do not depend on the earlier choices, multiply the numbers of options. Two coins give 2 × 2 = 4 results. Three dice give 6 × 6 × 6 = 216 results.

Factorials

Factorials count full arrangements. Think of arranging 4 distinct books on a shelf. There are 4 choices for the first book, 3 for the second, 2 for the third and 1 for the last. So there are 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 4! = 24 arrangements.

n! = n × (n - 1) × (n - 2) × ... × 2 × 1, with 0! = 1

The factorial grows very fast. 5! = 120, 6! = 720 and 10! = 3 628 800. Keep the numbers as factorials until the end, and cancel common factors early.

Worked example: arranging the word GATE

  1. Count the letters. G, A, T and E are four different letters.
  2. First position. Any of the 4 letters can go first: 4 choices.
  3. Second position. One letter is used, so 3 choices remain.
  4. Third position. 2 choices remain.
  5. Last position. 1 choice remains.
  6. Multiply. 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24 distinct arrangements.

TrapThe factorial of a sum is not the sum of factorials. (3 + 2)! = 120, but 3! + 2! = 8. Compute each factorial separately, then multiply or divide.

Question: How many ways can 5 people sit in a row of 5 chairs?

All 5 people are distinct and every seat is filled, so the count is 5! = 120.

Question: How many 3-letter codes can be made from 26 letters if letters may repeat?

Each of the 3 positions has 26 options, and repetition is allowed. So the count is 26 × 26 × 26 = 17 576.

Step 2: Permutations

Choosing some and arranging them

Suppose you want to award a gold, silver and bronze medal to 3 runners chosen from 8. The order matters, because gold is not the same as silver. The first medal has 8 choices, the second has 7 and the third has 6. So the count is 8 × 7 × 6 = 336. This is written 8P3:

nPr = n! / (n - r)! so 8P3 = 8!/5! = 8 × 7 × 6 = 336

Here n is the number of distinct objects available, and r is the number you place in order. The formula cancels the factors (n - r)! that would otherwise be counted.

Worked example: every step

How many ways can 3 letters be chosen from {A, B, C, D, E} and arranged in a row?

  1. Identify n and r. n = 5 letters available, r = 3 positions to fill.
  2. Apply the formula. 5P3 = 5!/(5 - 3)! = 5!/2!.
  3. Compute. 5! = 120 and 2! = 2, so 120/2 = 60.
  4. Check. Directly: 5 × 4 × 3 = 60. The two methods agree.

Repetition and identical objects

If repetition is allowed, each position has all n options. Then the count is nr. A 4-digit PIN with digits 0 to 9 gives 104 = 10 000 options.

If some objects are identical, many arrangements look the same. The word BANANA has 6 letters, but the three A's are identical, and so are the two N's. Swapping two identical A's does not create a new word. So we divide by the number of ways to permute the identical letters:

Words from BANANA = 6! / (3! × 2! × 1!) = 720 / (6 × 2 × 1) = 60

Here 3! counts the orders of the three A's, 2! counts the orders of the two N's, and 1! counts the single B.

Circular permutations

Seat 5 people around a round table. Rotating everyone one seat gives the same seating order, because only the neighbours matter. So fix one person's position and arrange the remaining 4 around them. The count is (5 - 1)! = 24. In general, the number of circular arrangements of n distinct objects is (n - 1)!.

GATE loves this"Around a circle" always means divide by n, or equivalently fix one object and arrange the rest. Forgetting this gives an answer n times too large.

TrapFor a necklace or a bracelet, you may also flip the object over, which halves the count again. A round table does not allow this, because the people sit on one side only.

Question: How many 2-digit numbers can be made from the digits 1 to 5 with no repeated digit?

This is 5P2 = 5 × 4 = 20. Order matters, because 12 and 21 are different numbers.

Question: How many arrangements of the letters in MISSISSIPPI are there?

There are 11 letters: 1 M, 4 I's, 4 S's and 2 P's. So the count is 11!/(1! × 4! × 4! × 2!) = 39 916 800/(24 × 24 × 2) = 34 650.

Step 3: Combinations and their properties

Choosing without order

Now suppose you need a committee of 3 people from 8. The order of the members does not matter, because a committee of Asha, Ben and Chen is the same as Ben, Chen and Asha. Each committee is counted 3! = 6 times in the permutation count 8P3 = 336. So the number of committees is 336/6 = 56. This is written 8C3:

nCr = n! / (r! (n - r)!) so 8C3 = 8!/(3! × 5!) = 56

The r! in the denominator removes the orders within the chosen group. The (n - r)! removes the unchosen objects, which do not appear in the group.

Worked example: every step

Find 6C2, the number of ways to pick 2 people from 6.

  1. Write the formula. 6C2 = 6!/(2! × 4!).
  2. Cancel early. 6!/4! = 6 × 5 = 30, so 6C2 = 30/2!.
  3. Divide. 2! = 2, so 30/2 = 15.
  4. Check. List them as pairs from {1,2,3,4,5,6}: 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 (5 pairs), 23, 24, 25, 26 (4 pairs), 34, 35, 36 (3 pairs), 45, 46 (2 pairs), 56 (1 pair). Total 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 15.

Properties you will use

  • Symmetry. nCr = nCn-r. Choosing r people to include is the same as choosing n - r people to leave out.
  • Edge cases. nC0 = 1 and nCn = 1, because there is exactly one way to take nothing or everything.
  • Pascal's rule. nCr = (n-1)Cr-1 + (n-1)Cr. Check with n = 5, r = 2: 5C2 = 10 and 4C1 + 4C2 = 4 + 6 = 10.
  • Row sum. The sum over all r of nCr equals 2n. This is because each object is either in or out of a chosen group.

Key ideanCr is the permutation count nPr divided by r!. Use combinations when you pick a group, and permutations when you assign distinct roles or positions.

TrapDo not mix up "choose" and "arrange". Choosing 2 members for a committee is nC2. Choosing a president and a vice president from the same people is nP2, which is twice as large.

Question: What is 10C7?

By symmetry, 10C7 = 10C3 = 10!/(3! × 7!) = (10 × 9 × 8)/(3 × 2 × 1) = 120.

Question: Why does the row sum of combinations equal 2n?

Each of the n objects is either in the chosen group or not, which gives 2 choices per object. Multiplying over all n objects gives 2n subsets. Counting those subsets by size gives the sum of nCr over r.

Step 4: Restrictions, complements, and probability

Handling restrictions

Many GATE counting questions add a rule. Two people must sit together, or at least one woman must be in the committee. Two methods handle most rules:

  1. Glue. If some objects must stay together, treat the group as one block. Count the arrangements of the blocks, then multiply by the arrangements inside each block.
  2. Complement. If the rule is "at least one", count the opposite event "none" and subtract from the total.

Worked example: two people must sit together

Three people, A, B and C, sit in a row. In how many ways can A and B sit next to each other?

  1. Glue A and B. Treat AB (or BA) as a single block. Now there are 2 units: the block and C.
  2. Arrange the units. 2 units can be arranged in 2! = 2 ways.
  3. Arrange inside the block. A and B can be in order AB or BA, which is 2! = 2 ways.
  4. Multiply. 2 × 2 = 4.

The Python check below confirms this by listing all 6 arrangements.

Worked example: committee with at least one woman

A committee of 3 is chosen from 4 men and 3 women. How many committees contain at least one woman?

  1. Total committees. 7C3 = 35.
  2. Complement. "No woman" means all 3 are men: 4C3 = 4.
  3. Subtract. 35 - 4 = 31 committees contain at least one woman.
  4. Check. Count directly: 1 woman gives 3C1 × 4C2 = 3 × 6 = 18. 2 women give 3C2 × 4C1 = 3 × 4 = 12. 3 women give 3C3 = 1. Total 18 + 12 + 1 = 31. The two methods agree.

Counting for probability

Probability is favourable outcomes divided by total outcomes, when outcomes are equally likely. Combinations give both counts. A bag holds 5 red and 3 blue balls. Two balls are drawn at random without replacement. The total number of ways to pick 2 of the 8 balls is 8C2 = 28. The number of ways to pick 2 red balls is 5C2 = 10. So:

P(both red) = 5C2 / 8C2 = 10/28 = 5/14
import math
from itertools import permutations
from fractions import Fraction

print("5P2 =", math.perm(5, 2))
print("5C2 =", math.comb(5, 2))
print("distinct words from BANANA:", len(set(permutations("BANANA"))))
print("Pascal check:", math.comb(5, 2) == math.comb(4, 1) + math.comb(4, 2))
print("row sum of C(6,k):", sum(math.comb(6, k) for k in range(7)), 2**6)
print("A and B sit together:", sum(1 for p in permutations("ABC") if abs(p.index("A") - p.index("B")) == 1))
print("P(both red) =", Fraction(math.comb(5, 2), math.comb(8, 2)))
print("circular arrangements of 5 people:", math.factorial(5 - 1))
5P2 = 20
5C2 = 10
distinct words from BANANA: 60
Pascal check: True
row sum of C(6,k): 64 64
A and B sit together: 4
P(both red) = 5/14
circular arrangements of 5 people: 24

TipBefore you count, write a one-line rule: "order matters, so permutation" or "the group matters, so combination". Then write the formula with the numbers in it, before you simplify.

Question: From 6 students, how many ways to choose a president and a secretary?

Order matters, because the two roles are different. So the count is 6P2 = 6 × 5 = 30. Choosing just a pair without roles would be 6C2 = 15.

Question: A bag has 4 red and 4 blue balls. What is the probability that 2 balls drawn at random are both blue?

The total is 8C2 = 28, and the favourable count is 4C2 = 6. So P = 6/28 = 3/14.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original GATE-style questions written for practice. They are not past exam questions.

Arranging four letters

In how many different orders can the four letters of the word GATE be written in a row?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The number of distinct arrangements of 4 letters.
  2. Which idea applies. All letters are distinct and all are used, so this is a full permutation, 4!.
  3. Work it out. 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24.
  4. Check. The formula 4P4 = 4!/0! = 24 gives the same answer.

Answer: 24.

Which counts are correct?

Which of the following are true? Select all that apply.

(A) 6P2 = 30. (B) 6C2 = 15. (C) 6C4 = 15. (D) 6P2 = 15.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Check each equation.
  2. Which idea applies. nPr = n!/(n - r)! and nCr = n!/(r!(n - r)!).
  3. Work it out. 6P2 = 6 × 5 = 30, so (A) is true and (D) is false. 6C2 = 15 (computed in the text), so (B) is true. 6C4 = 6C2 = 15 by symmetry, so (C) is true.
  4. Check. 6P2 = 2! × 6C2 = 2 × 15 = 30, which agrees with (A).

Answer: (A), (B) and (C).

Two students who should not both be chosen

A group of 3 students is chosen from 8 students. Two specific students, X and Y, must not both be chosen. How many groups are allowed? Give the number (NAT style).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Groups of 3 that do not contain both X and Y.
  2. Which idea applies. The complement: count all groups, then remove the bad ones.
  3. Work it out. Total groups: 8C3 = 56. Bad groups contain both X and Y. Then the third member is one of the remaining 6 students: 6C1 = 6. So allowed groups = 56 - 6 = 50.
  4. Check. Count the allowed groups directly. Groups with X but not Y: choose 2 from the other 6, which is 6C2 = 15. Groups with Y but not X: also 15. Groups with neither: choose 3 from 6, which is 6C3 = 20. Total 15 + 15 + 20 = 50.

Answer: 50.

Two people side by side around a table

Five people sit around a round table. Two specific people, P and Q, must sit next to each other. How many circular seatings are possible? Give the number (NAT style).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Circular arrangements where P and Q are adjacent.
  2. Which idea applies. Glue P and Q into one block, then count circular arrangements of the blocks, then the order inside the block.
  3. Work it out. With the block, there are 4 units around the table. Circular arrangements of 4 units: (4 - 1)! = 6. Inside the block P and Q can be in 2 orders. Total: 6 × 2 = 12.
  4. Check. Total circular seatings of 5 people are (5 - 1)! = 24. Fix P's seat. Q can sit in any of the 4 other seats, and exactly 2 of them are next to P. So the fraction with Q adjacent is 2/4 = 1/2, and 24 × 1/2 = 12.

Answer: 12.

Words starting with a fixed letter

How many different words (arrangements of all letters, whether or not they make sense) can be formed from the letters of BANANA if the word must begin with B? Give the number (NAT style).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Arrangements of the remaining letters after fixing the first letter.
  2. Which idea applies. Fix the first position, then count the arrangements of the rest with identical letters.
  3. Work it out. The first letter is B, leaving A, A, A, N, N. Their arrangements: 5!/(3! × 2!) = 120/12 = 10.
  4. Check. Earlier, all words from BANANA numbered 60. Words starting with B number 10. Words starting with A: the rest are B, A, A, N, N, giving 5!/(2! × 2!) = 30. Words starting with N: the rest are B, A, A, A, N, giving 5!/3! = 20. The total is 10 + 30 + 20 = 60, which agrees.

Answer: 10.

Exactly two red balls

A bag contains 5 red and 3 blue balls. Three balls are drawn at random without replacement. Find the probability that exactly 2 are red. Give the answer as a fraction.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A probability, so favourable and total counts are needed.
  2. Which idea applies. Combinations for both the total and the favourable count.
  3. Work it out. Total ways to draw 3 balls from 8: 8C3 = 56. Favourable: choose 2 red from 5 (5C2 = 10) and 1 blue from 3 (3C1 = 3). That gives 10 × 3 = 30. So P = 30/56 = 15/28.
  4. Check. The Python check below confirms the fraction. The other possible counts (0, 1, 3 red) must add to 56 - 30 = 26 outcomes.
import math
from fractions import Fraction

favourable = math.comb(5, 2) * math.comb(3, 1)
total = math.comb(8, 3)
print(favourable, total, Fraction(favourable, total))
other = sum(math.comb(5, k) * math.comb(3, 3 - k) for k in range(4)) - favourable
print("outcomes not exactly 2 red:", other)
30 56 15/28
outcomes not exactly 2 red: 26

Answer: 15/28.

A committee with girls and boys

A committee of 3 is chosen from 4 boys and 3 girls. Which of the following are true? Select all that apply.

(A) The total number of committees is 35. (B) The number of committees with at least one girl is 31. (C) The number of committees with exactly 2 girls is 12. (D) The number of committees with exactly 2 boys is 6.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Test each count.
  2. Which idea applies. Combinations, and the complement for "at least one".
  3. Work it out. (A) Total: 7C3 = 35, true. (B) At least one girl: 35 minus the all-boy committees 4C3 = 4, giving 31, true. (C) Exactly 2 girls: 3C2 × 4C1 = 3 × 4 = 12, true. (D) Exactly 2 boys means one girl: 4C2 × 3C1 = 6 × 3 = 18, not 6, so false.
  4. Check. The four cases by number of girls (0, 1, 2, 3) are 4, 18, 12 and 1. They sum to 35, which matches (A).

Answer: (A), (B) and (C).

A Pascal identity in disguise

In a group of 10 people, a sub-committee of size 3 or size 4 is formed. The total number of possible sub-committees of these two sizes equals 11C4. Find the number of sub-committees of size 3 or 4 (NAT style).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The count of sub-committees of size 3 plus size 4, and a check against 11C4.
  2. Which idea applies. Combinations, and Pascal's rule.
  3. Work it out. 10C3 = 120 and 10C4 = 210, so the total is 120 + 210 = 330. Pascal's rule with n = 11 and r = 4 gives 11C4 = 10C3 + 10C4 = 330, which agrees.
  4. Check. Directly, 11C4 = 11!/(4! × 7!) = (11 × 10 × 9 × 8)/(4 × 3 × 2 × 1) = 7920/24 = 330.

Answer: 330.

Exam-level 1: arrangements with repeated letters (NAT, 2 marks)

Consider the letters of the word STATISTICS. Letters that are the same cannot be told apart, so two arrangements are the same if they differ only by swapping equal letters. The word has 10 letters: three S, three T, two I, one A and one C. First find the number of different arrangements of all 10 letters. Then find how many of them have all three S's next to each other. Give the second number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The number of arrangements in which the three S's form one block.
  2. Which idea applies. The multinomial formula n! / (n1! n2! ...) for arrangements with repeats, and treating a group of letters as a single block.
  3. Work it out. All arrangements: 10! / (3! x 3! x 2!) = 3628800 / 72 = 50400. For the S's to be together, glue them into one block SSS. Now there are 8 items: the block, three T, two I, one A and one C. These give 8! / (3! x 2!) = 40320 / 12 = 3360 arrangements.
  4. Check. The Python check computes both counts with the factorial function.
from math import factorial as fact

total = fact(10) // (fact(3) * fact(3) * fact(2))
together = fact(8) // (fact(3) * fact(2))
print("all arrangements:", total)
print("with the three S's together:", together)
all arrangements: 50400
with the three S's together: 3360

Answer: 3360. (The total number of arrangements is 50400.)

Exam-level 2: committee choices (MSQ, 2 marks)

A club has 5 men and 4 women. A committee of 3 people is chosen at random, and every group of 3 is equally likely. Which of the following statements are true? Select all that apply.

(A) There are 84 possible committees. (B) There are 10 committees with only men. (C) There are 74 committees with at least one woman. (D) There are 40 committees with exactly two women.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which of the four counts are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. Combinations C(n, k), the complement trick for "at least one", and choosing the two groups separately for "exactly".
  3. Work it out. The total is C(9, 3) = 84, so (A) is true. All-men committees: C(5, 3) = 10, so (B) is true. At least one woman is the total minus all-men: 84 - 10 = 74, so (C) is true. Exactly two women: choose 2 of the 4 women and 1 of the 5 men, which gives C(4, 2) x C(5, 1) = 6 x 5 = 30. So (D) is false, since it is 30, not 40.
  4. Check. The Python check computes each count with the combination function.
from math import comb

print("total:", comb(9, 3))
print("all men:", comb(5, 3))
print("at least one woman:", comb(9, 3) - comb(5, 3))
print("exactly two women:", comb(4, 2) * comb(5, 1))
total: 84
all men: 10
at least one woman: 74
exactly two women: 30

Answer: (A), (B) and (C). (D) is wrong, because exactly two women can be chosen in 30 ways.

Exam-level 3: seating with a restriction (NAT, 2 marks)

Six people, including two specific people P and Q, sit around a round table. Two seatings are the same if one is a rotation of the other, so the number of seatings is 5! = 120 before any restriction. Find the number of seatings in which P and Q are not sitting next to each other. Give the number. Count the seatings where P and Q are next to each other, then subtract that from the total.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The number of seatings in which P and Q are not adjacent.
  2. Which idea applies. Circular permutations, plus the complement: total minus the bad cases.
  3. Work it out. Total seatings around a table of 6 is (6 - 1)! = 120. If P and Q are next to each other, glue them into one block. Around the table there are then 5 units, so (5 - 1)! = 24 arrangements, and the block can face either way, giving 2 x 24 = 48. The non-adjacent seatings are 120 - 48 = 72.
  4. Check. The Python check lists every seating with one person fixed in seat 0, which covers each rotation exactly once, and counts the good ones.
from itertools import permutations

count = 0
total = 0
for perm in permutations(range(1, 6)):
    seats = (0,) + perm
    pos = {p: i for i, p in enumerate(seats)}
    total += 1
    if abs(pos[1] - pos[2]) not in (1, 5):
        count += 1
print("all seatings:", total)
print("P and Q not adjacent:", count)
all seatings: 120
P and Q not adjacent: 72

Answer: 72.

Exam-level 4: binary strings with no adjacent ones (NAT, 2 marks)

A 6-bit binary string is a row of six digits, each either 0 or 1. Take all the 6-bit strings that contain exactly three 1s, and treat each one as equally likely. Find the number of these strings in which no two 1s are next to each other, then the probability that a randomly chosen string from this set has that property. Give the probability as a fraction in lowest terms.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The probability that three 1s, among six positions, are never adjacent.
  2. Which idea applies. Count favourable strings and all strings with three 1s using combinations, then divide.
  3. Work it out. The number of strings with exactly three 1s is C(6, 3) = 20. To place three 1s in a row of six with no two touching, you can list the cases by hand: the three 1s need 3 + 2 = 5 positions including gaps, which leaves one free position. The free position can go in any of 4 places, so there are 4 favourable strings. The probability is 4/20 = 1/5.
  4. Check. The Python check tries all 64 strings of six bits and counts the favourable ones.
from itertools import product
from math import comb
from fractions import Fraction

count = 0
for bits in product((0, 1), repeat=6):
    if sum(bits) == 3 and all(not (bits[i] == 1 and bits[i + 1] == 1) for i in range(5)):
        count += 1
print("favourable strings:", count)
print("strings with three 1s:", comb(6, 3))
print("probability:", Fraction(count, comb(6, 3)))
favourable strings: 4
strings with three 1s: 20
probability: 1/5

Answer: 1/5.

Summary

Fundamental counting principle
Multiply the number of options at each independent step.
Factorial n!
The number of ways to arrange n distinct objects in a line, with 0! = 1.
Permutation
An ordered choice or arrangement, where order matters.
nPr
n!/(n - r)!, the ordered count of r from n.
Combination
An unordered choice, where only the group matters.
nCr
n!/(r!(n - r)!), the unordered count of r from n.
Repetition count
nr ordered choices when items may repeat.
Identical objects
Divide n! by the factorial of each group of identical objects.
Circular permutation
(n - 1)! arrangements of n distinct objects around a circle.
Symmetry of combinations
nCr = nCn-r.
Pascal's rule
nCr = (n-1)Cr-1 + (n-1)Cr.
Row sum
The sum of nCr over all r is 2n.
Gluing
Treat objects that must stay together as one block, then multiply by the order inside the block.
Complement counting
For "at least one", subtract the count of "none" from the total.
Counting for probability
P(E) = favourable count / total count, both counted with combinations when draws are unordered.

Remember these

  • Order matters means permutation. The group matters means combination.
  • nCr = nPr / r!. Use it to switch between the two.
  • Around a circle, the count is (n - 1)!, not n!.
  • Identical letters divide the count by the factorial of how many are identical.
  • For "at least one", count the complement and subtract.
  • When two things must go together, glue them into one block.

Deflection of beams

A loaded beam bends, and its deflection is how far each point moves sideways from its original straight line. Engineers limit deflection so that floors do not sag and robot arms do not droop. This page builds the double integration method, Macaulay's method, the moment-area method and the standard formulas, all of which are common GATE Robotics and Automation questions.

The idea in plain words

Welcome. Hold a ruler at one end, on a table, so that the other end sticks out past the edge. Put a coin on the free end. The ruler dips. The more coins you add, the lower it goes. The end moves down because the ruler bends under the load. This downward movement is the deflection. Engineers call the bent shape of the beam the deflected curve, and they need to know its exact shape, not just the end point, so that they can check that a beam is stiff enough for its job.

Here is the key picture. A beam that bends is like a curved road. At each point along the road, the bend is sharper or gentler. A sharp bend is a large curvature, and it is caused by a large bending moment. A stiff beam, with a large EI, bends less for the same moment, just as a thick, stiff rod is harder to curl than a thin one. The bending moment, the stiffness and the curvature are linked by one short rule. From that rule, we can calculate the whole shape of the beam.

Why does this matter for GATE? Deflection questions are a standard part of GATE Robotics and Automation (RA) mechanics. They ask for the tip deflection of a cantilever, the deflection at the centre of a simply supported beam, the slope at a support, or the position of the largest deflection. Most of these can be answered by standard formulas, but you must understand where those formulas come from, so that you can use the methods when the loading is unusual.

We will go slowly. First we learn the curvature rule, then the double integration method, then the standard formulas, then Macaulay's method for mixed loads, and finally the moment-area method. Every step is shown with small numbers.

Key terms

Read this list once now, and return to it whenever a word feels new.

Deflection (y)
The sideways (usually downward) movement of a point on the beam from its original straight position. Plain meaning: how far the beam dips. Unit: mm or m.
Slope (theta or dy/dx)
The angle of the beam's tangent at a point, in radians. Plain meaning: how steep the beam is at that point. For small angles, slope is the rate of change of deflection along the beam.
Curvature
How sharply the beam bends at a point. Plain meaning: the tightness of the bend. It is the reciprocal of the radius of curvature.
Flexural rigidity (EI)
The product of Young's modulus E and the second moment of area I. Plain meaning: the stiffness of the beam in bending. Unit: N mm2 or kN m2.
Bending moment (M)
The turning effect on a section of the beam, as explained in the shear force and bending moment topic. Unit: N mm or kN m.
Double integration method
A method that integrates the bending moment twice to find the slope and the deflection.
Constant of integration
An unknown number that appears when you integrate, and that must be found from the boundary conditions. Plain meaning: a number that depends on how the beam is held.
Boundary condition
A known fact at a support or an end, such as zero deflection at a pin. Plain meaning: what we know about the beam at its ends.
Macaulay bracket (x - a)
A special bracket that equals (x minus a) when x is greater than a, and zero otherwise. It switches a load on or off as x passes a.
Moment-area method
A method that uses the area of the M/EI diagram to find slopes and deflections.
Tangent
The straight line that just touches the beam at a point, along the slope there.
Maximum deflection
The largest deflection anywhere along the beam, which occurs where the slope is zero.
Simply supported beam
A beam resting on a pin at one end and a roller at the other.
Cantilever beam
A beam fixed at one end and free at the other.
Uniformly distributed load (w)
A load per unit length spread evenly along the beam, in kN/m.

Why beams deflect and the curvature idea

Bending makes curvature

In the bending stress topic, we saw that a beam under a bending moment has stress that grows with distance from the neutral axis. The same moment also makes the beam curve. The link between the moment and the curvature is one of the most important results in mechanics. For a beam with flexural rigidity EI, the curvature at a point is:

1/R = M / (E I)

Here R is the radius of curvature, M is the bending moment, E is Young's modulus and I is the second moment of area. The radius R is the radius of the circle that best fits the bend at that point. A large bending moment gives a small radius, which means a sharp bend. A large EI gives a large radius, which means a gentle bend.

For the small deflections that engineers use, the slope is small, and we can write the curvature in terms of the deflection y, measured along the beam from the original straight line. This gives the working equation:

EI (d2y/dx2) = M

Here d2y/dx2 means "the rate of change of the slope", and x is the distance along the beam. The symbol d means a small change, and d2 means a change of a change. You do not need to know the derivation for GATE, but you must know what each symbol means and how to use the equation.

Key ideaThe bending moment equals EI times the second derivative of the deflection. So if you know the moment along the beam, you can find the deflection by integrating twice.

Sign convention

This page uses the convention where the deflection y is positive when it is upward and negative when it is downward, and the moment is positive when it sags. With this convention, the equation EI y'' = M has the correct sign for a simply supported beam with a downward load. Use the same convention in every step of one problem, and read the sign of the final answer as a direction.

If the flexural rigidity EI doubles and the moment stays the same, what happens to the curvature?

It halves, because curvature is M/(EI). A stiffer beam bends less.

What does the equation EI y'' = M say about a point where the moment is zero?

The second derivative of the deflection is zero there. The curvature is zero, so the beam is locally straight, although it may still be sloped.

The double integration method and its constants

Integrate twice

Integration is the reverse of differentiation. If the slope is the rate of change of deflection, then deflection is the sum of the slopes along the beam. Starting from EI y'' = M, we integrate once to get the slope, and again to get the deflection. Each integration adds a constant, which we must find from the supports.

EI y' = integral of M dx + C1
EI y = integral of (integral of M dx) dx + C1 x + C2

Here y' is the slope, C1 and C2 are the constants of integration, and the integrals are taken with respect to x. The constant C1 is the starting slope, and C2 is the starting deflection, when x = 0.

Worked example: cantilever with a tip load

A cantilever of length L has a point load P at its free end. Measure x from the fixed end, A, toward the free end, B. At a section x from A, the moment is M = -P (L - x). The minus sign shows that it hogs (the top is in tension). We solve EI y'' = -P(L - x).

  • Step 1: integrate once. EI y' = -P (L x - x2/2) + C1.
  • Step 2: integrate again. EI y = -P (L x2/2 - x3/6) + C1 x + C2.
  • Step 3: use the boundary conditions (next section). At the fixed end A, x = 0, the deflection is zero and the slope is zero. This gives C2 = 0 and C1 = 0.
  • Step 4: substitute x = L for the free end. EI y(L) = -P (L3/2 - L3/6) = -P L3/3.
  • Step 5: the deflection at the free end is delta = P L3/(3 EI), downward.

TipWrite the moment equation with its sign, then integrate step by step, and write each constant explicitly. Most errors come from losing a constant or a minus sign.

The small numbers check

Take P = 2 kN, L = 3 m and EI = 1500 kN m2. The tip deflection is P L3/(3EI) = 2 x 27/(3 x 1500) = 54/4500 = 0.012 m, which is 12 mm. The slope at the tip is P L2/(2EI) = 2 x 9/(2 x 1500) = 0.006 rad. The Python check in the next section repeats these numbers.

In the cantilever example, why is C1 zero?

Because the slope at the fixed end is zero, and substituting x = 0 into EI y' = -P(Lx - x2/2) + C1 gives EI y'(0) = C1, which must be zero.

Why does the fixed end of a cantilever have zero slope?

The beam is built in, so it cannot rotate at the wall. The tangent there is horizontal.

Boundary conditions that fix the constants

What the supports tell us

Each support gives a known fact, and these facts are the boundary conditions. The two constants of integration need two conditions. Here are the usual ones:

  • A pin or roller support has zero deflection, y = 0, because the beam cannot move down at the support.
  • A fixed (built-in) end has zero deflection and zero slope, y = 0 and y' = 0, because the beam cannot move or rotate there.
  • A free end has no condition from the support. Its deflection is unknown, and it is found from the solution.

For a simply supported beam, the two conditions are zero deflection at both ends. For a cantilever, the two conditions are zero deflection and zero slope at the fixed end. This is why the cantilever had two constants that both became zero.

TrapDo not set the slope to zero at a simple support. A simply supported beam has a slope at its ends, and the slope is what we want to find there.

What are the two boundary conditions for a simply supported beam of span L?

y = 0 at x = 0 and y = 0 at x = L. Both ends have zero deflection, and the slope is not known there.

A cantilever is built in at x = 0. Which two conditions apply there?

y = 0 and y' = 0 at x = 0. The deflection and the slope are both zero at the built-in end.

Standard deflection formulas for common beams

Why standard formulas are useful

The same few beam cases appear again and again. Engineers memorise the answers for these cases, so they do not have to integrate every time. Each formula has the same structure: a load term, a length term and a stiffness term. The power of the length tells you how sensitive the deflection is to length. For a cantilever tip load, the deflection grows with L cubed, so doubling the length makes the deflection eight times as large.

Using a standard formula

Take a simply supported beam of span L = 4 m with a central point load of P = 10 kN, and let EI = 2000 kN m2. The deflection at midspan is P L3/(48EI) = 10 x 64/(48 x 2000) = 640/96000 = 0.006667 m, which is 6.67 mm. The units work out: kN times m3, divided by kN m2 times m0, gives m. GATE loves thisGATE often gives a beam and asks how the deflection changes when the length or the stiffness changes. Remember the powers: deflection is proportional to L cubed for a point load on a cantilever, to L to the fourth for a UDL, and to 1/EI in every case.

If a simply supported beam with a central load has its span doubled, by what factor does the midspan deflection change?

By a factor of 8, because the deflection is proportional to L cubed.

For the same span, which loading gives more midspan deflection in a simply supported beam: a central point load P or a UDL w = P/L?

Put w = P/L, so the total load is the same in both cases. The UDL deflection is 5 w L4/(384EI) = 5 P L3/(384EI). The point load deflection is P L3/(48EI). Since 5/384 is about 0.013 and 1/48 is about 0.021, the central point load gives more deflection.

Macaulay's method for loads that change along the beam

The problem with a single equation

When a beam has a point load at one place and a support at another, the moment is different on each side of the load. The simple double integration then needs a separate equation for each part, with its own constants, and the matching at the join is tedious. Macaulay's method solves this with one equation for the whole beam, using the bracket that switches terms on and off.

The Macaulay bracket

<x - a> = (x - a) when x is greater than a, and 0 when x is less than or equal to a

The bracket is like a light switch that turns on at x = a. Before the switch, the term is zero. After it, the term is (x - a). The power after the bracket keeps its place when we integrate: integrating <x - a>n gives <x - a>n+1/(n + 1), but only for the terms that are switched on.

Worked example: simply supported beam with a point load

A beam of span L = 6 m is simply supported at A (x = 0) and B (x = 6 m). A point load of 12 kN acts at x = 2 m. We found earlier that RA = 8 kN. The moment for the whole beam is:

M(x) = 8x - 12 <x - 2>

Here the second term switches on only past the load at x = 2 m. Let EI be constant. Integrate twice, with the bracket kept in the same form:

  • Step 1: EI y'' = 8x - 12 <x - 2>.
  • Step 2: EI y' = 4x2 - 6 <x - 2>2 + C1.
  • Step 3: EI y = (4/3) x3 - 2 <x - 2>3 + C1 x + C2.
  • Step 4: at x = 0, y = 0, so C2 = 0 (the bracket is zero there).
  • Step 5: at x = 6, y = 0, so 0 = (4/3)(216) - 2(4)3 + 6 C1 = 288 - 128 + 6 C1. Hence C1 = -160/6 = -80/3.

Now the whole beam is described by one equation for EI y. The Python check below evaluates this equation, and it locates the point of maximum deflection, which is the next section's topic.

Key ideaMacaulay's method uses one set of constants for the whole beam, because each load term is switched on only where it acts.

What is the value of <x - 2> when x = 1 m?

Zero, because x is less than 2. The term is switched off.

Why did C2 come out as zero?

Because the deflection at x = 0, the left support, is zero, and every term in EI y is zero at x = 0 when C2 is zero.

The moment-area method

Two theorems

The moment-area method uses the area under the M/EI diagram. It gives slopes and deflections without integrating the full equation. It rests on two theorems, which we state in plain words:

  1. First theorem. The change in slope between two points on a beam equals the area of the M/EI diagram between those two points.
  2. Second theorem. The deviation of a point B from the tangent drawn at another point A equals the first moment of the M/EI area between A and B, taken about B. The first moment is the area times the distance of its centroid from B.
Change in slope = area of M/EI between the points
Deviation = (area of M/EI) x (distance from B to the centroid of that area)

The first theorem tells us how the slope changes. The second theorem tells us how far the beam sits from the tangent, and for a cantilever whose fixed end has zero slope, the tangent is horizontal, so the deviation is the deflection.

Worked example: cantilever tip load by moment-area

Take the cantilever with a tip load P at the free end B, and the fixed end at A. The M/EI diagram is a triangle. Its height at the fixed end is PL/(EI), and it is zero at the free end. The triangle's base is L.

  • Step 1: area of the triangle = (1/2) x base x height = (1/2) x L x PL/(EI) = P L2/(2EI).
  • Step 2: the centroid of a triangle is one-third of the base from the wide end. The zero-moment end is the free end B, where the apex is, so the centroid is two-thirds of the length from B, which is 2L/3 from B.
  • Step 3: deviation of B from the tangent at A = area x distance from B to the centroid = (P L2/(2EI)) x (2L/3) = P L3/(3EI).
  • Step 4: since the tangent at the fixed end is horizontal, this deviation is the tip deflection. It matches the double integration result.
P = 3.0
L = 2.0
EI = 1000.0
area = 0.5 * L * (P * L / EI)
centroid_from_B = 2 * L / 3
deviation = area * centroid_from_B
print("area of M/EI =", round(area, 4))
print("deviation of B =", round(deviation * 1000, 2), "mm")
area of M/EI = 0.006
deviation of B = 8.0 mm

TipIn moment-area questions, sketch the M/EI diagram first. Mark the areas and the centroids, then apply the two theorems in order.

TrapThe centroid distance is measured from the point whose deviation you want, not from the support. This is the most common error in moment-area problems.

What does the first moment-area theorem give for the slope change between two points?

The area of the M/EI diagram between those points.

Why is the centroid of a triangle at one-third of its base from the wide end?

The centroid of a triangle lies one-third of the way from its base toward the opposite corner. For the moment-area triangle, that is one-third of the length from the wide end, so it is two-thirds of the length from the apex.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original, written in the style of GATE. They are not real past questions. Try each one before you read the solution.

Doubling the stiffness

The tip deflection of a cantilever with a point load at its free end is 12 mm. If the flexural rigidity EI is doubled and the load stays the same, what is the new tip deflection in mm? Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The new deflection in mm.
  2. Which idea applies. delta = PL3/(3EI), which is inversely proportional to EI.
  3. Work it out. Doubling EI halves the deflection, so 12/2 = 6 mm.
  4. Check. The formula has EI in the denominator once, so the factor is exactly 1/2.

Answer: 6 mm.

Slope at the tip

A cantilever of length 3 m carries a point load of 2 kN at its free end. Take EI = 1500 kN m2. Find the slope at the free end in radians. Give a single number, to four decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The tip slope in radians.
  2. Which idea applies. theta = PL2/(2EI).
  3. Work it out. theta = 2 x 9/(2 x 1500) = 18/3000 = 0.0060 rad.
  4. Check. The deflection of 12 mm over a length of 3 m gives an average slope of about 0.004, and the tip slope is larger than this average, as it should be for a beam that bends more at the free end.

Answer: 0.0060 rad.

Midspan deflection (NAT)

A simply supported beam of span 4 m carries a point load of 10 kN at midspan. Take EI = 2000 kN m2. Find the midspan deflection in mm, to two decimal places. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The midspan deflection in mm.
  2. Which idea applies. delta = PL3/(48EI).
  3. Work it out. delta = 10 x 43/(48 x 2000) = 640/96000 = 0.006667 m = 6.67 mm. The Python check in the chapter gives the same value.
  4. Check. The cantilever tip deflection for the same load and span would be much larger, about 16 times the midspan value of a simply supported beam with the same load and span, because (1/3)/(1/48) = 16. This is consistent with the cantilever being more flexible.

Answer: 6.67 mm.

Which statements are correct? (MSQ)

Which of the following statements about beam deflection are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) Double integration of EI y'' = M needs two constants of integration
(B) A built-in end has zero deflection and zero slope
(C) The midspan deflection of a simply supported beam under a full UDL is 5wL4/(384EI)
(D) For the same total load W and span L, a UDL gives a larger midspan deflection than a central point load W

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Every correct statement.
  2. Which idea applies. The integration constants, the boundary conditions and the standard formulas.
  3. Work it out. (A) is correct, since two integrations give two constants. (B) is correct, since the fixed end cannot move or rotate. (C) is correct, it is the standard formula. (D) is wrong: with total load W, the UDL gives 5WL3/(384EI), and the central point load gives WL3/(48EI). Since 5/384 is smaller than 1/48, the central point load gives more deflection.
  4. Check. Compare the two coefficients: 5/384 = 0.013 and 1/48 = 0.021. The point load is larger.

Answer: (A), (B) and (C).

Tip deflection under a uniform load (NAT)

A cantilever of length 2 m carries a uniformly distributed load of 2 kN/m over its full length. Take EI = 1000 kN m2. Find the tip deflection in mm. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The deflection at the free end in mm.
  2. Which idea applies. delta = wL4/(8EI).
  3. Work it out. delta = 2 x 24/(8 x 1000) = 32/8000 = 0.004 m = 4 mm.
  4. Check. The total load is wL = 4 kN, and its tip-load equivalent would give PL3/(3EI) = 4 x 8/3000 = 0.0107 m. The uniform load gives less, because most of it acts close to the wall, which is correct.

Location of the maximum deflection (NAT)

A simply supported beam of span 6 m carries a point load of 12 kN at x = 2 m from the left support A. Use EI as a constant. Using Macaulay's method, the deflection function satisfies EI y = (4/3) x3 - 2 <x - 2>3 - (80/3) x, with the slope EI y' = 4x2 - 6 <x - 2>2 - 80/3. Find the distance x, from A, where the maximum deflection occurs, to two decimal places. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The x at which the deflection is largest.
  2. Which idea applies. The maximum deflection is where the slope is zero. Set EI y' = 0 for x greater than 2.
  3. Work it out. For x greater than 2, 4x2 - 6(x - 2)2 - 80/3 = 0. This simplifies to 2x2 - 24x + 152/3 = 0, whose root between 2 and 6 is x = 2.73 m (to two decimal places). The Python check below finds the same root by bisection.
  4. Check. At x = 2.73, the slope is nearly zero, and the deflection there is the largest negative value, about -46.45/EI kN m3. Its magnitude is largest there, as expected for a beam sagging under a central-ish load.
def ei_y(x):
    tail = max(x - 2.0, 0.0)
    return (4.0 / 3.0) * x ** 3 - 2.0 * tail ** 3 - (80.0 / 3.0) * x
def ei_slope(x):
    tail = max(x - 2.0, 0.0)
    return 4.0 * x ** 2 - 6.0 * tail ** 2 - 80.0 / 3.0
lo, hi = 2.0, 6.0
for _ in range(60):
    mid = (lo + hi) / 2
    if ei_slope(lo) * ei_slope(mid) <= 0:
        hi = mid
    else:
        lo = mid
x_max = (lo + hi) / 2
print("location of max deflection:", round(x_max, 2), "m")
print("EI y there:", round(ei_y(x_max), 2), "kN m^3")
print("EI y at the far support:", round(ei_y(6.0), 6))
location of max deflection: 2.73 m
EI y there: -46.45 kN m^3
EI y at the far support: 0.0

Answer: 2.73 m.

Tip deflection by the moment-area method (NAT)

A cantilever of length 2 m carries a point load of 3 kN at its free end. Take EI = 1000 kN m2. Using the moment-area method, find the tip deflection in mm, to one decimal place. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The tip deflection in mm, to one decimal place.
  2. Which idea applies. The M/EI diagram is a triangle. Its area gives the area term, and the deviation of the tip from the fixed-end tangent is that area times the distance from the tip to its centroid.
  3. Work it out. The moment at the wall is P L = 6 kN m, so the M/EI height there is 6/1000 = 0.006. The area is (1/2) x 2 x 0.006 = 0.006. The centroid is 2L/3 = 4/3 m from the tip. The deviation is 0.006 x 4/3 = 0.008 m, which is 8.0 mm. The Python check below gives the same value.
  4. Check. The double integration formula PL3/(3EI) = 3 x 8/3000 = 0.008 m agrees.
P = 3.0
L = 2.0
EI = 1000.0
area = 0.5 * L * (P * L / EI)
deviation = area * (2 * L / 3)
print("tip deflection =", round(deviation * 1000, 1), "mm")
tip deflection = 8.0 mm

Answer: 8.0 mm.

Exam-level 1: Cantilever tip deflection (NAT, 2 marks)

A cantilever of length 2 m, EI = 4 × 106 N·m², carries 6 kN at the tip. Find the tip deflection in mm.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. δ = PL³/(3EI).
  2. Which idea applies. Standard cantilever result.
  3. Work it out. 6000 · 8/(1.2e7) = 0.004 m = 4 mm.
  4. Check. The slope at the tip is PL²/(2EI) = 0.003 rad.

Exam-level 2: Simply supported with UDL (NAT, 2 marks)

A simply supported beam of span 4 m, EI = 2 × 107 N·m², carries 10 kN/m. Find the central deflection in mm, correct to 3 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. δ = 5wL⁴/(384EI).
  2. Which idea applies. Standard result.
  3. Work it out. 5 · 10000 · 256/(384 · 2e7) = 1.667 mm.
  4. Check. The code computes it.

Exam-level 3: Ratio of deflections (NAT, 2 marks)

A simply supported beam carries a central point load W. If the span is doubled and W is unchanged, by what factor does the central deflection grow?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Scaling of WL³/(48EI).
  2. Which idea applies. Deflection ∝ L³.
  3. Work it out. 2³ = 8.
  4. Check. With a UDL of the same intensity, it would be 2⁴ = 16.

Answer: 8.

Remember these

  1. Curvature is M/(EI), so a stiffer beam bends less.
  2. Integrate EI y'' = M twice, and find each constant from a support.
  3. A pin or roller has zero deflection. A built-in end has zero deflection and zero slope.
  4. Use the standard formulas for common cases, and check the units by hand.
  5. Macaulay's bracket switches a load on at its position, so one equation covers the whole beam.
  6. In moment-area, measure the centroid distance from the point whose deviation you want.

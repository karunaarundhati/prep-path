Resonance & critical speeds of shafts

A rotating shaft carrying a disc can bend strongly when it spins at a particular speed, called its critical speed. This happens when the speed matches the shaft's natural frequency, which is resonance. This page explains why, how to estimate the critical speed for a shaft with one or several discs, and how to use Dunkerley's and Rayleigh's methods.

The idea in plain words

Hold a long plastic ruler at one end and wave the other end up and down. At a slow rate, the ruler just follows your hand. At one particular rate, the tip starts to swing with a large amplitude, even though your hand moves only a little. You have found the ruler's natural frequency. This is resonance.

A rotating shaft behaves in the same way. A shaft carrying a heavy disc, such as a turbine rotor, a pump impeller or a flywheel, can bend sideways as it spins. If the spin speed is close to the shaft's natural bending frequency, the bend becomes large. The speed at which this happens is the critical speed. Running a machine near its critical speed can damage bearings, seals and the shaft itself, so designers must know where it lies.

In GATE, this topic appears in the Robotics and Automation (RA) paper through shaft and rotor questions, and in the machine design material. The methods on this page are short and mechanical, and they give quick estimates that GATE questions often ask for.

Key terms

Each term has a plain meaning first, then a precise definition.

Natural frequency (ωn)
The frequency at which a system vibrates freely, in rad/s. Plain meaning: the frequency the system likes to swing at.
Resonance
The condition in which an excitation frequency equals a natural frequency, so the response becomes large.
Excitation frequency
The frequency of the force acting on the system. For a rotor, it equals the spin speed.
Critical speed (Nc)
The rotational speed at which the shaft's lateral natural frequency equals the spin speed, so the shaft bends strongly.
Whirling
The sideways motion of a shaft's centre line as it rotates, like a skipping rope turning around.
Disc or rotor mass (m)
The mass attached to the shaft, in kg. A disc is a heavy wheel fixed to the shaft.
Shaft stiffness (k)
The force needed to produce unit sideways deflection at the disc, in N/m.
Second moment of area (I)
A measure of the shape of a cross section that controls bending. For a solid circular shaft, I = πd⁴/64.
Modulus of elasticity (E)
The stiffness of the material, in N/m² (Pa). Steel is about 200 GPa.
Eccentricity (e)
The distance between the disc's centre of mass and the geometric centre of the shaft.
Static deflection (δ)
The sideways displacement of the shaft at a point due to a steady load, in m.
Dunkerley's method
An approximate method that adds the reciprocal squares of the critical speeds of each mass acting alone.
Rayleigh's method
An energy method that estimates the natural frequency from the static deflections of the masses.

TrapThe critical speed is a speed in rev/s, rpm or rad/s, but the natural frequency formula gives rad/s. Convert before you compare, or the answer will be off by a factor of about 9.55.

Resonance: why the amplitude grows

The push at the right moment

Picture pushing a swing. Each push adds a little energy. If you push at the wrong moment, you cancel some of the energy. If you push at the natural rhythm of the swing, every push adds to the last, and the swing rises higher. A forcing that matches the natural frequency is exactly this situation.

For a system with a forcing at frequency ω, the amplitude (without damping) is

X = (F0/k) / (1 - r²), with r = ω/ωn

Here F0/k is the static deflection, and r is the frequency ratio. When r is close to 1, the denominator is close to zero and the amplitude is very large. At r = 1 exactly, the undamped amplitude is infinite in theory. Real systems have some damping, so the amplitude is large but finite.

Key ideaResonance is the condition r = 1, where the forcing frequency equals the natural frequency. It gives the largest response, and it is the basis of the critical speed.

Worked example: a disc at a known speed

A disc on a shaft gives a static deflection of 0.5 mm under its weight. The shaft's natural frequency is 100 rad/s. The shaft spins at 80 rad/s. Find the undamped amplitude ratio.

Step 1. Frequency ratio: r = 80/100 = 0.8, so r² = 0.64.

Step 2. Amplitude ratio: 1/(1 - 0.64) = 1/0.36 = 2.78.

Step 3. Amplitude: 2.78 × 0.5 mm = 1.39 mm.

Step 4. Check: r is below 1, so the amplitude is larger than the static deflection but not infinite. This is the warning region in which designers avoid running.

Self-check: at spin speed 0.5 times the natural frequency, is the amplitude larger or smaller than the static deflection?

Larger. The ratio is 1/(1 - 0.25) = 1.33, so the amplitude is 1.33 times the static deflection.

Self-check: why do real shafts not show infinite amplitude at critical speed?

Damping from bearings, air and material losses limits the amplitude. The shaft then shows a finite but still large peak.

Whirling of a shaft and its critical speed

What whirling looks like

Put a heavy disc in the middle of a shaft, and spin the shaft slowly. The disc sits low and the shaft is straight. Now raise the speed. At one speed the shaft begins to bend and the disc swings outwards in a circle around the bearing line. This sideways circular motion is called whirling. The whirl speed is the speed at which the centre of the shaft moves around the bearing line, and it can be the same as the spin speed.

  side view of a shaft with a disc at its centre

   bearing A                          bearing B
      o--------------[ disc ]------------o
      |                                  |

   at speed below critical:  the shaft bends a little
   at critical speed:        the bend becomes very large
   above critical speed:     the disc centre moves towards
                             the bearing line (self-centring)

The critical speed

The shaft acts like a spring of stiffness k, carrying the mass m of the disc. Its natural frequency is

ωc = √(k/m)

Here ωc is the critical speed in rad/s. The critical speed in rpm is Nc = 60ωc/(2π) = 9.549 ωc. The stiffness k depends on the shaft's size, length, material and how it is supported.

Key ideaThe critical speed of a shaft is its lateral natural frequency. It is found exactly like any spring-mass natural frequency, with k set by the shaft's bending stiffness.

Worked example: a disc on a simple shaft

A disc of mass 10 kg is at the centre of a simply supported steel shaft of length 0.5 m and diameter 20 mm. Find the critical speed in rpm. Use E = 200 GPa.

Step 1. Second moment of area: I = πd⁴/64 = π(0.02)⁴/64 = 7.854 × 10⁻⁹ m⁴.

Step 2. Shaft stiffness at the centre of a simply supported beam: k = 48EI/L³ = 48 × 200 × 10⁹ × 7.854 × 10⁻⁹ / 0.5³ = 603 200 N/m, approximately.

Step 3. Critical speed: ωc = √(603 200/10) = √60 320 = 245.6 rad/s.

Step 4. In rpm: Nc = 9.549 × 245.6 = 2345 rpm.

Step 5. Check: a steel shaft of this size with a 10 kg disc should have a critical speed of a few thousand rpm, which fits.

import math

d, L, E, m = 0.020, 0.5, 200e9, 10.0
I = math.pi * d ** 4 / 64
k = 48 * E * I / L ** 3
wc = math.sqrt(k / m)
Nc = 60 * wc / (2 * math.pi)
print(f"I = {I:.4e} m^4")
print(f"k = {k:.0f} N/m")
print(f"critical speed = {wc:.1f} rad/s")
print(f"critical speed = {Nc:.0f} rpm")
I = 7.8540e-09 m^4
k = 603186 N/m
critical speed = 245.6 rad/s
critical speed = 2345 rpm

TrapThe stiffness formula k = 48EI/L³ is for a simply supported shaft with the disc at midspan. For an overhung disc, or a different support, the constant changes. Check the support before you use the formula.

Self-check: the shaft diameter is doubled. How does k change?

I is proportional to d⁴, so doubling d makes I sixteen times bigger, and k is sixteen times bigger. The critical speed grows by √16 = 4 for the same disc mass.

Self-check: the disc mass is increased by four times. What happens to the critical speed?

It halves, because ωc = √(k/m) and the mass is under the square root.

Dunkerley's method for several masses

The idea

A real shaft often carries several discs. The exact critical speed then needs a matrix calculation, which is too long for an exam. Dunkerley's method gives a simple estimate. First find the critical speed of the shaft with only one disc at a time, ignoring the others. Call these ω1, ω2 and so on. Then combine them by adding reciprocal squares:

1/ωc² = 1/ω1² + 1/ω2² + 1/ω3² + ...

Here ωc is the estimated first critical speed. Each ωi is the critical speed when only disc i is on the shaft. The shaft's own mass is neglected.

A convenient form using static deflections

If δi is the static deflection of the shaft at disc i due to the weight of disc i alone, then ωi² = g/δi. Substituting gives a very clean formula:

1/ωc² = (δ1 + δ2 + δ3 + ...)/g

This form is the one to use in most GATE questions that give deflections. Remember that all deflections must be in metres, and g = 9.81 m/s².

Key ideaWith static deflections, Dunkerley's method simply adds the deflections, then takes the square root of g divided by that sum.

Worked example

A shaft carries two discs. With only the first disc on the shaft, its static deflection under its own weight is 0.5 mm. With only the second disc, its static deflection is 0.3 mm. Find the first critical speed in rad/s and in rpm.

Step 1. Sum of deflections: δ1 + δ2 = 0.5 + 0.3 = 0.8 mm = 0.0008 m.

Step 2. Reciprocal square: 1/ωc² = 0.0008/9.81, so ωc² = 9.81/0.0008 = 12262.5.

Step 3. Critical speed: ωc = √12262.5 = 110.74 rad/s.

Step 4. In rpm: 9.549 × 110.74 = 1057 rpm.

Step 5. Check: the critical speed is lower than either single-disc value. For the disc with 0.5 mm deflection alone, ω = √(9.81/0.0005) = 140.1 rad/s, and the combined value 110.7 is below it, as Dunkerley's method predicts.

import math

g = 9.81
d1, d2 = 0.0005, 0.0003       # static deflections in m
wc = math.sqrt(g / (d1 + d2))
print(f"critical speed = {wc:.2f} rad/s")
print(f"critical speed = {wc * 60 / (2 * math.pi):.0f} rpm")
print(f"disc 1 alone = {math.sqrt(g / d1):.1f} rad/s")
critical speed = 110.74 rad/s
critical speed = 1057 rpm
disc 1 alone = 140.1 rad/s

TrapDunkerley's method adds reciprocal squares of speeds, which is the same as adding the deflections. Do not add the speeds themselves. Adding speeds gives a number that is too high.

Self-check: what happens to the estimate if a third disc is added with a deflection of 0.2 mm?

The sum becomes 1.0 mm, so ωc = √(9.81/0.001) = 99.0 rad/s. Adding masses always lowers the critical speed.

Self-check: Dunkerley's value is compared with the true first critical speed. Is it usually above or below?

Usually below. It ignores the coupling between masses, so it gives a conservative, lower estimate of the first critical speed.

Rayleigh's method using static deflections

The idea

Rayleigh's method takes the energy of the system at its extreme positions. The maximum kinetic energy in vibration equals the maximum potential energy stored in the shaft. For a set of discs with masses mi and deflections yi under the actual loads (all discs present), this gives

ωc² = g Σ(mi yi) / Σ(mi yi²)

Read each symbol. mi is the mass of disc i, yi is the static deflection at disc i with all discs present, and Σ means the sum over all discs. The formula uses the deflection shape of the static load, which is a good guess for the vibration shape.

Key ideaRayleigh's method uses the deflections under the real weights, with all the discs present. Dunkerley's method uses the deflections of each disc alone.

Worked example

A shaft carries two discs of masses 10 kg and 5 kg. With both discs present, the static deflections are 0.4 mm at the 10 kg disc and 0.3 mm at the 5 kg disc. Find the critical speed in rad/s.

Step 1. Numerator: Σ(m y) = 10 × 0.0004 + 5 × 0.0003 = 0.004 + 0.0015 = 0.0055 kg m.

Step 2. Denominator: Σ(m y²) = 10 × (0.0004)² + 5 × (0.0003)² = 1.6 × 10⁻⁶ + 4.5 × 10⁻⁷ = 2.05 × 10⁻⁶ kg m².

Step 3. ωc² = 9.81 × 0.0055/2.05 × 10⁻⁶ = 26 320, so ωc = 162.2 rad/s.

Step 4. Check: the answer is in the right range. The discs have deflections of about a third of a millimetre, so the shaft is quite stiff.

import math

g = 9.81
masses = [10.0, 5.0]
y = [0.0004, 0.0003]          # deflections with all discs present, m

num = g * sum(m * yi for m, yi in zip(masses, y))
den = sum(m * yi ** 2 for m, yi in zip(masses, y))
wc = math.sqrt(num / den)
print(f"omega_c^2 = {num / den:.0f} rad^2/s^2")
print(f"critical speed = {wc:.1f} rad/s")
omega_c^2 = 26320 rad^2/s^2
critical speed = 162.2 rad/s

TrapRayleigh's method needs the deflections with all the masses present. Using single-disc deflections, which belong to Dunkerley's method, gives a wrong answer.

Self-check: what does Rayleigh's method approximate if the deflection shape is exact?

It gives the exact first natural frequency, because the energy balance is then exact. An assumed shape gives an estimate, which is usually a little higher than the true value.

Self-check: which of the two methods needs the full static load on the shaft?

Rayleigh's method, because it uses the deflections under the combined weights.

Whirl with eccentricity: speeds below and above critical

Why a disc is never perfectly centred

A real disc has a small offset, the eccentricity e, between its centre of mass and the centre of the shaft. As the shaft spins, this offset creates a centrifugal force that pushes the disc sideways. This is the same mechanism as the unbalance on the balancing page. At the critical speed the force and the stiffness combine to give a very large deflection.

The whirl formula

If r is the distance of the shaft centre from the bearing line, the whirl relation is

r = e ω² / (ωc² - ω²)

Here ω is the spin speed, ωc is the critical speed and e is the eccentricity. The formula shows three regimes:

  • Below critical (ω less than ωc): r is positive. The disc centre lies outside the bearing line, on the same side as the mass.
  • At critical (ω equal to ωc): r is infinite in theory. Damping limits it in practice.
  • Above critical (ω greater than ωc): r is negative. The disc centre moves inside the bearing line, so the heavy side points towards the centre. This is the self-centring effect that makes fast rotors run smoothly.

Key ideaRunning above the critical speed is safe for a rotor, because the disc settles towards its centre of rotation. Running at the critical speed is dangerous.

Worked example

A rotor has eccentricity e = 0.2 mm and critical speed 110 rad/s. It runs at 80 rad/s. Find the whirl radius r.

Step 1. Ratio: x = ω²/ωc² = (80/110)² = 0.5289.

Step 2. Using r = e x/(1 - x) = 0.2 × 0.5289/(1 - 0.5289) = 0.2 × 1.1224 = 0.2246 mm.

Step 3. Check: running below critical, the value is positive and larger than e. Since 0.2246 is larger than 0.2, the amplification is consistent.

import math

e, wc, w = 0.2, 110.0, 80.0
x = (w / wc) ** 2
r = e * x / (1 - x)
print(f"x = {x:.4f}")
print(f"r = {r:.4f} mm")
x = 0.5289
r = 0.2246 mm
Self-check: a rotor runs at 1.5 times its critical speed. What is the sign of r, and what does it mean?

r is negative. With x = 2.25, r = e × 2.25/(1 - 2.25) = -1.8e, so the disc centre is on the opposite side of the bearing line from its mass. The rotor is self-centring.

Self-check: why is the critical speed a speed to avoid rather than a speed to run at?

Because the deflection there is largest, which causes high stress, bearing loads and possible rubbing. Machines are designed to run well away from the critical speed.

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original GATE-style problems for practice, not real past paper questions. Work each one on paper before reading the answer.

Critical speed from stiffness

A disc of mass 4 kg sits on a shaft whose lateral stiffness at the disc is 40000 N/m. Find the critical speed in rpm (numerical answer).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The critical speed in rpm.
  2. Which idea applies. ωc = √(k/m), then Nc = 9.549 ωc.
  3. Work it out. ωc = √(40000/4) = √10000 = 100 rad/s. Nc = 9.549 × 100 = 955 rpm.
  4. Check. A critical speed near 1000 rpm for a small disc on a fairly stiff shaft is reasonable.
import math

k, m = 40000.0, 4.0
wc = math.sqrt(k / m)
print(f"critical speed = {60 * wc / (2 * math.pi):.0f} rpm")
critical speed = 955 rpm

Answer: 955 rpm.

Critical speed in rad/s

A rotor of mass 5 kg has a shaft stiffness of 20 kN/m at the rotor. Find the critical speed in rad/s (numerical answer, to one decimal place).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. ωc in rad/s.
  2. Which idea applies. Convert kN/m to N/m, then ωc = √(k/m).
  3. Work it out. k = 20000 N/m. ωc = √(20000/5) = √4000 = 63.2 rad/s.
  4. Check. √4000 is between 63 and 64, so 63.2 is correct.

Answer: 63.2 rad/s.

Which statements are true?

Consider these statements about resonance and critical speeds.

(A) Operating at the critical speed produces a large deflection of the shaft.
(B) Dunkerley's method usually gives a lower estimate of the first critical speed than the true value.
(C) Rayleigh's method uses the deflection shape of the static load, and it usually gives a value that is a little higher than the true value.
(D) Making the shaft less stiff always lowers the critical speed by a factor of 2.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true (multiple selection).
  2. Which idea applies. Resonance gives large deflection. Dunkerley ignores coupling and is conservative. Rayleigh uses an assumed shape. Critical speed scales with √(k/m).
  3. Work it out. (A) true. (B) true. (C) true. (D) false, because the critical speed scales with the square root of stiffness, so halving k gives a factor of about 0.71, not 0.5.
  4. Check. √0.5 = 0.707, which confirms that (D) is false.

Answer: (A), (B) and (C).

Dunkerley estimate for two discs

A shaft carries two discs. With only the first disc on the shaft, its static deflection is 0.5 mm. With only the second disc, its static deflection is 0.3 mm. Using Dunkerley's method, find the first critical speed in rpm (numerical answer, nearest rpm).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The estimated first critical speed in rpm.
  2. Which idea applies. 1/ωc² = (δ1 + δ2)/g.
  3. Work it out. δ sum = 0.0008 m. ωc = √(9.81/0.0008) = 110.74 rad/s. In rpm: 110.74 × 9.549 = 1057.
  4. Check. The combined value is below the single-disc value of 140.1 rad/s, as expected.

Answer: about 1057 rpm.

Critical speed of a longer steel shaft

A steel shaft of diameter 30 mm and length 0.4 m is simply supported, with a disc of mass 8 kg at midspan. Use E = 200 GPa. Find the critical speed in rad/s (numerical answer, nearest integer).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. ωc in rad/s.
  2. Which idea applies. I = πd⁴/64, k = 48EI/L³, then ωc = √(k/m).
  3. Work it out. I = π × (0.03)⁴/64 = 3.976 × 10⁻⁸ m⁴. k = 48 × 200 × 10⁹ × 3.976 × 10⁻⁸/0.064 = 5.964 × 10⁶ N/m. ωc = √(5.964 × 10⁶/8) = 863 rad/s.
  4. Check. A stiffer, shorter shaft than the worked example gives a much higher critical speed, which is consistent.
import math

d, L, E, m = 0.030, 0.4, 200e9, 8.0
I = math.pi * d ** 4 / 64
k = 48 * E * I / L ** 3
print(f"omega_c = {math.sqrt(k / m):.0f} rad/s")
omega_c = 863 rad/s

Answer: 863 rad/s.

Rayleigh estimate with two discs

A shaft carries two discs of masses 10 kg and 5 kg. With both discs present, the static deflections are 0.5 mm at the 10 kg disc and 0.4 mm at the 5 kg disc. Using Rayleigh's method, find the critical speed in rad/s (numerical answer, one decimal place).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. ωc in rad/s by Rayleigh's method.
  2. Which idea applies. ωc² = g Σ(m y)/Σ(m y²), with the deflections under all loads.
  3. Work it out. Σ(m y) = 10 × 0.0005 + 5 × 0.0004 = 0.007. Σ(m y²) = 10 × 2.5 × 10⁻⁷ + 5 × 1.6 × 10⁻⁷ = 3.3 × 10⁻⁶. ωc² = 9.81 × 0.007/3.3 × 10⁻⁶ = 20 809, so ωc = 144.3 rad/s.
  4. Check. The deflections are a little larger than in the worked example, so the critical speed should be a little lower than 162.2 rad/s. 144.3 is lower, as expected.
import math

g = 9.81
masses = [10.0, 5.0]
y = [0.0005, 0.0004]
num = g * sum(m * yi for m, yi in zip(masses, y))
den = sum(m * yi ** 2 for m, yi in zip(masses, y))
print(f"omega_c = {math.sqrt(num / den):.1f} rad/s")
omega_c = 144.3 rad/s

Answer: 144.3 rad/s.

Running above the critical speed

A rotor has eccentricity e = 0.5 mm and critical speed ωc. It runs at 1.5 times its critical speed. Find the magnitude of the whirl radius in millimetres, and state on which side of the bearing line the disc centre lies compared with the mass.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. |r| in mm and its side.
  2. Which idea applies. r = e x/(1 - x) with x = (ω/ωc)².
  3. Work it out. x = 1.5² = 2.25. r = 0.5 × 2.25/(1 - 2.25) = 0.5 × 2.25/(-1.25) = -0.9 mm. The magnitude is 0.9 mm.
  4. Check. Above critical the sign is negative, so the centre lies on the opposite side of the bearing line from the mass. This is the self-centring effect.
e = 0.5
x = 1.5 ** 2
r = e * x / (1 - x)
print(f"r = {r:.2f} mm")
r = -0.90 mm

Answer: 0.9 mm in magnitude, with the disc centre on the opposite side of the bearing line from its mass (r is negative).

Exam-level 1: Critical speed of a shaft (NAT, 2 marks)

A disc on a shaft causes a static deflection of 0.4 mm. Take g = 9.81. Find the critical speed in rpm, correct to 0 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Nc = (60/2π)√(g/δ).
  2. Which idea applies. Critical speed equals the natural frequency of transverse vibration.
  3. Work it out. √(24525) = 156.6 rad/s = 1495 rpm.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
import math
print("%.0f" % (math.sqrt(9.81 / 0.0004) * 60 / (2 * math.pi)))

1495

Answer: about 1495 rpm.

Exam-level 2: Whirling amplitude (NAT, 2 marks)

A disc has eccentricity e = 0.1 mm. The shaft runs at twice its critical speed with no damping. Find the whirl amplitude in mm.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. y = er²/|1 − r²|.
  2. Which idea applies. Above critical, the disc centre moves toward the axis.
  3. Work it out. r = 2: 0.1 · 4/3 = 0.1333 mm.
  4. Check. As r grows, y tends to e.

Answer: about 0.133 mm.

Exam-level 3: Dunkerley (NAT, 2 marks)

A shaft alone has critical speed 3000 rpm. With a disc only (shaft mass ignored), it is 2000 rpm. Estimate the combined critical speed by Dunkerley's formula, correct to 0 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. 1/N² = 1/N1² + 1/N2².
  2. Which idea applies. Dunkerley's method gives a lower bound.
  3. Work it out. 1/9e6 + 1/4e6 = 3.611e−7. N = 1664 rpm.

print("%.0f" % ((1 / 3000 ** 2 + 1 / 2000 ** 2) ** -0.5))

Remember these

  • Critical speed is the lateral natural frequency of the shaft.
  • Convert rad/s to rpm by multiplying by 9.549.
  • Dunkerley adds deflections of each disc alone, Rayleigh uses deflections with all discs present.
  • Adding mass lowers the critical speed.
  • Running above the critical speed is the normal way fast rotors are operated.
  • The formula k = 48EI/L³ applies only to the stated support and disc position.

Balancing of reciprocating & rotating masses

A spinning or moving mass in a machine pushes and pulls on its bearings and frame. Balancing arranges masses so that these forces and couples cancel. This page builds the ideas from a single spinning disc up to the reciprocating masses of an engine, with GATE-style numbers.

  • Single-plane balancing with a counterweight

The idea in plain words

Think of a washing machine spinning a load of wet clothes. The drum shakes hard and the whole machine walks across the floor. The clothes are not spread evenly, so the heavy side pulls the drum outwards each time it comes round. That outward pull is the trouble. In a machine, any mass that is not perfectly centred on its shaft creates a force that turns with the shaft. The bearings feel it, the frame vibrates, and noise and wear grow fast as the speed rises.

Balancing means arranging masses so that these forces cancel. We add a counterweight on the opposite side, or we place masses in two planes, until the net force and the net turning effect are zero. In an engine, pistons move up and down rather than round and round, and their forces must be balanced too. That is why balancing is a core topic in machine design and in the Robotics and Automation (RA) paper, where rotating joints and linkages need the same ideas.

The key tool is that forces are vectors. A force has a size and a direction. To cancel forces, you add vectors tip to tail, and the total must be zero. This page uses that idea again and again.

Key terms

Each term has a plain meaning first, then a precise definition.

Unbalanced mass
A mass m at distance r from the axis of rotation, whose centre is not on the axis. Plain meaning: a heavy spot on a spinning part.
Centrifugal force
The outward force on a mass moving in a circle, F = m r ω². Plain meaning: the outward pull you feel on a spinning object.
Angular speed (ω)
How fast the shaft turns, in radians per second. Convert from rpm with ω = 2πN/60.
Counterweight
A mass added on the opposite side of the shaft to cancel an unbalance.
Balance plane
A flat slice across a rotor where a balancing mass may be placed.
Static unbalance
The net force from the masses is not zero, so the centre of mass lies off the axis.
Couple unbalance
The net force is zero, but the masses sit in different planes, so they create a turning effect (a couple).
Dynamic balance
Both the net force and the net couple are zero.
Reciprocating mass
A mass that moves to and fro in a straight line, such as a piston.
Crank
A rotating arm of length r that changes to-and-fro motion into rotation, or the reverse.
Connecting rod
The link of length l between the piston and the crank.
Crank angle (θ)
The angle of the crank measured from top dead centre (TDC), the position where the piston is furthest from the shaft.
Slider-crank mechanism
The crank, connecting rod and piston together. It is the usual engine mechanism.
Primary force
The inertia force of a reciprocating mass that repeats once per revolution.
Secondary force
The smaller inertia force that repeats twice per revolution, caused by the finite length of the connecting rod.
Partial balancing
Balancing a fraction C of the reciprocating mass with a rotating counterweight.
Hammer blow
The maximum vertical force that appears when a rotating counterweight balances part of a reciprocating mass.

Rotating unbalance and single-plane balancing

Why a rotating mass creates a force

Tie a stone to a string and whirl it. You feel a pull outwards, and you must pull inwards to keep the stone moving in a circle. The outward force on a mass m at radius r spinning at angular speed ω is

F = m r ω²

Here m is the mass in kg, r is the radius in metres, ω is the angular speed in rad/s, and F is the force in newtons. The force grows with the square of the speed. Double the speed and the force becomes four times as big. Its direction turns with the shaft, so it shakes the bearings in a circular pattern.

The balance condition

To cancel the force, add a counterweight of mass mc at radius rc, pointing the opposite way. In one plane the condition is that the vector sum of all m r products is zero:

Σ (mi ri) = 0 (as vectors)

Here Σ means "add up all of", and the subscript i counts the masses. The counterweight must satisfy mc rc = size of the resultant, and it sits 180 degrees away from the resultant's direction.

Key ideaBalancing uses the product m r. Write the masses and radii as vectors, add them, and put a counterweight that cancels the total.

Worked example: several masses in one plane

A shaft carries two masses in the same plane. Mass A is 4 kg at radius 0.05 m, at 0 degrees. Mass B is 3 kg at radius 0.10 m, at 90 degrees. Find the counterweight at radius 0.10 m, and its angle.

Step 1. Write each m r as a vector (x, y). Mass A gives (4 × 0.05, 0) = (0.20, 0). Mass B gives (0, 3 × 0.10) = (0, 0.30).

Step 2. Add them. The resultant is (0.20, 0.30). Its size is √(0.20² + 0.30²) = √0.13 = 0.3606 kg m.

Step 3. Counterweight mass: mc = 0.3606 / 0.10 = 3.606 kg.

Step 4. Direction of the resultant: atan(0.30/0.20) = 56.31 degrees. The counterweight goes at 56.31 + 180 = 236.31 degrees.

Step 5. Check: the counterweight vector is 0.3606 at 236.31 degrees, which has components (-0.20, -0.30). Adding it to (0.20, 0.30) gives (0, 0). The balance is correct.

import math

masses = [(4.0, 0.05, 0.0), (3.0, 0.10, 90.0)]   # (mass kg, radius m, angle deg)

x = sum(m * r * math.cos(math.radians(a)) for m, r, a in masses)
y = sum(m * r * math.sin(math.radians(a)) for m, r, a in masses)

size = math.hypot(x, y)
angle = math.degrees(math.atan2(y, x))

r_c = 0.10
m_c = size / r_c
angle_c = (angle + 180) % 360

rx = x + m_c * r_c * math.cos(math.radians(angle_c))
ry = y + m_c * r_c * math.sin(math.radians(angle_c))

print(f"resultant m r = {size:.4f} kg m")
print(f"counterweight mass = {m_c:.3f} kg at {angle_c:.2f} deg")
print("balanced:", abs(rx) < 1e-9 and abs(ry) < 1e-9)
resultant m r = 0.3606 kg m
counterweight mass = 3.606 kg at 236.31 deg
balanced: True
Self-check: the speed doubles. By what factor does the unbalanced force change?

It becomes four times bigger, because F is proportional to ω².

Dynamic balancing in two planes

Why one plane is not always enough

Imagine a long roller with a heavy spot at one end. If you put a single counterweight in the middle, the force may cancel, but the two ends still swing like a dumbbell. The net force is zero, yet a turning effect (a couple) remains. This is a couple unbalance. To remove both the force and the couple, we need two balance planes, and we must solve both conditions together.

The conditions for a rotor with balance planes 1 and 2 are:

Force: B1 + B2 + U = 0 (vectors)
Moment about plane 1: B2 L + U a = 0 (vectors)

Here U is the unbalance (m r) of the rotor at distance a from plane 1, B1 and B2 are the balancing m r values in the two planes, and L is the distance between the planes. Solving gives a neat result, the lever rule:

|B2| = |U| × a/L and |B1| = |U| × (L - a)/L

Both balancing vectors point opposite to U. The plane nearer the unbalance gets the larger share, just like a seesaw.

Key ideaIn two-plane balancing, the far plane takes the share of the unbalance that is closer to it. Use the lever rule with the distances a and L.

Worked example

A rotor has a 2 kg unbalanced mass at radius 0.05 m, so U = 0.10 kg m. It sits a = 0.20 m from plane 1. The planes are L = 0.60 m apart. Balancing masses will be placed at radius 0.10 m. Find m1 and m2.

Step 1. Share in plane 2: |B2| = 0.10 × 0.20/0.60 = 0.0333 kg m. Mass m2 = 0.0333/0.10 = 0.333 kg.

Step 2. Share in plane 1: |B1| = 0.10 × (0.60 - 0.20)/0.60 = 0.0667 kg m. Mass m1 = 0.0667/0.10 = 0.667 kg.

Step 3. Check the force: 0.0667 + 0.0333 = 0.10 = U. The forces cancel.

Step 4. Check the moment about plane 1: 0.0333 × 0.60 = 0.02, and U × a = 0.10 × 0.20 = 0.02. The moments cancel.

U = 2.0 * 0.05          # unbalance m r in kg m
a, L = 0.20, 0.60       # distances in m
r_b = 0.10              # radius of the balancing masses in m

m2 = U * a / L / r_b
m1 = U * (L - a) / L / r_b

print(f"m1 = {m1:.3f} kg, m2 = {m2:.3f} kg")
print("force balance holds:", abs(m1 * r_b + m2 * r_b - U) < 1e-12)
print("moment balance holds:", abs(m2 * r_b * L - U * a) < 1e-12)
m1 = 0.667 kg, m2 = 0.333 kg
force balance holds: True
moment balance holds: True

TrapStudents often give the far plane the larger share. Remember the seesaw: the mass nearer the unbalance is pushed harder, so it takes more of the correction. Here plane 1 is farther from the unbalance and gets the larger share (0.667 kg), since it is 0.40 m away.

Self-check: if the unbalance sits exactly in plane 1 (a = 0), what is the balance in plane 2?

B2 = U × a/L = 0. Plane 2 needs no correction, and plane 1 takes the whole unbalance. This is the single-plane case.

Self-check: are the two planes' balancing vectors in the same direction?

Yes. Both are opposite to U, so they point in the same direction, and their magnitudes add to the unbalance.

Primary and secondary forces of a reciprocating mass

The slider-crank picture

In a petrol or diesel engine, a piston moves in a cylinder. A connecting rod links the piston to a crank, which turns the shaft. The piston and the part of the rod that moves with it form the reciprocating mass mR.

        TDC (theta = 0)
           |
     [piston]  m_R    <-- piston moves up and down the cylinder
           |
           |  connecting rod, length l
           |
         ( crank pin )  <-- crank radius r, angle theta from TDC
            \
             \  crank turns at omega
              \
            ( shaft )

The crank angle θ tells us where the crank is. The piston acceleration is shown approximately by

a ≈ r ω² (cos θ + (r/l) cos 2θ)

Here r is the crank radius, l is the connecting rod length, and r/l is the ratio that controls how much the rod is slanted. The inertia force is the mass times the acceleration, so the two parts are:

Primary force: Fp = mR r ω² cos θ (repeats once per revolution)
Secondary force: Fs = mR r ω² (r/l) cos 2θ (repeats twice per revolution)

The primary force is the big one. The secondary force is smaller, but it does not vanish, because the connecting rod is never infinitely long. The rod's length matters only through the ratio r/l.

Key ideaPrimary forces repeat once per revolution and secondary forces repeat twice. Both are proportional to mR r ω², so the speed is the most powerful lever on them.

Worked example

A reciprocating mass mR = 4 kg has crank radius r = 0.05 m and connecting rod l = 0.20 m. The crank speed is ω = 100 rad/s. Find the maximum primary and secondary forces.

Step 1. Basic quantity: mR r ω² = 4 × 0.05 × 100² = 4 × 0.05 × 10000 = 2000 N.

Step 2. Maximum primary force: the cosine is 1 at θ = 0, so Fp,max = 2000 N.

Step 3. Secondary factor: r/l = 0.05/0.20 = 0.25. So Fs,max = 2000 × 0.25 = 500 N.

Step 4. Check: the secondary force is one quarter of the primary force here, which is the right order of size for a rod of ratio 0.25.

mR, r, l, w = 4.0, 0.05, 0.20, 100.0

base = mR * r * w ** 2          # m_R r omega^2
primary_max = base              # cos(theta) is 1 at theta = 0
secondary_max = base * (r / l)  # (r/l) cos(2 theta) is largest when cos(2 theta) = 1

print(f"base m_R r w^2 = {base:.0f} N")
print(f"primary max = {primary_max:.0f} N")
print(f"secondary max = {secondary_max:.0f} N")
base m_R r w^2 = 2000 N
primary max = 2000 N
secondary max = 500 N
Self-check: at what crank angle is the secondary force a maximum in magnitude?

At θ = 0, 90, 180 and 270 degrees, where cos 2θ is ±1. The magnitude is the same at these points, with sign changes.

Self-check: if the rod gets longer and r/l gets smaller, what happens to the secondary force?

It gets smaller, because the secondary force is proportional to r/l.

Partial balancing of reciprocating masses

Why we balance only part of the mass

A reciprocating mass cannot be cancelled by a simple rotating counterweight without causing a new problem. A counterweight on the crank rotates in a circle, so its force always points around the shaft. The reciprocating force points along the cylinder. One way out is partial balancing: we replace a fraction C of the reciprocating mass by an equal rotating mass at the crank radius, and accept that some unbalance remains.

Balanced rotating mass: C × mR at radius r

Here C is a fraction between 0 and 1. Take C = 0.5 for a common compromise, and C = 1 to balance the primary force fully (which then leaves a sideways force).

The two unbalanced components

The rotating counterweight produces a force of size C mR r ω². Its components are along the stroke and across it. The reciprocating mass produces a force mR r ω² cos θ along the stroke. Adding the two, the unbalanced parts are:

Along the stroke (horizontal): (1 - C) mR r ω² cos θ
Across the stroke (vertical): C mR r ω² sin θ

The resultant is the vector sum of these two parts. Its size is

F = mR r ω² × √((1 - C)² cos² θ + C² sin² θ)

Key ideaWhen C = 1/2, the two parts have equal size, so the cos² and sin² terms add to 1/4 at every angle. The resultant is constant, equal to mR r ω²/2, and it no longer depends on the crank angle.

Worked example with C = 1/2 and C = 2/3

Use mR r ω² = 2000 N from the previous section. For C = 1/2 the resultant is 1000 N at every angle. For C = 2/3 the resultant changes with angle. Its largest value is at θ = 90 degrees, where it equals C × 2000 = 1333 N, and its smallest value is at θ = 0, where it equals (1 - C) × 2000 = 667 N.

import math

mR, r, w = 4.0, 0.05, 100.0
base = mR * r * w ** 2

for C in (0.5, 2 / 3):
    print(f"C = {C:.3f}")
    for th in (0, 30, 60, 90):
        t = math.radians(th)
        fh = (1 - C) * base * math.cos(t)     # along the stroke
        fv = C * base * math.sin(t)           # across the stroke
        R = math.hypot(fh, fv)
        print(f"  theta = {th:2d} deg, resultant = {R:7.1f} N")
C = 0.500
  theta =  0 deg, resultant =  1000.0 N
  theta = 30 deg, resultant =  1000.0 N
  theta = 60 deg, resultant =  1000.0 N
  theta = 90 deg, resultant =  1000.0 N
C = 0.667
  theta =  0 deg, resultant =   666.7 N
  theta = 30 deg, resultant =   881.9 N
  theta = 60 deg, resultant =  1201.9 N
  theta = 90 deg, resultant =  1333.3 N

The largest vertical force is the hammer blow, the value C mR r ω² that appears at θ = 90 degrees. Railway locomotives are a classic example, because the hammer blow pounds the track. The horizontal part is the force that shakes the machine along the stroke.

TrapThe hammer blow is C mR r ω², not the full reciprocating force. With C = 1/2 the hammer blow is 1000 N, and the resultant is also 1000 N at every angle, so the force never drops to zero.

Self-check: which value of C removes the horizontal (stroke) force at all angles?

C = 1. Then the stroke part (1 - C) is zero. The full vertical hammer blow remains, which is why full balancing of the primary force creates a sideways force.

Self-check: with C = 2/3, at which crank angle is the resultant smallest?

At θ = 0. There the vertical part is zero, and only the stroke part (1 - C) mR r ω² = 667 N remains.

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original GATE-style problems for practice, not real past paper questions. Work each one on paper before you read the answer.

Centrifugal force of a rotor

A rotor carries a 2 kg unbalanced mass at a radius of 0.1 m. It turns at 3000 rpm. Find the centrifugal force on the mass in newtons.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The force F in newtons.
  2. Which idea applies. Convert rpm to rad/s, then use F = m r ω².
  3. Work it out. ω = 2π × 3000/60 = 314.16 rad/s. ω² = 98696. F = 2 × 0.1 × 98696 = 19739 N.
  4. Check. About 20 kN. The force is large because the speed is high and the square of ω multiplies it.
import math

m, r, N = 2.0, 0.1, 3000
w = 2 * math.pi * N / 60
F = m * r * w ** 2
print(f"omega = {w:.2f} rad/s")
print(f"F = {F:.0f} N")
omega = 314.16 rad/s
F = 19739 N

Answer: about 19739 N (19.7 kN).

Counterweight for two masses

Two masses of 5 kg each sit at radius 0.1 m in the same plane. One is at 0 degrees and the other at 90 degrees. Find the mass of a counterweight at radius 0.2 m, and its angle.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Counterweight mass and angle.
  2. Which idea applies. Add the m r vectors, then cancel with a counterweight of equal size and opposite direction.
  3. Work it out. The vectors are (0.5, 0) and (0, 0.5). Sum = (0.5, 0.5), size 0.7071 kg m. mc = 0.7071/0.2 = 3.54 kg. Direction of the sum is 45 degrees, so the counterweight is at 225 degrees.
  4. Check. The counterweight's m r must be the same size as the sum, 0.7071 kg m. 3.54 × 0.2 = 0.708, which matches.

Answer: 3.54 kg at 225 degrees.

Which balancing statements are true?

Consider these statements about balancing rotating machinery.

(A) Two equal masses at equal radii always balance each other.
(B) Single-plane balancing corrects static unbalance.
(C) A couple unbalance needs at least two balance planes to correct it.
(D) The primary inertia force of a reciprocating mass repeats twice per revolution.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true (multiple selection).
  2. Which idea applies. Balance uses m r and vectors. A couple needs two planes. Primary forces repeat once per revolution.
  3. Work it out. (A) is false if the masses sit at different angles, since vectors can point apart. (B) is true. (C) is true, since a force pair in one plane cannot create a couple. (D) is false, because primary forces repeat once per revolution and secondary forces repeat twice.
  4. Check. Two equal masses at 0 and 180 degrees cancel, but at 0 and 90 degrees they do not, which confirms (A) is false.

Answer: (B) and (C).

Two-plane share

A rotor has an unbalance U = 0.2 kg m located 0.3 m from plane 1. The balance planes are 1.0 m apart. The balancing masses are placed at radius 0.1 m. Find the mass that must be placed in plane 1, in kg (numerical answer).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The mass m1 in kg.
  2. Which idea applies. The lever rule: B1 = U (L - a)/L.
  3. Work it out. B1 = 0.2 × (1.0 - 0.3)/1.0 = 0.14 kg m. Mass = 0.14/0.1 = 1.4 kg.
  4. Check. Plane 1 is the farther plane from the unbalance, so it should take the larger share (0.7 of U). 0.7 is larger than 0.3, which is consistent.

Answer: 1.4 kg.

Primary force of a single cylinder

A single-cylinder engine has a reciprocating mass of 2 kg, a crank radius of 40 mm and runs at 3000 rpm. Find the maximum primary inertia force in newtons (numerical answer). Ignore the secondary force.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Fp,max in newtons.
  2. Which idea applies. Fp = mR r ω², with the maximum at θ = 0.
  3. Work it out. Use r = 0.04 m and ω = 314.16 rad/s. F = 2 × 0.04 × 98696 = 7896 N.
  4. Check. The force is about 8 kN. Its size is consistent with the speed squared: 314 squared is about 10⁵.
import math

mR, r, N = 2.0, 0.040, 3000
w = 2 * math.pi * N / 60
print(f"F primary max = {mR * r * w ** 2:.0f} N")
F primary max = 7896 N

Answer: 7896 N (about 7.9 kN).

Partial balance extremes

A reciprocating mass of 4 kg has crank radius 0.05 m and the crank turns at 100 rad/s. The engineer balances a fraction C = 2/3 of the reciprocating mass with a rotating counterweight. Find the largest and smallest values of the unbalanced resultant force over all crank angles, in newtons. State the crank angle where the largest value occurs.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The maximum and minimum resultant, and the angle of the maximum.
  2. Which idea applies. F = mR r ω² √((1 - C)² cos² θ + C² sin² θ). The largest value is C mR r ω² at θ = 90 degrees, and the smallest is (1 - C) mR r ω² at θ = 0, because C is larger than 1 - C.
  3. Work it out. mR r ω² = 4 × 0.05 × 10000 = 2000 N. Largest = (2/3) × 2000 = 1333.3 N. Smallest = (1/3) × 2000 = 666.7 N.
  4. Check. A sweep over angles should show values between 666.7 N and 1333.3 N. The Python check below does that sweep.
import math

mR, r, w, C = 4.0, 0.05, 100.0, 2 / 3
base = mR * r * w ** 2
vals = []
for k in range(3601):
    t = math.radians(k / 10)
    vals.append(base * math.sqrt((1 - C) ** 2 * math.cos(t) ** 2 + C ** 2 * math.sin(t) ** 2))
print(f"largest = {max(vals):.1f} N")
print(f"smallest = {min(vals):.1f} N")
largest = 1333.3 N
smallest = 666.7 N

Answer: largest 1333.3 N at θ = 90 degrees, smallest 666.7 N at θ = 0.

Secondary force of a long rod

A reciprocating mass of 1.5 kg has crank radius 0.06 m and connecting rod length 0.24 m. The crank turns at 200 rad/s. Find the maximum secondary inertia force in newtons (numerical answer).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Fs,max in newtons.
  2. Which idea applies. Fs = mR r ω² (r/l) cos 2θ, with maximum magnitude when cos 2θ = ±1.
  3. Work it out. r/l = 0.06/0.24 = 0.25. mR r ω² = 1.5 × 0.06 × 40000 = 3600 N. Secondary = 3600 × 0.25 = 900 N.
  4. Check. The secondary force is one quarter of the basic force, which matches r/l = 1/4. Doubling l would halve r/l and halve the answer.

Answer: 900 N.

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Single plane balancing (NAT, 2 marks)

Masses on a shaft in one plane: 4 kg at 100 mm, 0°; 3 kg at 150 mm, 90°; 2 kg at 120 mm, 210°. Find the m·r of the balancing mass in kg·mm, correct to 1 decimal place.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The magnitude of the balancing m·r.
  2. Which idea applies. The vector sum of all m·r must be zero.
  3. Work it out. Sum x: 400 + 0 + 240 cos 210° = 400 − 207.8 = 192.2. y: 0 + 450 − 120 = 330. Magnitude 381.9. Balance = 381.9 kg·mm opposite.
  4. Check. The code adds the vectors.
import math
ms = [(4, 100, 0), (3, 150, 90), (2, 120, 210)]
x = sum(m * r * math.cos(math.radians(a)) for m, r, a in ms); y = sum(m * r * math.sin(math.radians(a)) for m, r, a in ms)
print("%.1f kg mm at %.1f deg" % (math.hypot(x, y), math.degrees(math.atan2(-y, -x)) % 360))

381.9 kg mm at 239.8 deg

Answer: about 381.9 kg·mm.

Exam-level 2: Primary unbalanced force (NAT, 2 marks)

A single cylinder engine has reciprocating mass 3 kg, crank 0.05 m, speed 1200 rpm. Find the maximum primary unbalanced force in N, correct to 1 decimal place.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. mω²r.
  2. Which idea applies. Primary force = mω²r cos θ.
  3. Work it out. ω = 125.66. 3 · 15791 · 0.05 = 2368.7 N.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
import math
w = 1200 * 2 * math.pi / 60
print("%.1f N" % (3 * w * w * 0.05))

2368.7 N

Answer: about 2368.7 N.

Exam-level 3: Balancing facts (MSQ, 2 marks)

Which are TRUE? (A) Rotating masses in several planes need balancing for both force and couple. (B) Reciprocating masses can be fully balanced by rotating masses. (C) Secondary forces vary at twice crank speed. (D) A six cylinder in line engine has inherent primary and secondary balance.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Balancing facts.
  2. Which idea applies. Rotating counterweights only partly cancel reciprocating forces.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) False. (C) True. (D) True.
  4. Check. This is why straight six engines run smoothly.

Answer: (A), (C) and (D).

Summary

Unbalance
A mass not centred on the shaft, which creates a rotating force.
Centrifugal force
F = m r ω², the outward force on a rotating mass.
Angular speed
ω = 2πN/60 in rad/s, from N in rpm.
Counterweight
A mass placed to cancel the net m r vector of a rotor.
Single-plane balance
The vector sum of m r is zero in one plane, which corrects static unbalance.
Couple unbalance
Forces in different planes that make a turning effect, which needs two planes to fix.
Dynamic balance
Both the net force and the net couple are zero.
Lever rule
In two planes, the balance shares are U a/L and U (L - a)/L.
Reciprocating mass
The moving mass of a piston and part of the connecting rod.
Primary force
mR r ω² cos θ, which repeats once per revolution.
Secondary force
mR r ω² (r/l) cos 2θ, which repeats twice per revolution.
Partial balancing
Balancing a fraction C of the reciprocating mass with a rotating counterweight.
Resultant of partial balance
mR r ω² √((1 - C)² cos² θ + C² sin² θ).
Hammer blow
C mR r ω², the maximum vertical unbalanced force.
Slider-crank
The crank, connecting rod and piston mechanism of an engine.
Top dead centre
Crank angle θ = 0, where the piston is furthest from the shaft.

Remember these

  • Balancing uses the product m r, and the masses must be added as vectors.
  • Centrifugal force grows with the square of the speed.
  • Two planes are needed when the forces cancel but a couple remains.
  • Primary forces repeat once per revolution, and secondary forces repeat twice.
  • Partial balancing with C = 1/2 gives a constant resultant of mR r ω²/2.
  • The hammer blow is C mR r ω².

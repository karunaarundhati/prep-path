Aptitude: verbal ability and data interpretation

Aptitude rounds test how quickly and accurately you read, reason and calculate. Today's block has two parts. Verbal ability asks about word meaning, sentence completion, analogies and reading passages. Data interpretation gives you a table or chart and asks questions that need a few calculations. Work each problem before reading the solution, and time yourself: aim for about one minute per verbal question and two to three minutes per data question.

Formulas you need for data interpretation

  • Percentage change = (new value minus old value) divided by old value, times 100. A positive result is growth, a negative result is decline.
  • Share of a part = part divided by total, times 100. The shares of all parts add up to 100 percent.
  • Pie chart angle for a value = (value divided by total) times 360 degrees.
  • Average = sum of the values divided by the number of values.
  • Ratio a:b = divide both numbers by their greatest common factor. For example, 150:104 becomes 75:52 after dividing by 2.
  • Growth of a total = (new total minus old total) divided by old total, times 100.

Change = 150 minus 120 = 30. Percentage change = 30 divided by 120, times 100 = 25 percent.

Problem 2: which product grew the most in percentage terms?

A: 30 divided by 120 = 25 percent. B: (180 minus 200) divided by 200 = minus 10 percent. C: (104 minus 80) divided by 80 = 24 divided by 80 = 30 percent. D: 20 divided by 150 = 13.3 percent. The answer is C. Notice that A has the largest absolute change (30 thousand units) but not the largest percentage change, because A started from a larger base.

Problem 3: product D's share of the 2023 total

Problem 4: the average 2024 sales per product

Average = 604 divided by 4 = 151 thousand units. Products B (180) and D (170) are above it, A (150) is just below it, and C (104) is far below. The average hides that spread, so it does not tell you what a typical product looks like.

Problem 5: the ratio of product A to product C in 2024

The ratio is 150:104. Both numbers are even, so divide by 2 to get 75:52. The two numbers have no common factor left, so the answer is 75:52.

Problem 6: the pie chart angle for product B in 2024

Angle = (180 divided by 604) times 360 = 180 times 360 divided by 604 = 64800 divided by 604, which is about 107.3 degrees. Check the scale: a full circle is 360 degrees, and B's share (about 29.8 percent) is a little under a third of the circle, which is about 120 degrees. 107 is a little less than 120, so it is reasonable.

TipIn data questions, compute the simplest route first. Shares and percentage changes are often easier as fractions. Comparing a fraction to a benchmark such as one half, one third or one quarter usually answers "is this more than that?" without a full calculation.

Verbal ability: strategies and solved items

For reading questions, read the question first, then scan the passage for the words it uses. For sentence completion, decide the missing word's meaning from the sentence before you look at the options, then choose the closest option. For para jumbles, look for the sentence that introduces the topic (usually general, with no "this" or "it" pointing backwards) and then follow pronouns and linking words such as "however", "therefore" and "this is why".

Verbal 1: synonym

"Lucid" means easy to understand and clear. The answer is clear, which is the closest meaning among options such as "vague", "clear", "heavy" and "hidden".

Verbal 2: antonym

"Frugal" means careful with money and avoiding waste. Its opposite is extravagant, meaning spending money freely. "Economical" is a synonym, which is a common trap in antonym questions.

Verbal 3: sentence completion

"The evidence was so ___ that the jury reached a verdict within an hour." Options: (a) ambiguous, (b) conclusive, (c) irrelevant, (d) fragile. A quick verdict needs evidence that settles the question, so the answer is (b) conclusive. Ambiguous evidence would slow the jury down.

Verbal 4: para jumble

Four sentences are given out of order. Q: "Wood is a common building material." R: "It has a density lower than water." P: "This is why a piece of pine floats." S: "Many small boats were once made of wood for this reason." Q introduces the topic. R gives the property. P explains the consequence of that property, using "this is why". S refers back to the reason in P. The correct order is Q, R, P, S.

Verbal 5: reading comprehension

Passage: "Cities that build bicycle lanes often see more cyclists within a few years. The change is not automatic, however. Short or disconnected lanes rarely attract riders who are new to cycling, because they feel unsafe moving between separate stretches. Planners who link lanes into continuous routes report much higher use."

Question: What is the main idea? The passage says that more lanes alone are not enough. Lanes must form connected routes to attract new riders. So the answer is "connected bicycle routes, rather than isolated lanes, bring in new cyclists". Options that say "any new lane increases cycling" are wrong, because the passage says the change is not automatic.

Verbal 6: analogy

Pen is to write as knife is to ___. A pen is the tool used to write, so a knife is the tool used to cut. Name the function, then match it.

TrapIn data questions, a larger number is not always a larger share or a larger growth. Always check the base. A product with 300 units that grows by 30 units has grown 10 percent, while a product with 100 units that grows by 20 units has grown 20 percent.

Key ideaPercentage change always divides by the old value. Share always divides by the total. Mixing these two up is the most common error in data interpretation.

Revision checklist: week 3

Week 3 covered networks, APIs and security, DBMS theory, SQL, and NoSQL. Cover each answer, say it aloud in two or three sentences, then reveal it to check. Mark any item you could not answer and return to it before the mock below.

Name the layers of the OSI model in order, and the four layers of the TCP/IP model.

OSI, from the bottom: physical, data link, network, transport, session, presentation, application. TCP/IP has four layers: network access (link), internet, transport and application. The TCP/IP layers roughly group the OSI layers: the top three OSI layers are one application layer in TCP/IP.

What is the difference between TCP and UDP, and when would you choose UDP?

TCP is connection-oriented and reliable: it sets up a connection with a handshake, retransmits lost data, and delivers bytes in order. UDP is connectionless and sends independent datagrams with no delivery guarantee or ordering. Choose UDP when a late packet is worse than a lost one, such as live voice and video, DNS queries, and games. HTTP/3 runs over QUIC, which is built on UDP.

Which HTTP methods are idempotent?

GET, HEAD, PUT, DELETE, and OPTIONS are idempotent: repeating the same request leaves the server in the same state. POST is not, because each repetition can create another resource. PATCH is not guaranteed to be idempotent, because it depends on what the patch says.

What do the status codes 401, 403, 404 and 500 mean?

401 Unauthorized means the request has no valid authentication, so the client should log in. 403 Forbidden means the server knows who the client is but refuses access. 404 Not Found means no resource exists at that URL. 500 Internal Server Error means the server failed while handling a valid request.

What are the three parts of a JWT, and what does it protect?

A JWT has a header, a payload and a signature, each base64url-encoded and joined by dots. The signature lets the server detect tampering, so it protects integrity and proves who issued the token. The payload is readable by anyone who has the token, so it must not contain secrets unless the token is also encrypted.

What is the difference between hashing and encryption, and how should passwords be stored?

Hashing is one-way: you cannot recover the input from the output, and the output has a fixed length. Encryption is reversible with a key. Passwords should be stored with a slow, salted hash designed for passwords, such as bcrypt or argon2. A plain fast hash such as SHA-256 without a salt is weak, because attackers can test billions of guesses quickly.

What is SQL injection, and how do you prevent it?

SQL injection happens when user input is pasted into a SQL string, so the input can change the query's meaning. Prevent it with parameterized queries (prepared statements), where the query text and the values are sent separately. Input validation helps, but it is not a substitute. Never build SQL with string formatting from user input.

Explain 1NF, 2NF, 3NF and BCNF in one line each.

1NF: every column holds atomic values and there are no repeating groups. 2NF: 1NF, and no non-key column depends on only part of a composite key. 3NF: 2NF, and no non-key column depends on another non-key column. BCNF: every determinant is a candidate key.

List the four isolation levels and the anomalies each one allows.

Read uncommitted allows dirty reads, reading data another transaction has not committed. Read committed prevents dirty reads but allows non-repeatable reads, where the same row returns different values within one transaction. Repeatable read prevents those but the SQL standard allows phantom reads, where a repeated range query returns new rows. Serializable prevents all three. PostgreSQL treats read uncommitted as read committed, and its repeatable read also prevents phantoms.

Why do databases use B+ tree indexes?

A B+ tree has a very high fan-out, so each node holds hundreds of keys and the tree stays only three or four levels deep even for millions of rows. Every lookup reads only a few pages. All values sit in linked leaf nodes, so a range scan walks the leaves in order without going back up the tree.

What is the difference between CAP and PACELC?

CAP says that during a network partition a distributed system must choose between consistency and availability. PACELC adds the normal case: if there is a partition choose availability or consistency; else choose latency or consistency.

Explain the cache-aside pattern, and name one way it can go wrong.

The application checks the cache first, reads the database on a miss, and stores the result with a TTL. It can serve stale data after a write unless the cached key is deleted or updated, and a popular key that expires can cause a cache stampede of simultaneous database reads.

Mock interview: questions and model answers

Set a timer for 30 minutes. Answer each question out loud in under two minutes, then compare with the model answer. Score yourself on accuracy first and clarity second. Where the model answer uses a term you could not explain, go back to the matching day.

Walk me through what happens when you type a URL into a browser and press Enter.

The browser parses the URL into scheme, host, port and path. It checks its cache and the operating system's DNS cache for the host's IP address. If the answer is not cached, a DNS lookup runs: a recursive resolver asks the root, then the top-level domain server, then the domain's authoritative server, and each answer is cached for its TTL. The browser opens a TCP connection to the IP on port 443 with a three-way handshake (SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK). It then runs a TLS handshake: the server presents a certificate, the browser checks it against trusted certificate authorities, and both sides agree on session keys. The browser sends an HTTP GET request with headers such as Host and cookies. The server, often through a load balancer and an application that queries a database, returns a status code and a body. The browser parses the HTML into the DOM, parses CSS into the CSSOM, fetches scripts, styles and images, runs JavaScript, and renders the page.

Compare TCP and UDP, and give one use case for each.

TCP provides a reliable, ordered byte stream with connection setup, retransmission and flow control. Use it for web pages, email and file transfer. UDP provides unordered, unacknowledged datagrams with very little overhead. Use it for live video calls, where a late frame is useless and retransmitting it would only add delay.

When would you choose REST, GraphQL or gRPC?

REST uses resources and standard HTTP methods, cacheable and easy to explore, a good default for public APIs. GraphQL lets the client ask for exactly the fields it needs in one request, which suits many-screen apps with varied data needs, but caching and rate limiting are harder. gRPC uses HTTP/2 and binary Protocol Buffers with typed contracts, a strong fit for fast service-to-service calls inside a backend.

Explain third normal form with an example.

Take a table orders(order_id, customer_id, customer_city, total). The city depends on the customer, not on the order, which is a transitive dependency through customer_id. To reach 3NF, move customer_city into a customers table keyed by customer_id, and keep only customer_id in orders. Updating a customer's city then changes one row instead of every order.

What is a phantom read, and which isolation level prevents it?

A phantom read happens when a transaction runs a query with a condition, another transaction inserts or deletes a row that matches the condition and commits, and the first transaction runs the query again and sees a different set of rows. The SQL standard prevents phantoms only at Serializable. PostgreSQL's Repeatable Read also prevents them, because each transaction works on a snapshot taken at its start.

Why do databases use B+ tree indexes instead of binary search trees on disk?

Disk reads happen in blocks, so the goal is to read as few blocks as possible. A B+ tree puts hundreds of keys in each node, one block per node, so the height is small. Its leaves are linked in order, which makes range queries efficient. A binary tree would be far deeper, needing many more disk reads per lookup.

Which NoSQL database would you choose for a session store with millions of users, and why?

Redis. Sessions are looked up by a single key, need to be read on almost every request, and should expire automatically. Redis is in memory, so reads are very fast, and TTLs remove old sessions without a cleanup job. Mention the trade-off: the data must fit in memory, and the persistence settings decide how much session data can be lost in a crash. Then explain how the application recovers, for example by asking the user to log in again.

What is a deadlock, and how does a database handle it?

A deadlock is a cycle of waiting: transaction T1 holds lock A and waits for lock B, while T2 holds B and waits for A. Four conditions must hold together: mutual exclusion, hold and wait, no preemption, and circular wait. Databases usually break the cycle by detection: they build a wait-for graph, find the cycle, and abort one transaction, the victim, which the application can retry. Keeping transactions short and always locking rows in the same order prevents most deadlocks.

Resume and HR: stories, templates and common questions

HR and behavioural rounds ask for evidence. Interviewers want to know what you did, how you thought, and what changed. The STAR method gives each story four parts:

  • Situation. One or two sentences of context: where you were and what was at stake.
  • Task. Your specific responsibility, which may differ from the team's goal.
  • Action. What you did, in the first person, with the key decisions and why you made them. This is the longest part.
  • Result. What changed, with a number if you have one, and what you learned.

Longest Increasing Subsequence (LeetCode 300)

Given an integer array, return the length of the longest strictly increasing subsequence. A subsequence keeps the original order but may skip elements. Example: [10,9,2,5,3,7,101,18] gives 4, for example 2, 5, 7, 101. Example: [7,7,7,7] gives 1, because "strictly" increasing forbids equal values.

How to think about it

  1. Understand. A subsequence is not a contiguous run. Strict means equal neighbours do not count. The answer is at least 1 for a non-empty array.
  2. Brute force. Try every subset of indices, keep those whose values increase, and take the largest. That is 2 to the power n subsets, which is impossible for n of 2500.
  3. Find the waste. The best subsequence ending at each position is reused by many longer subsequences, but the brute force recomputes it.
  4. Optimise. Dynamic programming: let dp[i] be the length of the longest increasing subsequence ending at index i. dp[i] is 1 plus the maximum dp[j] over earlier j with nums[j] less than nums[i]. That is O(n squared). For O(n log n), keep an array tails where tails[k] is the smallest possible last value of an increasing subsequence of length k+1. For each number, binary search for the first tail that is not smaller than it and replace that tail, or append if none exists. The length of tails is the answer.
  5. Dry run. On [10,9,2,5,3,7,101,18]: 10 gives [10]. 9 replaces 10, giving [9]. 2 replaces 9, giving [2]. 5 appends, giving [2,5]. 3 replaces 5, giving [2,3]. 7 appends, giving [2,3,7]. 101 appends, giving [2,3,7,101]. 18 replaces 101, giving [2,3,7,18]. The length is 4.

Code

from bisect import bisect_left


def length_of_lis(nums):
    tails = []                       # tails[k] = smallest tail of an increasing run of length k+1
    for x in nums:
        i = bisect_left(tails, x)    # first tail that is not smaller than x
        if i == len(tails):
            tails.append(x)          # x extends the longest run found so far
        else:
            tails[i] = x             # x makes a run of length i+1 end lower
    return len(tails)


print(length_of_lis([10, 9, 2, 5, 3, 7, 101, 18]))
print(length_of_lis([0, 1, 0, 3, 2, 3]))

Word Break (LeetCode 139)

Given a string s and a list of words wordDict, return true if s can be split into one or more dictionary words, with each word used as many times as needed. Example: "leetcode" with ["leet","code"] gives true. Example: "catsandog" with ["cats","dog","sand","and","cat"] gives false, because the final "g" cannot be covered.

How to think about it

  1. Understand. We need one valid segmentation, not all of them. A word may appear more than once in the split.
  2. Brute force. Recursion: from the start, try each dictionary word that matches the prefix, then recurse on the rest. Return true if any branch finishes.
  3. Find the waste. The same suffix is checked again through different splits. For "aaaa...", the number of splits grows exponentially, while the number of distinct suffixes is only n.
  4. Optimise. Dynamic programming on prefixes. Let dp[i] be true if the first i characters can be segmented. dp[0] is true. dp[i] is true if some j less than i has dp[j] true and s[j:i] is a dictionary word. Checking every j gives O(n squared) substring comparisons, which is fast enough for n up to 300. Put the words in a set so each lookup is quick.
  5. Dry run. On "leetcode" with ["leet","code"]: dp[0] is true. dp[4] is true because "leet" is a word. dp[8] is true because dp[4] is true and "code" is a word. The answer is dp[8], which is true.

def word_break(s, word_dict):
    words = set(word_dict)
    n = len(s)
    dp = [False] * (n + 1)           # dp[i] is True if s[:i] can be segmented
    dp[0] = True
    for i in range(1, n + 1):
        for j in range(i):
            if dp[j] and s[j:i] in words:
                dp[i] = True
                break
    return dp[n]


def word_break_brute(s, word_dict):
    words = set(word_dict)

    def can(start):
        if start == len(s):
            return True
        for end in range(start + 1, len(s) + 1):
            if s[start:end] in words and can(end):
                return True
        return False

    return can(0)

Merge k Sorted Lists (LeetCode 23)

You are given an array of k linked lists, each sorted in ascending order. Merge them into one sorted linked list and return its head. Example: lists [1,4,5], [1,3,4] and [2,6] merge into [1,1,2,3,4,4,5,6]. Example: an empty array of lists gives an empty list.

How to think about it

  1. Understand. Let N be the total number of nodes and k the number of lists. The output must be a single list in sorted order, and you may relink the existing nodes.
  2. Brute force. Copy every value into one array, sort it, and build a new list. That is simple and correct, costing O(N log N) time and O(N) extra space for the array.
  3. Find the waste. Sorting everything ignores the fact that each input list is already sorted. Only the k current heads matter at any moment.
  4. Optimise. Keep a min-heap of the current head of each list. Pop the smallest, append it to the output, and push the next node from the same list. Each of the N pops and pushes costs O(log k), so the total is O(N log k). Heap entries are tuples (value, list index, node); the list index breaks ties because ListNode objects cannot be compared.
  5. Dry run. Heads are 1 (list 0), 1 (list 1), and 2 (list 2). Pop 1 from list 0 and push 4. Pop 1 from list 1 and push 3. Pop 2 and push 6. Pop 3, push 4. Pop 4 from list 0, push 5. Pop 4 from list 1, list 1 is empty. Pop 5, pop 6. Output: 1, 1, 2, 3, 4, 4, 5, 6.

import heapq


class ListNode:
    def __init__(self, val=0, next=None):
        self.val = val
        self.next = next


def build(values):
    dummy = ListNode()
    tail = dummy
    for v in values:
        tail.next = ListNode(v)
        tail = tail.next
    return dummy.next


def to_list(head):
    out = []
    while head:
        out.append(head.val)
        head = head.next
    return out


def merge_k_lists(lists):
    heap = []
    for i, node in enumerate(lists):
        if node:
            heapq.heappush(heap, (node.val, i, node))   # i breaks ties; nodes are not comparable
    dummy = ListNode()
    tail = dummy
    while heap:
        val, i, node = heapq.heappop(heap)
        tail.next = node
        tail = node
        if node.next:
            heapq.heappush(heap, (node.next.val, i, node.next))
    tail.next = None
    return dummy.next


def merge_k_brute(lists):
    values = []
    for head in lists:
        values.extend(to_list(head))
    return build(sorted(values))


print(to_list(merge_k_lists([build([1, 4, 5]), build([1, 3, 4]), build([2, 6])])))
print(to_list(merge_k_lists([])))
print(to_list(merge_k_lists([None, build([2])])))
print(to_list(merge_k_brute([build([1, 4, 5]), build([1, 3, 4]), build([2, 6])])))

TrapPushing a bare ListNode onto the heap raises an error as soon as two nodes have equal values, because Python cannot order the objects. Add a unique tiebreaker such as the list index, as the code does.

Balanced three-phase circuits

A three-phase system uses three sinusoidal voltages that are equal in size and spaced 120 degrees apart. It delivers power smoothly and drives motors and actuators efficiently. For GATE RA, where robots and machines run on three-phase power, balanced three-phase calculations are a standard topic.

The idea in plain words

Imagine three children on three swings, all pushed by the same rhythm. Each swing moves in the same way, but each child starts a little later than the one before. The three swings are never at the same position at the same time. They are spread evenly around the cycle.

A three-phase system works the same way. It has three sinusoidal voltages with the same size and the same frequency. They differ only in timing. Each one lags the previous one by one third of a full cycle, which is 120 degrees. A system like this is called balanced when the three voltages are equal and evenly spaced.

Why do power companies and factories use three phases? Three sinusoids that are spread evenly add up to zero at every instant. This means the power they deliver to a balanced load is steady, not pulsing. A three-phase motor also gets a rotating magnetic field from the three supply phases, which is why motors and actuators are built for three phase. Robot arms and industrial machines are often driven this way.

Key ideaIn a balanced three-phase system, the three phase voltages are equal in size and 120 degrees apart. Their sum is zero at every instant, so you can often analyse just one phase and multiply by three.

Key terms

Each term has a plain meaning first, then a precise definition.

Phase
One of the three sinusoids in the system. Precisely: one of the three voltage sources, labelled a, b and c.
Balanced system
A system with equal magnitudes and equal 120 degree spacing. Precisely: Va + Vb + Vc = 0.
Phase sequence
The order in which the phases reach their peaks. Precisely: positive sequence a, b, c, and negative sequence a, c, b.
Phase voltage Vp
The voltage across one phase of a load or source. Precisely: the voltage of one winding or one load branch.
Line voltage VL
The voltage between two supply lines. Precisely: the voltage between any two of the three conductors.
Phase current Ip
The current through one phase of a load or source. Precisely: the current in one winding or one load branch.
Line current IL
The current in one supply conductor. Precisely: the current flowing in any of the three lines.
Star (Y) connection
Three branches joined at one common point, the neutral. Precisely: each branch runs from a line to the neutral.
Delta (Δ) connection
Three branches joined in a closed loop. Precisely: each branch runs from one line to another.
Neutral
The common point of a star connection. Precisely: the junction that the three star branches share.
Real power P
The power that does useful work, in watt (W). Precisely: P = V I cos φ per phase.
Reactive power Q
The power that swings back and forth, in var. Precisely: Q = V I sin φ per phase.
Apparent power S
The product of voltage and current, in volt-ampere (VA). Precisely: S = V I per phase.
Power factor
The fraction of apparent power that is real power. Precisely: cos φ, where φ is the angle of the load impedance.

Three balanced sinusoids and phase sequence

The three phasors

Take the peak of phase a as the reference. Then, using phasors (complex numbers with a length and an angle), the three phase voltages are:

Va = Vp ∠0°, Vb = Vp ∠−120°, Vc = Vp ∠120°

Here Vp is the magnitude of each phase voltage, and the angles show the timing. Phase b lags phase a by 120 degrees, and phase c lags phase b by another 120 degrees. Since −240° is the same as 120°, phase c is shown as ∠120° here. Usually the magnitudes are given as rms values, so Vp means the rms phase voltage.

Add the three phasors. Each has length Vp, spaced evenly around a circle. They point in three directions that cancel out, so Va + Vb + Vc = 0. This is the defining feature of a balanced system.

Phase sequence

The order a, b, c, with b lagging a and c lagging b, is the positive phase sequence. If we swap two of the lines, the order becomes a, c, b, which is the negative sequence. The phase sequence matters for motors, because it sets the direction of rotation. Reversing two lines reverses the motor.

TrapBalanced systems are only balanced if all three voltages are equal in size and exactly 120 degrees apart. If one phase is smaller or the angles are not 120 degrees, the sum is not zero, and the simple one-phase method does not apply.

Self-check

What is the sum of the three phase voltages of a balanced system?

Zero, at every instant. The three phasors point in directions that cancel out.

How do you change the direction of rotation of a three-phase motor?

Swap any two of the three supply lines. This changes the phase sequence from positive to negative.

Star (Y) connection

The relationships

In a star connection, each phase branch runs from one line to the neutral. So the voltage across each branch is a line-to-neutral voltage. For a balanced star:

VL = √3 Vp, and IL = Ip

The line voltage is √3 times the phase voltage, and it leads the phase voltage by 30 degrees. The line current equals the phase current, because each line carries the current of its own branch. Here √3 is about 1.732.

The line voltage is the voltage between two lines, which is the difference of two phase voltages. Two phasors that are 120 degrees apart, with equal length Vp, have a difference of length √3 Vp. This is the geometry behind the √3 factor.

Worked example 1: a star-connected load

A balanced star load has impedance Z = 3 + j4 ohm in each phase. The supply line voltage is 400 V (rms). Find the phase voltage, the phase current, and the total power.

  1. Phase voltage. Vp = VL/√3 = 400/1.732 = 230.94 V.
  2. Impedance magnitude. |Z| = √(32 + 42) = 5 ohm. The power factor is cos φ = 3/5 = 0.6.
  3. Phase current. Ip = Vp/|Z| = 230.94/5 = 46.19 A. In a star, IL = Ip, so the line current is also 46.19 A.
  4. Total power. P = 3 Vp Ip cos φ = 3 × 230.94 × 46.19 × 0.6, which is about 19,200 W, or 19.2 kW.

The Python check below does the same calculation, and it also computes the delta case in the next section.

import cmath, math
VL = 400.0
Vp = VL / math.sqrt(3)
Z = 3 + 4j
pf = Z.real / abs(Z)
Ip_Y = Vp / abs(Z)
P_Y = 3 * Vp * Ip_Y * pf
print("star: phase voltage =", round(Vp, 2), "V")
print("star: phase current =", round(Ip_Y, 2), "A")
print("star: total power =", round(P_Y, 2), "W")
Ip_D = VL / abs(Z)
Il_D = math.sqrt(3) * Ip_D
P_D = 3 * VL * Ip_D * pf
print("delta: phase current =", round(Ip_D, 2), "A")
print("delta: line current =", round(Il_D, 2), "A")
print("delta: total power =", round(P_D, 2), "W")
print("ratio delta/star =", round(P_D / P_Y, 2))

star: phase current = 46.19 A

delta: phase current = 80.0 A

Self-check

In a balanced star, a line voltage is 208 V. What is the phase voltage?

Vp = 208/√3, which is about 120 V.

Why does the √3 factor appear in the line voltage of a star?

The line voltage is the difference of two phase voltages that are 120 degrees apart. Two phasors of length Vp at 120 degrees have a difference of length √3 Vp.

Delta (Δ) connection

The relationships

In a delta connection, each phase branch runs from one line to the next line. So the voltage across each branch is a line-to-line voltage. For a balanced delta:

VL = Vp, and IL = √3 Ip

The line voltage equals the phase voltage. Each line current is the difference of two branch currents, and that difference has size √3 times the branch current. The line current lags its matching phase current by 30 degrees.

Worked example 2: the same load in delta

The same load as Worked example 1, with Z = 3 + j4 ohm in each phase, is now connected in delta to the same 400 V supply. Find the phase current, the line current and the total power.

  1. Phase voltage. Vp = VL = 400 V.
  2. Phase current. Ip = 400/5 = 80 A.
  3. Line current. IL = √3 × 80 = 138.56 A.
  4. Total power. P = 3 × 400 × 80 × 0.6 = 57,600 W, which is 57.6 kW.
  5. Compare with the star. The star gave 19.2 kW. The delta gives exactly three times as much, 57.6 kW.

Key ideaFor the same impedance per phase and the same line voltage, a delta load draws three times the power of a star load. Its phase voltage is √3 times larger, and power depends on the square of voltage.

TrapThe most common error is to use the star formulas for a delta, or the reverse. For a star, the phase voltage is VL/√3 and the line current equals the phase current. For a delta, the phase voltage equals VL and the line current is √3 times the phase current.

Self-check

In a balanced delta, the phase current is 10 A. What is the line current?

IL = √3 × 10 = 17.32 A.

A delta load has the same impedance as a star load on the same supply. Which draws more power, and by what factor?

The delta draws three times as much power, because its phase voltage is √3 times larger, and power is proportional to the square of voltage.

Power in balanced three-phase circuits

The three power formulas

Add the power of the three phases. In a balanced system, each phase carries the same power, so the total is three times one phase. Written with line quantities, which are what meters and supplies show, the formulas are:

P = √3 VL IL cos φ (real power, in W)
Q = √3 VL IL sin φ (reactive power, in var)
S = √3 VL IL (apparent power, in VA)

Here φ is the angle of the phase impedance, which is the same in star and delta. The three quantities are linked by P2 + Q2 = S2. The power factor is cos φ.

These line-quantity formulas work for both star and delta, because they use VL and IL, which are the same whatever the connection. This is their great advantage.

Worked example 3: the power triangle

Return to the star load of Worked example 1, with VL = 400 V and IL = 46.19 A. The power factor is 0.6, and sin φ = 0.8.

  1. Real power. P = √3 × 400 × 46.19 × 0.6, which is 19,200 W.
  2. Apparent power. S = √3 × 400 × 46.19 = 32,000 VA, or 32 kVA.
  3. Reactive power. Q = √3 × 400 × 46.19 × 0.8 = 25,600 var, or 25.6 kvar.
  4. Check. P2 + Q2 = 19,2002 + 25,6002 = 368.64 + 655.36 (in million) = 1024 (in million), and S2 = 32,0002 = 1024 (in million). The triangle closes.
import math
VL = 400.0
Vp = VL / math.sqrt(3)
Ip = Vp / 5
Q = 3 * Vp * Ip * 0.8
S = 3 * Vp * Ip
print("S =", round(S / 1000, 2), "kVA")
print("Q =", round(Q / 1000, 2), "kvar")
S = 32.0 kVA
Q = 25.6 kvar

TrapUse the line current and line voltage in the three-phase formulas, not the phase values, unless you multiply by three and use the phase values consistently. Mixing the two is a common GATE error.

Self-check

A balanced load draws line voltage 400 V and line current 10 A at power factor 0.8. What is P?

P = √3 × 400 × 10 × 0.8 = 5,542 W, about 5.54 kW.

Why is the power formula written with line quantities?

Line quantities are the same whether the load is star or delta. Using them lets you ignore the connection when you only need the total power.

Why three phase is used

Constant power

A single-phase supply delivers power that pulses twice in every cycle, because the voltage passes through zero. A balanced three-phase supply delivers the same total power at every instant. The three pulses fill each other's gaps. This is why three-phase machines run more smoothly and with less vibration.

Less conductor for the same power

For a given power and voltage, a three-phase line needs less copper than a single-phase line of the same length. This is one of the main reasons power is distributed in three phases. The savings come from using three conductors that share the load efficiently, without a separate return path for each phase.

Rotating fields for motors and actuators

Three windings placed 120 degrees apart in space, fed by three phases 120 degrees apart in time, produce a magnetic field that rotates at constant speed. A three-phase induction motor uses this rotating field to turn its rotor, with no brushes and no switch. Reversing two phases reverses the rotation. For robotics and automation, this is the reason so many actuators and drive systems are three-phase.

GATE loves thisExpect questions that combine the balanced-system facts. For example, a question may give a delta load and ask for line current, or give power and ask for a line current. Find the connection first, then choose the matching formulas.

Self-check

Why does a three-phase supply deliver steady power while a single-phase supply does not?

In a balanced three-phase supply, the three phase powers peak at different times and add to a constant total. A single-phase supply has no other phase to fill the gaps, so its power pulses.

What does reversing two supply lines do to a three-phase motor?

It reverses the phase sequence, which reverses the direction of the rotating field, and so reverses the motor's rotation.

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original problems in the GATE style, not past exam questions. Try each one on paper first.

Phase voltage of a star

A balanced star is supplied from a 400 V line voltage system. Find the phase voltage, in volt.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The phase voltage Vp.
  2. Which idea applies. In a star, VL = √3 Vp.
  3. Work it out. Vp = 400/√3 = 230.9 V.
  4. Check. Multiply back: 230.9 × 1.732 is about 400, which matches the line voltage.

Answer: about 230.9 V.

import math
print("phase voltage =", round(400 / math.sqrt(3), 1), "V")
phase voltage = 230.9 V

Line current of a delta

A balanced delta has a phase current of 10 A. Find the line current, in ampere.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The line current IL.
  2. Which idea applies. In a delta, IL = √3 Ip.
  3. Work it out. IL = 10 × 1.732 = 17.32 A.
  4. Check. The line current is larger than the phase current, as expected for a delta.

Answer: 17.32 A.

import math
print("line current =", round(10 * math.sqrt(3), 2), "A")
line current = 17.32 A

Statements about balanced systems (MSQ)

Select all that apply, for a balanced three-phase system.

(A) In a balanced star, the line current equals the phase current.
(B) In a balanced delta, the line voltage equals the phase voltage.
(C) Reversing any two supply lines leaves the rotation of a motor unchanged.
(D) The total real power of a balanced three-phase load is three times the power of one phase.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true.
  2. Which idea applies. The star and delta relations, the phase sequence, and the power sum.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) True. (C) False: reversing two lines reverses the phase sequence and the rotation. (D) True.
  4. Check. Statement (C) is the usual motor trap. The other three are the basic facts of this page.

Answer: (A), (B) and (D).

Line current of a resistive star (NAT)

A balanced star has a 6 ohm resistor in each phase. The line voltage is 208 V. Find the line current, in ampere, to two decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The line current IL.
  2. Which idea applies. Vp = VL/√3, and in a star IL = Ip = Vp/R.
  3. Work it out. Vp = 208/√3, about 120.09 V. IL = 208/(√3 × 6), which is about 20.01 A.
  4. Check. The same answer comes from IL = 208/(√3 × 6) = 208/10.39 = 20.01 A.

Answer: 20.01 A.

import math
print("line current =", round(208 / (math.sqrt(3) * 6), 2), "A")
line current = 20.01 A

Total power of a resistive delta (NAT)

A balanced delta has a 10 ohm resistor in each phase. The line voltage is 100 V. Find the total real power, in watt.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The total real power P.
  2. Which idea applies. In a delta, Vp = 100 V, so each phase current is Ip = 100/10 = 10 A, and P = 3 Vp Ip cos φ with cos φ = 1.
  3. Work it out. P = 3 × 100 × 10 × 1 = 3000 W.
  4. Check. The line current is 10√3 A, and P = √3 × 100 × 10√3 = 3000 W. The same value.

Answer: 3000 W.

print("total power =", 3 * 100 * 10, "W")
total power = 3000 W

Delta to star power ratio (NAT)

The same impedance per phase is connected once in star and once in delta, to the same line voltage supply. Find the ratio of the delta power to the star power.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Pdelta/Pstar.
  2. Which idea applies. The phase voltage is VL in delta and VL/√3 in star. Power in each phase is Vp2/Z (resistive case).
  3. Work it out. Delta power ∝ VL2, star power ∝ VL2/3. The ratio is 3.
  4. Check. Worked example 2 gave 57.6 kW against 19.2 kW, a ratio of 3.

Answer: 3.

from fractions import Fraction as F
print("ratio =", F(400 ** 2) / (F(400 ** 2) / 3))
ratio = 3

Reactive power of a star load (NAT)

A balanced star has an impedance of 3 + j4 ohm in each phase, and is supplied at 400 V line voltage. Find the total reactive power, in kvar, to one decimal place.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The total Q in kvar.
  2. Which idea applies. Q = √3 VL IL sin φ, with sin φ = 4/5 = 0.8.
  3. Work it out. In a star, IL = VL/(√3 |Z|) = 400/(√3 × 5) = 46.19 A. Then Q = √3 × 400 × 46.19 × 0.8 = 25,600 var, which is 25.6 kvar.
  4. Check. The same answer is obtained from 3 Vp Ip sin φ, since the phase values give the same total.

Answer: 25.6 kvar.

import math
VL = 400.0
Vp = VL / math.sqrt(3)
Ip = Vp / 5
Q = 3 * Vp * Ip * 0.8
print("Q =", round(Q / 1000, 1), "kvar")
Q = 25.6 kvar

Statements about three-phase facts (MSQ)

Select all that apply.

(A) In a balanced delta, the line current lags its matching phase current by 30 degrees.
(B) In a balanced star, the line voltage equals the phase voltage.
(C) The sum of the three phase voltages of a balanced system is zero at every instant.
(D) Swapping any two of the three supply lines reverses the phase sequence.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true.
  2. Which idea applies. The delta phase angle, the star voltage relation, the balanced sum, and the phase sequence.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) False: the line voltage is √3 times the phase voltage. (C) True. (D) True.
  4. Check. Statements (C) and (D) are standard facts used in motor and machine questions.

Answer: (A), (C) and (D).

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Line and phase quantities (NAT, 2 marks)

A balanced star load has 10 Ω per phase on a 400 V (line) three phase supply. Find the line current, correct to 2 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The line current.
  2. Which idea applies. In star, Vphase = Vline/√3 and Iline = Iphase.
  3. Work it out. Vph = 230.94 V, I = 23.09 A.
  4. Check. Total power = 3 · 23.09² · 10 = 16 kW, which matches √3 · 400 · 23.09 at unity power factor.
import math
I = 400 / math.sqrt(3) / 10
print("%.2f A, P = %.0f W" % (I, 3 * I * I * 10))

23.09 A, P = 16000 W

Answer: about 23.09 A.

Exam-level 2: Star to delta (NAT, 2 marks)

The same resistors (10 Ω per phase) are reconnected in delta on the same 400 V supply. By what factor does the total power change?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The ratio of delta to star power.
  2. Which idea applies. Each delta resistor sees the full line voltage.
  3. Work it out. PΔ = 3 · 400²/10 = 48 kW. Ratio 48/16 = 3.
  4. Check. This is why star-delta starters reduce starting power by a factor of 3.

Answer: 3.

Exam-level 3: Two wattmeter method (NAT, 2 marks)

In the two wattmeter method on a balanced load, the readings are 3 kW and 1 kW. Find the power factor, correct to 3 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The power factor from the two readings.
  2. Which idea applies. tan φ = √3(W1 − W2)/(W1 + W2).
  3. Work it out. tan φ = √3 · 2/4 = 0.866. φ = 40.89°, cos φ = 0.756.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
import math
phi = math.atan(math.sqrt(3) * 2 / 4)
print("%.2f deg, pf = %.3f" % (math.degrees(phi), math.cos(phi)))

40.89 deg, pf = 0.756

Answer: about 0.756.

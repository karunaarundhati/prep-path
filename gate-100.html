K-Nearest Neighbour

K-nearest neighbour, or KNN, predicts a new point from the points closest to it. It needs no fitted formula, only a distance and a choice of k. You will learn distance measures, how to classify and predict with neighbours, how to choose k, and why high dimensions cause trouble.

The idea in plain words

Imagine you start at a new school and want to guess which sport a new classmate likes. You look at the three students who sit closest to them and see what they play. If two play football and one plays cricket, you guess football. You did not build a theory of why people like sports. You simply trusted the people nearest to the new student.

That is the whole idea of K-nearest neighbour, usually written KNN. To predict for a new point:

  1. Measure how far the new point is from every training point.
  2. Pick the k closest training points. These are the neighbours.
  3. For classification, let the neighbours vote and pick the most common label. For regression, take the average of their values.

KNN is different from the earlier methods in one important way. Linear regression and logistic regression learn a set of weights, and then they throw the training data away. KNN keeps all the training data and does its work when a new point arrives. This is why it is called a lazy learner.

For GATE, KNN appears in the Machine Learning part of the Data Science and AI paper (DA). Typical questions give a small table of points, ask you to compute distances, and ask for the class or value with a given k. Questions also test the effect of k, the curse of dimensionality and the meaning of lazy learning. You need only basic arithmetic and square roots to work through this page.

Key terms

Read these first. Each one is used again on the page.

Training point
A data point whose correct label or value is known and which the model stores.
Query point
The new point we want to predict for.
Feature
One measured number describing a point, such as height or weight. Points with several features are vectors.
Distance
A number that says how far apart two points are. Smaller means more alike.
Euclidean distance
The straight-line distance. Precisely: the square root of the sum of squared differences of the features.
Manhattan distance
The distance along a city grid, moving only sideways and up or down. Precisely: the sum of absolute differences of the features.
Hyperparameter k
The number of neighbours used. It is chosen by us, not learned from the data.
Majority vote
Picking the label that appears most often among the neighbours.
Lazy learner
A method that stores the training data and does its work only at prediction time.
Curse of dimensionality
The set of problems that appear when the number of features is large. Distances become less informative and data becomes sparse.
Overfitting
Following noise so closely that new predictions are poor. Small k can overfit.
Underfitting
Using a model too simple to capture the pattern. Very large k can underfit.
Feature scaling
Rescaling features so that they are comparable in size before measuring distances.

Distance measures

Why we need a distance

KNN depends entirely on the word "near". Near in what sense? A distance gives us a number for every pair of points. We then sort the training points from nearest to farthest. The choice of distance can change which points are nearest, so it matters.

Euclidean distance

Take two points, a and b, each with two features. Point a is (a1, a2) and point b is (b1, b2). The Euclidean distance is the length of the straight line between them. Pythagoras gives the formula.

dE(a, b) = √( (a1 minus b1)² + (a2 minus b2)² )

Read it. Each difference is how far apart the two points are along one feature. Squaring removes signs. Adding and taking the square root gives the straight-line length. With more features, add one more squared difference inside the root for each feature.

Worked example: Euclidean

Find the distance between a = (1, 2) and b = (4, 6).

  1. Differences. 1 minus 4 = minus 3. 2 minus 6 = minus 4.
  2. Square them. (minus 3)² = 9. (minus 4)² = 16.
  3. Add. 9 + 16 = 25.
  4. Take the square root. √25 = 5.

The straight-line distance is 5 units.

Manhattan distance

Now picture a taxi driving through a city laid out on a grid. It cannot cut through buildings. It must travel along the streets, first east or west, then north or south. The Manhattan distance measures this trip. It adds the absolute differences.

dM(a, b) = |a1 minus b1| + |a2 minus b2|

The bars | | mean absolute value, which removes the minus sign. For the same points, the Manhattan distance is 3 + 4 = 7. It is always at least as large as the Euclidean distance.

TipEuclidean distance is the default. Manhattan is useful when moving along each feature separately makes sense, such as blocks in a city, or when you want a measure less influenced by one large difference.

Feature scaling matters

Suppose one feature is height in centimetres (values around 170) and another is shoe size (values around 8). The height difference of 10 centimetres dominates the distance, while a shoe size difference of 2 barely counts. The distance then ignores shoe size almost completely, even if shoe size is informative. Rescale each feature first, for example by subtracting its mean and dividing by its standard deviation, so that each feature has a similar range.

TrapNever compute KNN distances on raw features with very different units without scaling. The result depends on the units, not on the meaning of the data.

Question: what is the Euclidean distance between (0, 0) and (3, 4)?

√(9 + 16) = √25 = 5.

Question: which distance is always larger, Manhattan or Euclidean?

Manhattan is never smaller than Euclidean and is usually larger. The two are equal only when the points differ along a single feature.

Classifying and predicting

Step 1: measure every distance

Suppose we have six training points, each with two features and a colour label. The query point is q = (2, 1). We want the label of q using k = 3.

PointFeature 1Feature 2Label
A11red
B32blue
C55blue
D13red
E61red
F46blue

The Euclidean distances from q = (2, 1) are:

  • A: differences 1 and 0, distance √(1 + 0) = 1.
  • B: differences 1 and 1, distance √(1 + 1) = √2, about 1.414.
  • C: differences 3 and 4, distance √(9 + 16) = 5.
  • D: differences 1 and 2, distance √(1 + 4) = √5, about 2.236.
  • E: differences 4 and 0, distance √(16 + 0) = 4.
  • F: differences 2 and 5, distance √(4 + 25) = √29, about 5.385.

Step 2: pick the k nearest

Sort by distance: A (1), B (1.414), D (2.236), E (4), C (5), F (5.385). The three nearest are A, B and D.

Step 3: vote

A is red, B is blue, D is red. Two votes for red and one for blue, so the prediction is red. The majority wins.

Key ideaThe label is decided by the local neighbourhood of the query, not by a global formula. Move the query a little and the neighbours can change, and so can the answer.

KNN for regression

For a numerical output, we take the average of the k neighbours' values instead of a vote. Suppose the three nearest neighbours have house prices 10, 14 and 18 (in lakh). The predicted price is (10 + 14 + 18)/3 = 42/3 = 14. The average is a sensible guess when nearby points have similar values.

Why ties need care

With two classes and an even k, the vote can tie, for example one red and one blue with k = 2. For binary problems choose an odd k to avoid ties. With several classes, ties can still happen and need a rule, such as choosing the nearest of the tied classes.

Question: with k = 1, what is the prediction for q above?

The nearest point is A, which is red. So the prediction is red, from one neighbour alone.

Question: why do we pick k from data, not from the rule that gives smallest training error?

With k = 1 each training point is its own nearest neighbour, so the training error is always zero. That tells you nothing about new data. We choose k by testing on data the model has not seen, as explained below.

Choosing k and the bias-variance trade-off

What k controls

The number k decides how local the prediction is. A small k, such as 1, makes the prediction follow every single training point. A large k averages over many points and smooths everything out. The best k sits between these extremes.

  • k = 1. The boundary between classes is jagged. A single noisy point can create a small island of the wrong class. This is overfitting. The bias is low, because the model follows the data closely, but the variance is high, because a different training set would change the boundary a lot.
  • Medium k. The boundary is smoother, and noise is averaged out. Usually this gives the best balance.
  • k equal to the number of training points. Every query gets all the training points as neighbours. For classification, the prediction is simply the most common class in the whole training set. This is underfitting, with high bias and low variance.

Key ideaSmall k gives low bias and high variance. Large k gives high bias and low variance. Choose k in the middle using validation data, often with cross-validation as in the ridge topic.

How to choose k in practice

  1. Split the data into training and validation sets, or use k-fold cross-validation.
  2. Try several values of k, for example 1, 3, 5, 7, 9.
  3. For each k, measure the error on the validation data.
  4. Pick the k with the lowest validation error, preferring a smaller k when the errors are about the same.

A small check on the effect of k

Use the same query q = (2, 1) from the earlier table. With k = 1 the answer is red, from point A. With k = 3 it is red, from A, B and D. With k = 5 the neighbours are A, B, D, E and C, which are red, blue, red, red and blue. The votes are three red and two blue, so the answer is still red. Here k changed the set of neighbours but not the answer. In other examples a change in k flips the class.

Question: a model with k = 1 has zero training error. Is it a good model?

Not necessarily. Zero training error is expected because each training point is its own nearest neighbour. Validation or test error is the real measure of quality.

Question: why is an odd k common for two classes?

An odd number of voters cannot split exactly in half, so there is always a winner.

Curse of dimensionality and lazy learning

What happens in many dimensions

Think of searching for a friend in a town. On a single street, a few people are close to you. On a flat map, the circle of nearby people is bigger. In a building with twenty floors, the nearby region becomes very thin and sparse. As we add features, the space grows enormously, and the training points spread out thinly. This is the curse of dimensionality.

A concrete example shows the effect. Suppose data is spread evenly in a unit cube, a square in two dimensions or a cube in three dimensions, and so on. We want a small sub-cube around the query point that holds 10 percent of the data. In one dimension that sub-cube has side 0.1, which is 10 percent of the range. In d dimensions its side must be s, where sd = 0.1, so s = 0.1(1/d).

  • In 1 dimension, s = 0.1. A tiny interval.
  • In 2 dimensions, s = 0.10.5 ≈ 0.316. Almost a third of each axis.
  • In 10 dimensions, s = 0.10.1 ≈ 0.794. Almost 80 percent of each axis.

In 10 dimensions, the "nearby" points are no longer near in any useful sense. A neighbourhood that holds 10 percent of the data stretches across nearly the whole range of every feature. The neighbours are not really similar to the query, and KNN loses its power. The fix is more data, fewer features, or dimension reduction methods such as principal component analysis (PCA).

GATE loves thisWhen a question asks why KNN fails with many features, the answer is the curse of dimensionality: distances become less informative and data becomes sparse. Feature selection or dimension reduction is the usual remedy.

Lazy learning and its cost

A lazy learner, such as KNN, has almost no training step. It just stores the data. All the work happens at prediction time. For each query it must measure the distance to every stored point. That makes prediction slow for large data sets and uses a lot of memory.

An eager learner, such as logistic regression, does the work once. It fits the weights during training. Prediction is then fast, because it only computes a weighted sum. Choosing between eager and lazy learning is a trade-off between training cost and prediction cost, and between memory and speed.

FeatureKNN (lazy)Logistic regression (eager)
Training stepNone, just store the dataFit the weights by iterative steps
Prediction costCompute distances to all stored pointsOne weighted sum
MemoryMust keep all training dataKeeps only the weights
Shape of boundaryCan be very irregularAlways linear in the inputs

import math

points = {"A": ((1, 1), "red"), "B": ((3, 2), "blue"), "C": ((5, 5), "blue"),
          "D": ((1, 3), "red"), "E": ((6, 1), "red"), "F": ((4, 6), "blue")}
q = (2, 1)

def euclid(a, b):
    return math.sqrt(sum((x - y) ** 2 for x, y in zip(a, b)))

def manhattan(a, b):
    return sum(abs(x - y) for x, y in zip(a, b))

print("Euclid (1,2) to (4,6) =", euclid((1, 2), (4, 6)))
print("Manhattan (1,2) to (4,6) =", manhattan((1, 2), (4, 6)))

ranked = sorted(points.items(), key=lambda item: euclid(item[1][0], q))
for name, (p, label) in ranked:
    print(name, label, round(euclid(p, q), 3))

k = 3
votes = [label for name, (p, label) in ranked[:k]]
print("k =", k, "votes:", votes, "prediction:", max(set(votes), key=votes.count))

print("regression average of 10, 14, 18 =", (10 + 14 + 18) / 3)
print("side length for 10 percent in 10 dimensions =", round(0.1 ** (1 / 10), 3))

Straight-line distance (NAT)

Find the Euclidean distance between the points (2, 3) and (5, 7). (NAT)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. One straight-line distance.
  2. Which idea applies. Euclidean distance is the square root of the sum of squared differences.
  3. Work it out. The differences are 5 minus 2 = 3 and 7 minus 3 = 4. Squares: 9 and 16. Sum: 25. Square root: 5.
  4. Check. A 3-4-5 triangle is a classic right triangle, so 5 is correct.

Answer: 5

Facts about KNN (MSQ)

Which statements about K-nearest neighbour are true? Select all that apply.

(A) KNN is a lazy learner, because it stores the data and works at prediction time.

(B) A small k tends to give high variance.

(C) KNN never needs feature scaling.

(D) The decision boundary of KNN is always a straight line.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which facts about KNN are right.
  2. Which idea applies. Lazy learning, the bias-variance trade-off, and the role of distances.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true. (B) is true, because a small k follows noise. (C) is false, because raw features with different units distort distances. (D) is false, because KNN boundaries can be very irregular.
  4. Check. The false statements are the ones that deny distances depend on units or that give KNN a neat linear boundary.

Answer: (A) and (B)

Manhattan distance in three dimensions (NAT)

Find the Manhattan distance between the points (2, minus 1, 3) and (5, 1, 0). (NAT)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The city-block distance across three features.
  2. Which idea applies. Add the absolute differences of the features.
  3. Work it out. |2 minus 5| = 3. |minus 1 minus 1| = |minus 2| = 2. |3 minus 0| = 3. The sum is 3 + 2 + 3 = 8.
  4. Check. The Euclidean distance would be √(9 + 4 + 9) = √22, about 4.69, which is smaller than 8. This matches the rule that Manhattan is the larger one.

Answer: 8

Majority vote with k = 3 (MCQ)

Training points, with labels: P = (1, 0) green, Q = (0, 3) red, R = (2, 2) red, S = (4, 0) green, T = (minus 1, minus 1) red. A new point is (0, 0). Using Euclidean distance and k = 3, which label is predicted? Select the single correct option.

(A) green (B) red

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The label of (0, 0) from its three nearest points.
  2. Which idea applies. Compute distances, sort, take the k nearest, then vote.
  3. Work it out. Distances: P = 1, T = √2 ≈ 1.414, R = √8 ≈ 2.828, Q = 3, S = 4. The three nearest are P (green), T (red) and R (red). Votes: red 2, green 1.
  4. Check. With k = 1 the answer would be green, from P alone. The change shows how sensitive small k can be.

Answer: (B) red

Averaging the neighbours (NAT)

A KNN regression uses k = 4. The four nearest neighbours have output values 8, 10, 12 and 14. Find the predicted value. (NAT)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The predicted number for a regression query.
  2. Which idea applies. The prediction is the average of the k neighbours' outputs.
  3. Work it out. The sum is 8 + 10 + 12 + 14 = 44. Divide by 4 to get 11.
  4. Check. The answer lies between the smallest and largest neighbour values, as an average must.

Answer: 11

Sub-cube in four dimensions (NAT)

Points are spread evenly in a unit hypercube with d = 4 features. We want a small sub-cube around a query that contains 1 percent of the points. Find the side length of this sub-cube, rounded to three decimal places. (NAT)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The side s of a cube in 4 dimensions whose volume is 1 percent of the unit cube.
  2. Which idea applies. In d dimensions, sd = c, so s = c1/d.
  3. Work it out. c = 0.01 and d = 4. So s = 0.010.25. Since 0.01 = 0.1², the value is 0.10.5 = √0.1, about 0.316.
  4. Check. Raise 0.316 to the power 4: 0.316² is about 0.0999, and squaring again gives about 0.00997, close to 0.01.

Answer: 0.316

Distances, scaling and dimensions (MSQ)

A KNN classifier uses two features: height in centimetres and weight in grams. Select all true statements.

(A) Without scaling, the weight feature in grams can dominate the distances.

(B) With two classes and k = 2, a tie is impossible.

(C) A very large k can underfit the data.

(D) As the number of dimensions grows, the nearest and farthest distances from a query become relatively similar.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements about scaling, ties, k and dimensions are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. Units change distances. Even k allows ties. Large k smooths too much. In high dimensions distances concentrate.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true: grams have much larger numbers than centimetres, so the weight difference swamps the height difference. (B) is false: with k = 2 one vote can go each way. (C) is true: with huge k the prediction is almost always the overall majority. (D) is true: this is the distance concentration part of the curse of dimensionality.
  4. Check. The false statement mentions the tie, which needs an even k with two classes.

Answer: (A), (C) and (D)

Exam-level 1: Count the label 1 neighbours (NAT, 2 marks)

A k nearest neighbour classifier uses Euclidean distance and k = 3. The training set has six labelled points: (0, 0) with label 0, (3, 2) with label 1, (2, 4) with label 1, (5, 5) with label 0, (1, 3) with label 0, and (4, 1) with label 1. A new point is at (2, 2). Compute the distance from the new point to every training point, keep the three closest, and count how many of them have label 1. (NAT)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A count of label 1 points among the three nearest neighbours.
  2. Which idea applies. Euclidean distance is the square root of the sum of squared differences. Rank the points by that distance, then take the top three.
  3. Work it out. (3, 2) is at distance 1. (1, 3) is at square root of 2, about 1.414. (2, 4) is at distance 2. (4, 1) is at square root of 5, about 2.236. (0, 0) is at square root of 8, about 2.828. (5, 5) is at square root of 18, about 4.243. The three closest are (3, 2) with label 1, (1, 3) with label 0, and (2, 4) with label 1. Two have label 1.
  4. Check. The point (1, 3) is closer than (2, 4), so it is in the top three. The fourth closest, (4, 1), is at 2.236, which is further than 2, so it is correctly left out.

Answer: 2

Exam-level 2: Which KNN facts hold (MSQ, 2 marks)

A team tunes a k nearest neighbour classifier on a dataset with numeric features measured in very different units. Which of the following statements are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) A very small k gives low bias and high variance.

(B) KNN is a lazy learner: training mostly stores the data, and the work is done when a prediction is made.

(C) With a very large k, the prediction tends toward the majority class of the training data.

(D) Euclidean distance is unaffected by feature scale, so standardising the features never changes which neighbours are chosen.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which of four statements about KNN are true.
  2. Which idea applies. Small k follows the training noise. Large k averages over many points. Distances depend directly on the units of each feature.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true: one nearest point decides the answer, so the model is flexible and noisy. (B) is true: there is no real training step, and each prediction searches the data. (C) is true: with many neighbours, the vote is dominated by the most common class. (D) is false: a feature in thousands and a feature in ones changes which points are close, so scaling can change the neighbours.
  4. Check. The code below shows one example where scaling changes the nearest neighbour, which confirms that (D) is wrong.

Answer: (A), (B) and (C). Option (D) is wrong because scaling changes Euclidean distances.

Exam-level 3: Edge length in ten dimensions (NAT, 2 marks)

Suppose data points are spread uniformly at random in a d-dimensional unit cube. A small sub-cube that is centred in the cube must have edge length 0.01 raised to the power 1/d to hold 1 percent of the points on average. Compare d = 10 with d = 2. Find the ratio of the edge length needed in 10 dimensions to the edge length needed in 2 dimensions. Give the ratio to two decimal places. (NAT)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A ratio of two edge lengths, which shows the curse of dimensionality.
  2. Which idea applies. A sub-cube holding a fraction f of the volume has edge f raised to the power 1/d.
  3. Work it out. In 10 dimensions the edge is 0.01 to the power 0.1, which is 10 to the power minus 0.2, about 0.631. In 2 dimensions the edge is 0.01 to the power 0.5, which is 0.1. The ratio is 0.631 divided by 0.1, which is about 6.31.
  4. Check. The edge in 10 dimensions is much larger than in 2 dimensions, so a KNN neighbourhood must reach far across each axis. That is why distance-based methods lose accuracy in high dimensions.

Answer: 6.31

Exam-level 4: Distance-weighted KNN prediction (NAT, 2 marks)

A k nearest neighbour regressor with k = 3 predicts a house price index for a new location. The three nearest training points have target values 10, 14 and 18, and they lie at distances 1, 2 and 4 from the new point. Compare two predictions: the plain average of the three target values, and an average weighted by inverse distance, where each weight is 1 divided by the distance. Find the inverse-distance weighted prediction to two decimal places. (NAT)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The weighted prediction, which depends on the distance weights.
  2. Which idea applies. The weighted average is the sum of weight times value, divided by the sum of weights.
  3. Work it out. The weights are 1, 1/2 and 1/4. The weighted sum is 10 times 1 plus 14 times 0.5 plus 18 times 0.25 = 10 + 7 + 4.5 = 21.5. The sum of weights is 1.75. So the prediction is 21.5/1.75, which is about 12.29.
  4. Check. The plain average is 14. The weighted prediction 12.29 is lower, because the nearest point has the lowest value 10 and gets the largest weight.

Answer: 12.29

Euclidean distance
The straight-line distance, the square root of summed squared differences.
Manhattan distance
The sum of absolute differences across features.
Hyperparameter k
The number of neighbours, chosen by validation.
Majority vote
The most common label among the neighbours, used for classification.
Average of neighbours
The mean of the neighbours' values, used for regression.
Lazy learner
A method that stores the data and does its work only at prediction time.
Eager learner
A method that fits a model during training, such as logistic regression.
Bias-variance trade-off in k
Small k gives low bias and high variance. Large k gives high bias and low variance.
Overfitting
Following noise so closely that new predictions are poor, as with k = 1.
Underfitting
Using a model too simple to capture the pattern, as with very large k.
Feature scaling
Rescaling features so that distances are not dominated by units.
Curse of dimensionality
Distances become less informative and data becomes sparse as features grow.
Distance concentration
In high dimensions, nearest and farthest points become relatively similar in distance.

Remember these

  • KNN keeps the training data and votes or averages among the k nearest points.
  • Euclidean distance is the default. Manhattan adds absolute differences.
  • Always scale features with different units before computing distances.
  • Small k follows noise. Large k smooths too much. Choose k with validation data.
  • Use an odd k for two classes to avoid ties.
  • In many dimensions, neighbourhoods stop being local and KNN weakens.

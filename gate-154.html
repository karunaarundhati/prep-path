Robot accuracy and repeatability

Accuracy is how close a robot gets to the position you asked for. Repeatability is how well it returns to the same position every time. The two are easy to mix up, and GATE often asks you to tell them apart and to name what affects each one.

The idea in plain words

Imagine an archer shooting five arrows at a target. Three things can happen.

  • The arrows land close to the bullseye, and close to each other. The archer is accurate and consistent.
  • The arrows land close to each other, but far from the bullseye. The archer is consistent but not accurate. The sight may be a little off.
  • The arrows land all around the bullseye, spread out. The archer is roughly right on average, but not consistent.

A robot is the same. You tell it to go to a point, which is the commanded position. It goes to a point in space, which is the actual position. Two questions matter:

  1. Accuracy: how close is the actual position to the commanded position?
  2. Repeatability: when the robot goes to the same commanded point again and again, how close together do the actual positions land?

Accuracy is about the distance from the target. Repeatability is about the spread of the group. In GATE RA, the most common question is to decide which of these a given situation describes, and what causes it.

Key ideaAccuracy compares the robot with the target. Repeatability compares the robot with itself.

Key terms

Read these now. Each one is used in the sections below.

Commanded position
The point the program asks the robot to reach. Precise definition: the target coordinates sent to the controller.
Actual position
Where the robot really ends up. Precise definition: the measured position of the tool centre point after the motion finishes.
Position error
The gap between the actual and commanded positions. Precise definition: the vector difference between actual and commanded position, with a size (magnitude) given by its length.
Accuracy
How close the robot gets to the commanded position. Precise definition: the closeness of the mean reached position to the commanded position, usually given as a distance in mm.
Repeatability
How close the reached positions are to each other over repeated trials to the same command. Precise definition: a measure of the spread of positions reached for the same command, usually given as a distance in mm.
Mean
The average of the values. Precise definition: the sum of the values divided by the number of values, written as x̄.
Standard deviation
A typical size of the spread around the mean. Precise definition: the square root of the average squared deviation from the mean (sample version divides by n − 1).
Calibration
Adjusting the robot's model so it matches the real machine. Precise definition: finding the true link lengths, joint offsets, and sensor reference so that the controller's calculated position matches the real position.
Kinematic error
A mistake in the model of the arm's geometry. Precise definition: a difference between the assumed and the real link lengths, joint angles, or joint offsets.
Backlash
Small free play in a gear train. Precise definition: the gap between meshing gear teeth that lets the output move slightly before the next tooth takes the load, so reversing direction gives a different position.
Encoder
A sensor that measures joint angle. Precise definition: a device that outputs a digital count proportional to the shaft angle.
Resolution
The smallest step the robot or sensor can measure or command. Precise definition: the smallest change in position that can be represented, set by the encoder counts and the gear ratio.
Compliance (deflection)
Bending or stretching under load. Precise definition: the amount a link or joint bends when a force or torque acts on it, for example because of the payload or gravity.
Thermal expansion
Growth or shrinkage caused by temperature change. Precise definition: the change in length of a material when its temperature changes, given by a coefficient times the length times the temperature change.

Accuracy: how close to the target

Accuracy is the simplest idea. You command a point. The robot goes to a point. How far apart are they? That gap is the position error.

In one dimension (a straight line) the error is just a subtraction:

e = xactual − xcommanded

Here e is the error, xactual is where the robot is, and xcommanded is where you asked it to go. Units are usually mm. A positive error means the robot went too far, and a negative error means it stopped short.

In two or three dimensions the error has several parts. We use the distance, which is found with Pythagoras:

|e| = √(dx² + dy² + dz²)

Here dx, dy, and dz are the differences along each axis. The vertical bars around e mean "size only", so the direction is removed.

Worked example. You command the tool to (300, 400) mm in a flat plane. The robot actually reaches (302, 398) mm.

  1. Find the differences. dx = 302 − 300 = 2 mm. dy = 398 − 400 = −2 mm.
  2. Square and add. dx² + dy² = 4 + 4 = 8 mm².
  3. Take the square root. |e| = √8 = 2.83 mm (rounded).
  4. Check. A 2 mm step along each axis makes a diagonal of about 2.83 mm, which matches the length of a square's diagonal (side × √2).
import math
target = (300.0, 400.0)
actual = (302.0, 398.0)
dx = actual[0] - target[0]
dy = actual[1] - target[1]
error = math.sqrt(dx**2 + dy**2)
print("dx, dy (mm):", dx, dy)
print("Position error (mm):", round(error, 2))
dx, dy (mm): 2.0 -2.0
Position error (mm): 2.83

What causes poor accuracy?

The robot's controller calculates joint angles from a model of the arm. If the model is wrong, the arm misses the target. The main causes are:

  • Kinematic model errors: the real link lengths differ from the numbers in the controller.
  • Joint zero offsets: the encoder's zero is not exactly where the model thinks it is.
  • Compliance: the arm bends under its own weight or the payload, so the tool sags.
  • Thermal expansion: the arm grows when warm, changing its geometry.
  • Gear errors and backlash: the real gear ratio or play differs from the assumed value.

TrapAn accuracy error can be large even when the robot is very precise. A robot can repeat an error exactly every time. Accuracy measures the error, not the consistency.

Question: A robot is commanded to x = 200.0 mm and stops at 200.4 mm. What is the signed accuracy error?

e = 200.4 − 200.0 = +0.4 mm. The robot went 0.4 mm too far.

Question: Name two causes of poor accuracy that are caused by the model rather than by the motors.

Wrong link lengths and wrong joint zero offsets in the controller's model.

Repeatability: how tight the group is

Now imagine you do not care where the robot is on average. You only care whether it lands in the same place each time. You send it to the same command ten times, and you record where it stops. The ten points form a small cloud. The size of that cloud is the repeatability.

A small cloud means good repeatability. A large cloud means poor repeatability, even if the centre of the cloud is right on the target. This is the key difference from accuracy.

Measuring repeatability step by step

Suppose the commanded position is 100.00 mm and the robot reaches five times at these positions: 100.2, 100.3, 100.1, 100.2, and 100.4 mm. The steps are:

  1. Mean. Add the five readings and divide by 5. The mean is 100.24 mm. The mean shows where the group is centred.
  2. Accuracy error. Mean minus target: 100.24 − 100.00 = 0.24 mm. This is the accuracy error.
  3. Spread. The largest reading minus the smallest: 100.4 − 100.1 = 0.3 mm. This is a simple spread number.
  4. Standard deviation. Measure each reading's distance from the mean, square it, add up, divide by n − 1 = 4, and take the square root. The result is about 0.114 mm.
s = √( Σ (xi − x̄)² / (n − 1) )

Here s is the sample standard deviation, xi is each reading, x̄ is the mean, n is the number of readings, and Σ means "add up over all readings".

A common rough way to express repeatability is about 3 times the standard deviation, so that most readings fall within that band. For our data, 3 × 0.114 = 0.34 mm. Be careful: exact definitions depend on the test standard, so for GATE, the safe reading is that repeatability is a spread measure, and you use the definition given in the question.

import statistics
target = 100.0
readings = [100.2, 100.3, 100.1, 100.2, 100.4]
mean = statistics.mean(readings)
accuracy_error = mean - target
spread = max(readings) - min(readings)
s = statistics.stdev(readings)
print("Mean position (mm):", round(mean, 3))
print("Accuracy error (mm):", round(accuracy_error, 3))
print("Spread max-min (mm):", round(spread, 3))
print("Std dev (mm):", round(s, 4))
print("3 x std dev (mm):", round(3 * s, 4))
Mean position (mm): 100.24
Accuracy error (mm): 0.24
Spread max-min (mm): 0.3
Std dev (mm): 0.114
3 x std dev (mm): 0.3421

TipAlways separate the two questions in your head. First ask, "is the mean near the target?" That is accuracy. Then ask, "are the readings close to each other?" That is repeatability.

What causes poor repeatability?

Repeatability is hurt by things that change from one trial to the next:

  • Backlash: the gear play can be taken up in a different way each time.
  • Friction: the joint may stick and then slip (stick-slip), so it stops at a slightly different point.
  • Vibration and control noise: small jitter at the end of each move.
  • Encoder resolution: the position can only be read in fixed steps.
  • Temperature drift: the arm changes size slowly during a shift.
Question: Five readings are 10.0, 10.0, 10.0, 10.0, and 10.0 mm, but the target was 10.5 mm. What is the repeatability and what is the accuracy error?

The spread is zero, so the repeatability is perfect. The accuracy error is 10.0 − 10.5 = −0.5 mm. This is the case of a robot that is consistent but not accurate.

Question: Why does the standard deviation use n − 1 instead of n for a small sample?

The mean is calculated from the same readings, so they are a little closer to it than the true spread would be. Dividing by n − 1 corrects that small bias for a sample.

Why repeatability is usually better than accuracy

In most industrial robots, repeatability is much better than accuracy. A typical industrial arm might repeat to within ±0.05 mm but be accurate only to about ±0.5 mm. Why is that?

The answer is that the two errors have different sources. Many accuracy errors are systematic. They are the same every time, so they push every reading in the same direction. Systematic errors are not random, so they do not spread the group. The arm is consistent, but consistently wrong.

Repeatability errors are mostly random, or at least change from trial to trial. Random errors spread the group out. They are smaller in good robots because the joints, gears, and controller are built to behave the same way each time.

Key ideaA systematic error can be fixed by calibration, because it is the same every time. A random error cannot be fixed by calibration, because it changes. This is why good robots are usually repeatable first, then calibrated for accuracy.

Fixing a repeatable but inaccurate robot

Suppose the robot always lands 0.24 mm past the command, as in the example in the previous section. The error is systematic. A simple fix is to command a point 0.24 mm before the desired point:

xcommand = xtarget − e = 100.00 − 0.24 = 99.76 mm

If the error stays the same, the robot will now reach about 100.00 mm. This is one reason that calibration (measuring the robot and correcting its model) improves accuracy so much while leaving repeatability almost unchanged.

TrapDo not assume a high repeatability means a high accuracy. The robot in the example can be perfectly repeatable at 10.0 mm while the target is 10.5 mm. The repeatability says nothing about the 0.5 mm miss.

Question: A robot repeats to ±0.02 mm but its accuracy is ±0.6 mm. What is the most likely cause of the poor accuracy?

A systematic model error, such as wrong link lengths, joint offsets, or an unmodelled load bending. These errors are the same every time, so they are removed by calibration.

Question: Which error is easier to correct in software, a systematic error or a random one?

A systematic error, because it is predictable. A random error changes each time, so no fixed offset removes it.

Resolution is not accuracy

A common mistake is to think a robot with a very fine encoder must be accurate. That is not true. Resolution is the smallest step the robot can measure or command. Accuracy is how close the result is to the target. A robot can have fine steps and still miss the target by a large amount.

Think of a ruler with 0.001 mm markings but a bent frame. The markings are very fine, but if the frame is bent, all your measurements are off. Fine resolution is necessary for good accuracy, but it is not enough.

Working out the resolution of a joint

Suppose a rotary joint has a resolution of 0.01 degree, and the arm length from the joint to the tool is 0.8 m. The smallest tip movement from one count is roughly:

Δs = L × Δθ

Here L is the arm length in metres and Δθ is the smallest angle step in radians. Radians are needed because the arc length formula uses them: one full turn is 2π radians, which is 360 degrees.

import math
resolution_deg = 0.01
arm_length_m = 0.8
theta = math.radians(resolution_deg)
step = arm_length_m * theta
print("Angle step (rad):", round(theta, 7))
print("Tip step (mm):", round(step * 1000, 3))
Angle step (rad): 0.0001745
Tip step (mm): 0.14

So the tip can only move in steps of about 0.14 mm from this joint alone. A repeatability better than this step is not possible with a simple count-based read. Accuracy depends on many other errors as well.

GATE loves thisQuestions often give a resolution, a repeatability, and an accuracy together, and ask which is which. Resolution is a step size, repeatability is a spread, and accuracy is an offset from the target.

Question: A robot has a resolution of 0.1 mm but an accuracy of 1 mm. Can the robot be accurate to 0.1 mm?

Not from resolution alone. The resolution is the smallest step, but the position error is 1 mm, so the accuracy is 1 mm. Better resolution does not fix the model or the bending.

Measuring both from data

In a lab test, you send the robot to the same command many times, measure each final position, and then compute the two numbers. The method is:

  1. Compute the mean of the readings. This gives the centre of the group.
  2. Compare the mean with the target. The difference is the accuracy error.
  3. Compute the spread, for example the standard deviation. This gives the repeatability.

Worked example. The commanded position is 50.00 mm. Six readings are 50.04, 49.97, 50.02, 50.05, 49.99, and 50.01 mm. The Python check below finds the mean error and the spread. The mean is 50.0133 mm, so the accuracy error is 0.0133 mm, which is very small. The standard deviation is 0.0301 mm, which means the group is tight.

import statistics
target = 50.0
readings = [50.04, 49.97, 50.02, 50.05, 49.99, 50.01]
mean = statistics.mean(readings)
s = statistics.stdev(readings)
print("Mean (mm):", round(mean, 4))
print("Accuracy error (mm):", round(mean - target, 4))
print("Std dev (mm):", round(s, 4))
print("3 x std dev (mm):", round(3 * s, 4))
Mean (mm): 50.0133
Accuracy error (mm): 0.0133
Std dev (mm): 0.0301
3 x std dev (mm): 0.0903

TrapStudents often use the target instead of the mean when computing repeatability. Repeatability uses the spread around the mean, not the spread around the target.

Question: In the example, what would the accuracy error be if you forgot the mean and used the first reading only?

The first reading is 50.04, so the error would look like 0.04 mm. That is a wrong method. Always use the mean of many readings to judge accuracy.

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original GATE-style practice problems, not real past exam questions. Attempt each one before reading the method.

Accuracy error at one point

A robot is commanded to x = 480.0 mm. It stops at x = 479.7 mm. What is the signed accuracy error, in mm?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The signed error e in mm.
  2. Which idea applies. e = xactual − xcommanded.
  3. Work it out. e = 479.7 − 480.0 = −0.3 mm.
  4. Check. The robot stopped short, and a negative sign says so.

Answer: −0.3 mm (the robot stopped 0.3 mm short).

Which word fits?

A robot always reaches 0.5 mm past the target, to within 0.01 mm each time. Which pair of statements is correct?

(A) Good accuracy, poor repeatability   (B) Poor accuracy, good repeatability   (C) Poor accuracy, poor repeatability   (D) Good accuracy, good repeatability

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which combination of accuracy and repeatability fits.
  2. Which idea applies. The 0.5 mm offset is a miss from the target, so it is an accuracy error. The 0.01 mm spread is small, so repeatability is good.
  3. Work it out. Poor accuracy (0.5 mm miss) and good repeatability (0.01 mm spread) is option (B).
  4. Check. Each word matches one half of the situation.

Answer: (B).

Mean error and spread from five trials

The commanded position is 250.0 mm. Five trials give 250.3, 250.4, 250.2, 250.5, and 250.3 mm. Find the mean position, the accuracy error, and the max-minus-min spread.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Three numbers: mean, accuracy error, and spread.
  2. Which idea applies. Mean is the sum divided by the count. Accuracy error is mean minus target. Spread is largest minus smallest.
  3. Work it out. Sum = 250.3 + 250.4 + 250.2 + 250.5 + 250.3 = 1251.7. Mean = 1251.7 / 5 = 250.34 mm. Accuracy error = 250.34 − 250.0 = 0.34 mm. Spread = 250.5 − 250.2 = 0.3 mm.
  4. Check. The mean lies between the smallest (250.2) and the largest (250.5) readings, as it must.
import statistics
target = 250.0
readings = [250.3, 250.4, 250.2, 250.5, 250.3]
mean = statistics.mean(readings)
print("Mean position (mm):", round(mean, 2))
print("Accuracy error (mm):", round(mean - target, 2))
print("Spread max-min (mm):", round(max(readings) - min(readings), 2))
Mean position (mm): 250.34
Accuracy error (mm): 0.34
Spread max-min (mm): 0.3

Answer: mean 250.34 mm, accuracy error 0.34 mm, spread 0.3 mm.

Which statements are true? (select all that apply)

Select all the statements that are correct.

(A) Repeatability can be good even when accuracy is poor.   (B) Accuracy is measured from the spread of repeated readings alone.   (C) Backlash is a common cause of poor repeatability.   (D) Calibration of link lengths mainly improves accuracy.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. Accuracy compares the mean with the target. Repeatability compares readings with each other. Backlash makes positions vary. Calibration fixes model errors.
  3. Work it out. (A) True, a robot can be consistently off. (B) False, accuracy needs the target. (C) True, backlash varies with direction and load. (D) True, wrong link lengths are systematic errors.
  4. Check. Statement (B) confuses the two words. That is the trap.

Answer: (A), (C) and (D).

Position error in two dimensions

The commanded position is (500, 300) mm in a plane. The robot reaches (503, 304) mm. Find the size of the position error in mm, to two decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The error size |e| in mm.
  2. Which idea applies. dx and dy first, then |e| = √(dx² + dy²).
  3. Work it out. dx = 3, dy = 4. dx² + dy² = 9 + 16 = 25. |e| = √25 = 5.00 mm.
  4. Check. A 3-4-5 triangle is a well known right triangle, so the answer 5 is right.

Answer: 5.00 mm.

Joint resolution and tip step

A rotary joint has an angle resolution of 0.005 degree. The distance from the joint axis to the tool is 1.2 m. Find the smallest tip step caused by this joint alone, in mm, to two decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The tip step in mm.
  2. Which idea applies. Δs = L × Δθ, with Δθ in radians.
  3. Work it out. Δθ = 0.005 × π / 180 = 0.00008727 rad. Δs = 1.2 × 0.00008727 = 0.00010472 m = 0.10 mm.
  4. Check. Halving the resolution from the 0.01 degree example (which gave 0.14 mm for 0.8 m) gives 0.07 mm for 0.8 m. Scaling to 1.2 m multiplies by 1.5, which gives about 0.10 mm. This matches.
import math
theta = math.radians(0.005)
step = 1.2 * theta
print("Angle step (rad):", round(theta, 8))
print("Tip step (mm):", round(step * 1000, 2))
Angle step (rad): 8.727e-05
Tip step (mm): 0.1

Answer: about 0.10 mm.

Sources of accuracy error (select all that apply)

Which of the following are sources of poor accuracy, for a robot that is otherwise repeatable? Select all that apply.

(A) The link length in the controller's model is 2 mm too short.   (B) A joint encoder zero is off by a fixed angle.   (C) Random jitter of 0.02 mm at each move.   (D) A tool offset of 15 mm is not entered in the program.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which causes are accuracy errors.
  2. Which idea applies. Accuracy errors are systematic (the same every time) and come from the model. Random jitter spreads the readings, so it is a repeatability effect.
  3. Work it out. (A) Systematic model error: yes. (B) Fixed zero offset: yes, same every time. (C) Random jitter: a repeatability effect, not a main accuracy effect. (D) Missing tool offset: a fixed error in the TCP, so yes.
  4. Check. The answers are the errors that repeat in the same direction each time.

Answer: (A), (B) and (D).

Standard deviation and a rough repeatability band

The commanded position is 50.00 mm. Six readings are 50.04, 49.97, 50.02, 50.05, 49.99, and 50.01 mm. Find the mean accuracy error in mm and the sample standard deviation in mm, both to four decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The accuracy error and the sample standard deviation.
  2. Which idea applies. Accuracy error = mean − target. Sample standard deviation divides by n − 1.
  3. Work it out. The mean is 50.0133 mm, so the error is 0.0133 mm. The deviations from the mean are small, and the sum of their squares divided by 5 gives the variance. The square root is 0.0301 mm.
  4. Check. The mean sits within the readings (49.97 to 50.05), and the standard deviation is much smaller than the spread of 0.08 mm.

Answer: accuracy error 0.0133 mm, standard deviation 0.0301 mm. (The worked example above shows the Python check.)

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Accuracy and repeatability (NAT, 2 marks)

A robot is commanded to the point x = 100 mm ten times. The mean of the reached positions is 100.3 mm and the spread is ±0.05 mm (3σ). Find the accuracy error and the repeatability.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Two numbers.
  2. Which idea applies. Accuracy is the offset of the mean from the target. Repeatability is the spread about the mean.
  3. Work it out. Accuracy error 0.3 mm. Repeatability ±0.05 mm.
  4. Check. Calibration can fix the offset, not the spread.

Answer: 0.3 mm and ±0.05 mm.

Exam-level 2: Control resolution (NAT, 2 marks)

A linear axis of travel 1 m is controlled by a 12 bit encoder over the full range. Find the control resolution in mm, correct to 3 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Travel divided by the number of increments.
  2. Which idea applies. A 12 bit counter has 212 − 1 = 4095 increments.
  3. Work it out. 1000/4095 = 0.244 mm.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
print("%.3f mm" % (1000 / (2 ** 12 - 1)))

0.244 mm

Answer: about 0.244 mm.

Exam-level 3: Spatial resolution (NAT, 2 marks)

With the control resolution above and mechanical errors with σ = 0.02 mm, find the accuracy using the rule accuracy = CR/2 + 3σ, in mm.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The accuracy figure.
  2. Which idea applies. Use the given rule.
  3. Work it out. 0.244/2 + 0.06 = 0.182 mm.
  4. Check. Repeatability by the same rule is ±3σ = ±0.06 mm.

Answer: about 0.182 mm.

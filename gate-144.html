Trusses and frames

A truss is a frame of straight members joined at pins, and it carries loads well because each member is pulled or pushed along its length. Engineers find each member force with the method of joints or the method of sections. GATE asks for these methods, zero-force members and the difference between determinate and indeterminate structures.

The idea in plain words

Hold a long thin ruler at both ends and press down in the middle. It bends a lot. Now build the same length from many short pieces joined into triangles. The triangles stiffen the whole shape, and each piece now carries a push or a pull along its own length, instead of bending. That is the idea behind a truss.

Everyday analogy. Think of a bicycle wheel. The spokes are thin, but they hold the rim in a stiff circle, because each spoke only pulls. The hub, the rim and the spokes together are a truss of sorts. A roof truss, a bridge and a crane arm all rely on the same effect.

Why it matters. A truss is light, strong and easy to analyse, because the members carry only axial forces. An engineer who knows each member force can choose the right size of steel bar for each member and save material.

Where it appears in GATE. In the Robotics and Automation (RA) paper, expect numerical questions that ask for the force in one member, the reactions of a truss, or the number of zero-force members. Conceptual questions ask you to compare the method of joints and the method of sections, or to count the degree of indeterminacy.

Key terms

Truss
A structure made of straight members joined at their ends by pins, and loaded only at the joints.
Member
One straight piece of a truss, between two joints.
Joint (node)
A point where members meet, usually a pin.
Two-force member
A member loaded only at its two ends. Its forces are equal, opposite and along the member.
Axial force
A force along the length of a member. Tension pulls the ends apart, and compression pushes them together.
Tension
An axial force that stretches a member. By convention, a tensile force is positive in this page.
Compression
An axial force that squeezes a member. By convention, a compressive force is negative in this page.
Zero-force member
A member that carries no force for the given loading. It may still matter for stability or for other loads.
Method of joints
A way of finding member forces by writing equilibrium at each pin in turn.
Method of sections
A way of finding member forces by cutting the truss, taking one part as a free body, and applying equilibrium to it.
Frame
A structure in which some members carry bending and forces at angles, so they are not all two-force members.
Multi-force member
A member loaded at more than two points, or with a force at a point between its ends. It needs its own free-body diagram.
Statically determinate
A structure whose unknown forces can all be found from equilibrium equations alone.
Statically indeterminate
A structure with more unknowns than equilibrium equations. Extra information about deformation is needed.
Degree of indeterminacy
The number of extra unknowns. Precisely: m + r - 2j, where m is members, r is reaction unknowns and j is joints.

What a truss is

The ideal truss

Textbooks use an idealised truss with three simple rules:

  • Members are straight and joined by frictionless pins at their ends.
  • All loads act at the joints, not along the middle of a member.
  • Members are two-force members, so each carries only an axial force.

Because of these rules, the force in any member is either a tension or a compression, and it is the same at both ends. This simplicity is why trusses are so common in exam problems.

A simple triangle

The smallest truss is a triangle. Three members and three joints form a rigid shape. Push on any joint and the triangle does not change shape, because each side resists being stretched or squeezed.

                C  (apex, load P down here)
               / \
              /   \
             /     \
            /       \
           A ------- B
     (pin)          (roller)

   Members: AB (bottom chord), AC and BC (inclined)
Question: why does a truss member carry only axial force under the ideal rules?

Its ends are pinned, so the pins cannot transmit a turning effect, and the loads act only at joints. A two-force member carries only forces along its line, which is axial.

Question: a triangle is rigid, but a four-sided shape is not. Why?

A triangle has fixed side lengths, so its shape cannot change without breaking a member. A four-sided shape with pinned corners can collapse into a parallelogram without changing its side lengths.

Counting members, joints and reactions

The counting rule

Before you solve a truss, count the unknowns and the equations. Each joint gives two equations (ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0). Each member gives one unknown, its axial force. The supports add their own reaction unknowns.

unknowns = m + r, equations = 2j

Here m is the number of members, r is the number of reaction unknowns, and j is the number of joints. A pin gives two reactions, a roller gives one, and a fixed support gives three.

  • If m + r = 2j, the truss is statically determinate, and equilibrium alone solves it.
  • If m + r > 2j, the truss is statically indeterminate.
  • If m + r < 2j, the truss does not have enough members or supports to be stable.

Worked example: the triangle

The triangle has m = 3 members, j = 3 joints, and r = 3 reactions (a pin at A and a roller at B). Check the counts:

  1. Unknowns: m + r = 3 + 3 = 6.
  2. Equations: 2j = 2 × 3 = 6.
  3. Since 6 = 6, the triangle is statically determinate.
def degree(m, j, r):
    return m + r - 2 * j

print("Triangle degree = %d" % degree(3, 3, 3))
print("Four-member truss degree = %d" % degree(4, 3, 3))
Triangle degree = 0
Four-member truss degree = 1

A degree of zero means the truss is determinate. A degree of 1 means one extra member or support is present, so equilibrium alone cannot find every force.

Question: a truss has 9 members, 6 joints and 3 reactions. Is it determinate?

m + r = 9 + 3 = 12, and 2j = 12. The degree is 0, so the truss is statically determinate.

Question: what is the degree of indeterminacy of a truss with 10 members, 6 joints and 3 reactions?

10 + 3 - 12 = 1. The truss is indeterminate to the first degree.

The method of joints

The idea

Every joint in a truss is a pin in equilibrium. If you draw one joint with all the forces on it, you get two equations, ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0. If you solve the joints one at a time, starting with a joint that has only two unknown members, you find every member force.

Use this sign rule. Assume every member force is tension, meaning it pulls the joint away from the member. A negative answer means the member is in compression.

The steps

  1. Find the support reactions first, using the whole truss.
  2. Pick a joint with at most two unknown member forces.
  3. Draw that joint alone, with the external loads, the reactions and the member forces, all shown as tension.
  4. Resolve each force into x and y components.
  5. Write ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0, and solve.
  6. Move to the next joint, using the forces you have just found.

Worked example: the triangle truss

The triangle has a pin at A, a roller at B, and a load P = 10 kN down at the apex C. The span AB is 4 m and the height is 3 m, so the apex is directly above the middle of AB. Find all three member forces.

  1. Reactions: the load is symmetric, so RA = RB = 10 / 2 = 5 kN, both upward.
  2. Length of AC: √(2² + 3²) = √13 = 3.606 m.
  3. Joint A, vertical: 5 + FAC × (3 / 3.606) = 0, so FAC = -5 × 3.606 / 3 = -6.01 kN. The minus sign means compression.
  4. Joint A, horizontal: FAB + FAC × (2 / 3.606) = 0, so FAB = -(-6.01) × 0.5547 = 3.33 kN, a tension.
  5. By symmetry, FBC = FAC = -6.01 kN.
import math

P = 10.0             # load at the apex C, in kN, downward
span = 4.0           # length AB in metres
height = 3.0         # height of C above AB in metres

R_A = P / 2          # symmetric supports, each takes half
L_AC = math.sqrt((span / 2) ** 2 + height ** 2)
sin_t = height / L_AC
cos_t = (span / 2) / L_AC

F_AC = -R_A / sin_t          # joint A, vertical balance
F_AB = -F_AC * cos_t         # joint A, horizontal balance

print("Length AC = %.3f m" % L_AC)
print("Force in AC = %.2f kN" % F_AC)
print("Force in AB = %.2f kN" % F_AB)
Length AC = 3.606 m
Force in AC = -6.01 kN
Force in AB = 3.33 kN

Checking at joint C

The apex C has the load and two inclined members. Their vertical parts must balance the load. Each member is in compression, so each pushes C upward. The vertical part of each push is 6.01 × 3 / 3.606 = 5.00 kN. Two of them give 10.00 kN upward, which balances the 10 kN load. Good.

TrapWhen you draw a member at a joint, its force points away from the joint if it is tension, and toward the joint if it is compression. Draw all forces as tension first. A negative answer then tells you the truth, and you do not need to guess the sign.

Question: if the apex load is 12 kN on the same truss, what is the force in AB?

RA = 6 kN. The ratio FAB / RA = cos θ / sin θ = 2 / 3, so FAB = 6 × 2 / 3 = 4 kN, a tension.

Question: why do we start the method of joints with a joint that has only two unknowns?

A joint gives only two equations. With two unknowns you can solve them directly. A joint with three or more unknowns cannot be solved until another joint has been solved.

Zero-force members

Spotting them quickly

A zero-force member carries no force under a given load. Finding them early saves work. Two rules cover most cases, and both apply only at joints that have no external load.

  • Rule 1. If two members meet at an unloaded joint, and they are not in a straight line, both members are zero-force members.
  • Rule 2. If three members meet at an unloaded joint, and two of them are in a straight line, the third member is a zero-force member. The two in-line members can push or pull only along their own line, so nothing can balance a force along the third member. The third member must carry zero force.
Rule 1: unloaded joint, two members not in line

          |
          |   (member 1 is vertical)
          |
   ------+------  (member 2 is horizontal)
          joint with no load

   Both members carry zero force.

Rule 2: unloaded joint, three members, two in line

   ------+------ (members 1 and 2 in line)
          |
          |  (member 3 is vertical)

   Member 3 carries zero force. Members 1 and 2 carry equal forces.

Key ideaA zero-force member is not useless. It may carry a force under a different load, or it may keep the structure from collapsing when a load acts elsewhere. Only the given loading decides whether it is zero.

Question: an unloaded joint has three members at 0, 90 and 180 degrees. Which member is zero-force?

The members at 0 and 180 degrees are in line, so the member at 90 degrees is zero-force. Rule 2 applies.

Question: can a zero-force member be removed from a truss?

Not always. It may be needed for stability, or it may carry load under another case. Remove it only if you know the truss will still be stable and the other loads are not a concern.

The method of sections

When it is faster

The method of joints gives every member force, but it is slow when you need only one or two. The method of sections is faster for that case. You cut the truss with an imaginary line through the member you want, separate the truss into two parts, and take one part as a free body.

The steps

  1. Find the support reactions if you need them.
  2. Cut through the members of interest. Cut no more than three members with unknown forces, so that the three equations can solve them.
  3. Choose one of the two parts, usually the one with fewer loads.
  4. Draw that part as a free body, with the cut members shown as tension.
  5. Take moments about the point where two of the unknown cut members meet. Their forces then have zero moment, and the equation contains only one unknown.

Worked example: the bottom chord

Use the triangle truss again, with RB = 5 kN. Find the force in the bottom member AB by cutting through AB and BC, and taking the right-hand part, which holds B.

  1. The cut members are AB and BC. Their forces are shown as tension.
  2. Take moments about C, where BC meets the load line. The force in BC passes through C, so it has zero moment and drops out.
  3. Forces on the right part: RB = 5 kN up at B, which is 4 m from A and 2 m to the right of C. Its moment about C is 5 × 2 = 10 kN·m, anticlockwise.
  4. The tension FAB pulls B to the left, and it is 3 m below C. Its moment about C is 3 × FAB kN·m, clockwise.
  5. Sum of moments about C: 10 - 3 × FAB = 0, so FAB = 3.33 kN, a tension. This agrees with the method of joints.
R_B = 5.0            # reaction at B in kN
horizontal_dist = 2.0   # from C to B, horizontally, in metres
height = 3.0            # from C down to the bottom chord, in metres

# Moments about C: R_B * horizontal_dist - F_AB * height = 0
F_AB = R_B * horizontal_dist / height
print("Section check: F_AB = %.2f kN (tension)" % F_AB)
Section check: F_AB = 3.33 kN (tension)

TipWhen you take moments in a section, pick the point where the unknowns you do not want meet. Their moment arms become zero, which removes them from the equation.

Question: why is the moment point C chosen, and not A?

At C, the force in BC has zero moment, so the equation contains only FAB. Taking moments at A would include the unknown force in AC.

Question: when would you use the method of joints instead of sections?

When you need the forces in all members, or when the joints are simple and each has only two unknowns.

Frames and indeterminate structures

Frames are not trusses

A frame has members that carry more than an axial force. A member may be bent by a load along its length, or it may have a force applied between its ends. Such a member is a multi-force member. You cannot treat it as a two-force member, so its free-body diagram must show every force, and the pins at its ends transmit both x and y forces.

Newton's third law links the members. The pin force that member 1 applies to member 2 is equal and opposite to the force member 2 applies to member 1. Draw each member on its own, show the pin forces on each, and then write equilibrium for each member.

  Frame with a bent member and a pin

          load P (down)
             |
             v
   ----------------------   member 1 (has a load between ends)
            |
            | pin force from member 2 (x and y)
            v
   ----------------------   member 2 (a two-force member, if
                              unloaded between its ends)

Indeterminate structures

A structure is indeterminate if it has more unknowns than equilibrium equations. The degree of indeterminacy counts the extra unknowns. Equilibrium cannot find them, so you need compatibility equations, which say how the members deform together. For GATE, the key skill is to recognise the degree and to say that equilibrium alone is not enough.

degree = m + r - 2j

A degree of zero is determinate. A positive degree is indeterminate, and a negative degree means the structure is unstable.

GATE loves thisWhen a question asks whether a truss can be solved by equilibrium alone, count m + r and 2j first. If m + r is larger, the answer is no, and the question is probably testing that exact idea.

Question: a frame with 12 members, 8 joints and 3 reactions. What is its degree?

12 + 3 - 16 = -1. A negative degree means the frame is unstable, not determinate.

Question: does a frame with a multi-force member need the same counting rule as a truss?

The counting idea is the same, but each member may have three or more unknowns when drawn as a free body. Count the unknown forces at each pin and member carefully, and compare with the three equations per rigid body.

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original problems in the GATE style. They are not real past paper questions.

Reactions of a symmetric truss

A symmetric triangle truss has span 6 m. A 12 kN load acts downward at the apex, which is directly above the midpoint of the span. Find the vertical reaction at each support, in kN.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. RA and RB in kN.
  2. Which idea applies. Symmetry: the two reactions share the load equally.
  3. Work it out. RA = RB = 12 / 2 = 6 kN.
  4. Check. RA + RB = 12 kN, which equals the applied load. Good.
P = 12.0
R_A = P / 2
R_B = P / 2
print("R_A = %.1f kN, R_B = %.1f kN" % (R_A, R_B))
R_A = 6.0 kN, R_B = 6.0 kN

Answer: 6 kN at each support.

Is this truss determinate?

A plane truss has 9 members and 6 joints. Its supports give 3 reaction unknowns in total. Is the truss statically determinate? Give the degree of indeterminacy.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Whether it is determinate, and its degree.
  2. Which idea applies. Degree = m + r - 2j.
  3. Work it out. Degree = 9 + 3 - 2 × 6 = 12 - 12 = 0. So the truss is determinate.
  4. Check. Unknowns 12 and equations 12 are equal, which matches a degree of zero. Good.
m, j, r = 9, 6, 3
print("Degree = %d" % (m + r - 2 * j))
Degree = 0

Answer: determinate, with degree 0.

Which statements about trusses are true?

Consider the following statements about trusses and their analysis. Select all that apply.

(A) The method of joints uses ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0 at each pin.
(B) A joint with two members that are not in line, and no load, has both member forces equal to zero.
(C) The method of sections can cut through three members with unknown forces, and solve them with the three equilibrium equations.
(D) A frame with a member carrying a load between its ends can be analysed exactly as a truss.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. The joint equations, zero-force rules, the limit of three cut members, and the difference between trusses and frames.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) True, this is Rule 1 for zero-force members. (C) True, three equations can solve three unknowns. (D) False. A member with a load between its ends is a multi-force member, not a two-force member.
  4. Check. Statement (D) describes a frame, which needs its own free-body diagrams, so it is wrong.

Answer: (A), (B) and (C). Statement (D) is false.

Force in an inclined member

A symmetric triangle truss has span 8 m and height 4 m. A 20 kN load acts downward at the apex. Find the magnitude of the force in an inclined member, in kN, to two decimal places. State whether it is tension or compression.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The size of the inclined member force and its type.
  2. Which idea applies. Reactions by symmetry, then the vertical balance at joint A.
  3. Work it out. RA = 10 kN. The member length is √(4² + 4²) = 5.657 m. Its vertical part is 4 / 5.657 of the force. From joint A: 10 + F × 4 / 5.657 = 0, so F = -14.14 kN. The magnitude is 14.14 kN, in compression.
  4. Check. The compression is larger than the reaction because the member is inclined at 45 degrees and only part of its force is vertical. Good.
import math

P = 20.0
R_A = P / 2
L = math.sqrt(4.0 ** 2 + 4.0 ** 2)
F = -R_A * L / 4.0
print("Length = %.3f m" % L)
print("Force = %.2f kN" % F)
Length = 5.657 m
Force = -14.14 kN

Answer: 14.14 kN, in compression.

Bottom chord force by sections

The same symmetric truss (span 8 m, height 4 m, 20 kN load at the apex) is cut through the bottom chord AB and the inclined member BC. Using the right-hand part, find the force in AB, in kN. State whether it is tension or compression.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. FAB in kN, with its type.
  2. Which idea applies. Moments about C, where the force in BC has no moment arm.
  3. Work it out. RB = 10 kN, acting upward at B, which is 4 m to the right of C. Its moment about C is 10 × 4 = 40 kN·m, anticlockwise. The tension FAB acts at 4 m below C, so its moment is 4 × FAB, clockwise. So 40 - 4 × FAB = 0, which gives FAB = 10 kN, a tension.
  4. Check. Check at joint A: the 14.14 kN compression in AC pushes A to the left with a horizontal part of 14.14 × cos 45° = 10 kN. This balances the 10 kN tension in AB. Good.
R_B = 10.0
dist_x = 4.0
dist_y = 4.0
F_AB = R_B * dist_x / dist_y
print("F_AB = %.2f kN (tension)" % F_AB)
F_AB = 10.00 kN (tension)

Answer: 10 kN, in tension.

Vertical reaction under an inclined load

A truss has a pin at A (0, 0) and a roller at B (6, 0), with coordinates in metres. The apex C is at (3, 3). A 12 kN load acts at C, inclined at 30 degrees from the vertical, pointing downward and to the right. Find the vertical reaction RA at the pin, in kN, to two decimal places. Take anticlockwise moments as positive.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. RA in kN, upward.
  2. Which idea applies. Resolve the load into components, then take moments about B, which removes RB.
  3. Work it out. Load components: Fx = 12 sin 30° = 6 kN (to the right), Fy = -12 cos 30° = -10.392 kN (down). Moments about B: RA acts at A, which is 6 m to the left of B, giving -6 RA. The load acts at C, which is (-3, 3) from B. Its moment is (-3)(-10.392) - (3)(6) = 31.176 - 18 = 13.176 kN·m. So -6 RA + 13.176 = 0, and RA = 2.196 kN, about 2.20 kN.
  4. Check. From the vertical balance RB = 10.392 - 2.196 = 8.196 kN. Taking moments about A then gives 6 × 8.196 = 49.18, which matches the clockwise moment of the load about A. Good.
import math

F = 12.0
ang = math.radians(30.0)
Fx = F * math.sin(ang)          # to the right
Fy = -F * math.cos(ang)         # downward

# Moments about B. The load at C is at (-3, 3) from B.
M_load = (-3.0) * Fy - 3.0 * Fx
R_A = M_load / 6.0              # R_A acts 6 m to the left of B
R_B = -Fy - R_A
print("R_A = %.2f kN" % R_A)
print("R_B = %.2f kN" % R_B)
R_A = 2.20 kN
R_B = 8.20 kN

Answer: RA = 2.20 kN (upward).

Choose the correct statements about indeterminacy and frames

Consider the following statements. Select all that apply.

(A) A plane truss with m + r greater than 2j is statically indeterminate.
(B) A member with a force applied between its ends must be drawn as a multi-force member in a frame.
(C) Zero-force members are always unnecessary for carrying any load on the structure.
(D) When only one member force is needed, the method of sections is often the quicker route.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. The counting rule, the definition of a multi-force member, and the purpose of zero-force members.
  3. Work it out. (A) True, the degree m + r - 2j is positive. (B) True, the member cannot be treated as a two-force member. (C) False. A zero-force member carries nothing under the given load, but it may carry load under another load case, or it may keep the structure stable. (D) True, a single cut gives the member directly.
  4. Check. Statement (C) confuses "zero under this load" with "never needed", so it is rejected.

Answer: (A), (B) and (D). Statement (C) is false.

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Method of joints (NAT, 2 marks)

A symmetric triangular truss has a 4 m base and a 3 m height. A 10 kN vertical load acts at the apex. Find the force in each inclined member.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Member force at the apex.
  2. Which idea applies. By symmetry, each inclined member carries half the vertical load in its vertical component.
  3. Work it out. Inclined length = √(4 + 9) = 3.606 m. Vertical component 5 kN = F · 3/3.606, so F = 6.01 kN (compression).
  4. Check. Base member tension = F · 2/3.606 = 3.33 kN.
import math
L = math.hypot(2, 3)
F = 5 * L / 3
print("%.2f kN, base %.2f kN" % (F, F * 2 / L))

6.01 kN, base 3.33 kN

Answer: about 6.01 kN (compression).

Exam-level 2: Determinacy (NAT, 1 mark)

A planar pin jointed truss has 9 joints and is supported by a pin and a roller. How many members make it statically determinate?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. m for determinacy.
  2. Which idea applies. m + r = 2j.
  3. Work it out. m + 3 = 18 gives m = 15.
  4. Check. Fewer members make it a mechanism.

Answer: 15.

Exam-level 3: Zero force members (MSQ, 2 marks)

Which are TRUE? (A) At an unloaded joint with two non-collinear members, both are zero force members. (B) At an unloaded joint with three members, two collinear, the third is zero force. (C) Zero force members can be removed without any effect in every design. (D) Truss members carry only axial forces in the ideal model.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Truss facts.
  2. Which idea applies. Equilibrium along directions perpendicular to members.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) True. (C) They give stability and resist buckling, so not removable. False. (D) True.
  4. Check. Zero force members change with the load case.

Answer: (A), (B) and (D).

A member loaded only at its ends, so its force is axial.
Tension and compression
Tension stretches a member, compression squeezes it. Tension is positive here.
Determinacy count
m + r = 2j for a determinate plane truss.
Degree of indeterminacy
m + r - 2j. Zero is determinate, positive is indeterminate.

Write ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0 at each pin in turn.

Cut through at most three unknown members and take moments at a point where two of them meet.
Zero-force member
A member that carries no force under the given loading.

Two non-collinear members at an unloaded joint are both zero.

A structure with multi-force members, not just two-force members.

Solvable by equilibrium alone.

Remember these

  1. Count m + r and 2j before you start any truss.
  2. Use the method of joints for all forces, and the method of sections for one or two.
  3. Draw every member force as tension, and let the sign show compression.
  4. Look for zero-force members at unloaded joints before you calculate.
  5. Choose the moment point where two unknown members meet.
  6. Frames have multi-force members, so they need their own free-body diagrams.

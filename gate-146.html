Dynamics of rigid bodies in plane motion

A force pushes a body, and a torque turns it. This topic links the forces and torques acting on a rigid body to its motion. The key new idea is the moment of inertia, which measures how hard a body is to turn. Rolling bodies and turning wheels are common GATE Robotics questions.

The idea in plain words

Push an empty shopping trolley and it speeds up easily. Push a loaded trolley with the same force and it speeds up slowly. The heavier one has more mass, so it resists a change in its straight-line motion. A rigid body also resists a change in its turning. That resistance is called the moment of inertia.

Newton's second law says force equals mass times acceleration. For a rigid body we need two such statements. One says how the centre of mass moves when forces push it. The other says how the body turns when torques (turning effects of forces) act on it. Together they solve most rigid body dynamics problems.

Everyday analogy. Hold a metre stick at one end and try to turn it. It is easy. Hold it at the middle and turn it. It is harder when the mass is spread out far from the turning axis. Mass far from the axis is hard to turn, and that is the idea behind moment of inertia.

Where it appears in GATE. In the Robotics and Automation (RA) paper you may be asked for the angular acceleration of a link under a given torque, the acceleration of a rolling wheel on a slope, or the moment of inertia of a common shape. These are short calculations, but each step must be correct.

Key terms

Mass (m)
The amount of matter in a body, in kilograms. It measures resistance to a change in straight-line motion.
Centre of mass (G)
The average position of the mass of a body. For a uniform shape, this is its geometric centre. We write G for the centre of mass or centroid.
Force (F)
A push or pull, in newtons (N). Newton's second law: ΣF = m aG.
Torque or moment (M)
The turning effect of a force about a point, in N·m. It equals force times the perpendicular distance from the point to the line of action of the force.
Moment of inertia (I)
The resistance of a body to angular acceleration about an axis, in kg·m². It depends on mass and on how far the mass is from the axis.
Angular acceleration (α)
The rate of change of angular velocity, in rad/s².
Fixed axis
A line that does not move while the body turns about it.
Radius of gyration (k)
A distance such that I = m k². It tells you how far from the axis the mass is, on average.
Parallel axis theorem
A rule that gives the moment of inertia about any axis parallel to the centroidal axis: I = IG + m d².
Rolling without slipping
A wheel rolls on a surface and the contact point does not skid, so v = ωR.
Friction (f)
The force between two surfaces that resists sliding. Static friction can act without motion at the contact.
D'Alembert's principle
A trick that turns a dynamics problem into a statics problem by adding inertia forces and inertia torques.

Newton's laws for a rigid body

Two equations, not one

A point particle needs one equation: ΣF = ma. A rigid body also turns, so it needs a second equation about its turning. We write both about the centre of mass G.

ΣF = m aG
ΣMG = IG α

Here ΣF is the sum of the forces, in N. The sum ΣMG is the sum of the torques about G, in N·m. IG is the moment of inertia about G, and α is the angular acceleration.

Worked example. A uniform bar is pushed by a force of 10 N at its centre of mass. It has mass 2 kg and no torque. Find aG. The force gives aG = ΣF/m = 10/2 = 5 m/s². Since the force passes through G, the torque about G is zero, so α = 0. The bar slides without turning.

Key ideaA force through the centre of mass changes the motion of the centre only. A force away from the centre also produces a torque, and the body turns.

If a force acts at a point away from G, what happens to the rotation?

The torque about G is the force times its perpendicular distance from G. A nonzero torque gives an angular acceleration α = ΣMG/IG, so the body turns.

Why do we sum torques about the centre of mass?

Taking moments about G makes the equation simpler, since the acceleration of G then does not appear in the moment equation. This is the standard choice for free bodies.

Moment of inertia and common shapes

What the moment of inertia measures

For a particle of mass m at distance r from an axis, the moment of inertia is I = mr². The square makes distance count a lot. Moving mass twice as far from the axis makes I four times bigger. For a whole body we add up the contributions of all its small pieces.

I = Σ m r² (for a body, the sum becomes an integral)

Read it as: take each small piece, multiply its mass by the square of its distance from the axis, and add. You do not need to do the integral for the shapes below. Use the table.

ShapeAxisMoment of inertia
Solid disc or cylinder, radius Rthrough the centre, perpendicular to the faceI = (1/2) m R²
Thin ring or hoop, radius Rthrough the centreI = m R²
Thin rod, length Lthrough the centreI = (1/12) m L²
Thin rod, length Lthrough one endI = (1/3) m L²
Solid sphere, radius Rthrough the centreI = (2/5) m R²

Worked example. A solid disc has mass 2 kg and radius 0.1 m. Its moment of inertia about its centre is I = (1/2)(2)(0.1)² = (1/2)(2)(0.01) = 0.01 kg·m².

m = 2.0                  # mass of the disc in kg
R = 0.1                  # radius in metres
I = 0.5 * m * R ** 2     # solid disc about its centre
print(f"I = {I:.2f} kg m^2")
I = 0.01 kg m^2

TrapThe disc and the ring have different moments of inertia even with the same mass and radius. A ring has all its mass at distance R, so I = mR². A solid disc has mass spread inside, so I = (1/2)mR². The ring is harder to turn.

TipLearn the table in pairs: the disc and the rod about the centre have a factor 1/2 and 1/12, while the ends and rings have bigger factors. Bigger factor means harder to turn.

Which is harder to turn about its centre, a solid disc or a thin ring, with the same mass and radius?

The ring. Its moment of inertia is mR², which is twice the disc's (1/2)mR².

Why does a rod turned about its end have a bigger moment of inertia than about its centre?

Its mass is further from an end axis. Average distance squared is bigger, so I = (1/3)mL² is bigger than (1/12)mL².

The parallel axis theorem

Moving the axis

The table gives I about the centre. Often a question asks about an axis at a different place, such as the end of a rod or the contact point of a wheel. The parallel axis theorem handles this. For an axis parallel to the centroidal axis at distance d, the moment of inertia is

I = IG + m d²

Symbols: I is the moment of inertia about the new axis, IG is about the centre, m is the mass, and d is the distance between the two parallel axes. Read it as: the body's own inertia, plus the inertia of all its mass acting as if it sat at distance d.

Worked example. A thin rod has mass 3 kg and length 1 m. Its centre is at distance d = 0.5 m from one end. First find IG = (1/12)(3)(1)² = 0.25 kg·m². Then Iend = 0.25 + 3 × (0.5)² = 0.25 + 0.75 = 1.0 kg·m². This equals (1/3)mL² = (1/3)(3)(1)² = 1.0, which confirms the table value.

m, L = 3.0, 1.0                    # rod mass in kg and length in m
I_G = m * L ** 2 / 12              # about the centre
d = L / 2                          # distance from centre to end
I_end = I_G + m * d ** 2           # parallel axis theorem
print(f"I about centre = {I_G:.2f} kg m^2")
print(f"I about end = {I_end:.2f} kg m^2")
print(f"table value (1/3) m L^2 = {m * L ** 2 / 3:.2f} kg m^2")
I about centre = 0.25 kg m^2
I about end = 1.00 kg m^2
table value (1/3) m L^2 = 1.00 kg m^2

TrapUse the parallel axis theorem only for parallel axes. The distance d is measured perpendicular to the axes, from the centre of mass to the new axis. Adding d instead of squaring it is a common error.

A uniform rod of mass 6 kg and length 2 m is pivoted at one end. What is its moment of inertia about that end?

I = (1/3)mL² = (1/3)(6)(4) = 8 kg·m². Check with the theorem: IG = (1/12)(6)(4) = 2, and d = 1, so I = 2 + 6 × 1 = 8.

Rotation about a fixed axis

The simplest dynamics problem

When a body turns about a fixed axis O, its centre moves in a circle, so the force equation is more complicated. It is simpler to take moments about the fixed axis O. Because O does not move, the reaction forces at O have zero moment about O, and they drop out of the equation.

ΣMO = IO α

Read it as: the sum of all torques about the fixed axis O equals the moment of inertia about O times the angular acceleration. So α = ΣMO/IO. A larger torque or a smaller inertia gives a bigger acceleration.

Setup of the pivoted rod

A thin rod of mass 3 kg and length 1 m is pivoted at its end O. It is held horizontal and released. Its centre G is 0.5 m from O. Gravity acts at G, downward, with g = 10 m/s².

   O (pivot)
   o============================ rod, length L = 1 m
   |<------ 0.5 m ------>|  G (centre, weight mg acts here)
                          |
                          v  mg = 30 N

   Released from rest, the rod swings down about O.

Worked example. The weight acts at G, at horizontal distance 0.5 m from O. Its torque about O is mg × 0.5 = 30 × 0.5 = 15 N·m, which turns the rod down. The moment of inertia about O is IO = 1.0 kg·m² (from the parallel axis theorem). So α = 15/1 = 15 rad/s². The rod starts from rest with this angular acceleration.

m, L, g = 3.0, 1.0, 10.0          # mass in kg, length in m, gravity in m/s^2
torque = m * g * (L / 2)          # weight times distance from O to G
I_O = m * L ** 2 / 3              # rod about its end
alpha = torque / I_O
print(f"torque about O = {torque:.1f} N m")
print(f"alpha = {alpha:.1f} rad/s^2")
torque about O = 15.0 N m
alpha = 15.0 rad/s^2

TrapDo not use the centre of mass moment of inertia (1/12)mL² about the pivot. Use I about the pivot, because the moment equation is taken about the pivot.

TipMark the sign direction first. Pick "counterclockwise positive" and write the torque with that sign. The weight here turns the rod clockwise, so its torque is negative in that convention.

What happens to α if the rod is pivoted at its centre instead?

The weight has no torque about the centre, since its line of action passes through the pivot. So the rod does not accelerate angularly and stays horizontal. This shows why the position of the pivot matters.

Rolling bodies on an incline

Why the shape matters

Roll a solid ball and a hollow tube down the same slope. The ball wins. Both have the same mass and size, but the ball's mass is closer to its centre, so its moment of inertia is smaller and it turns more easily. A rolling body splits its energy and effort between sliding and turning, so the answer depends on I.

Setting up the disc on the slope

          ____
         /    \      disc, centre G, radius R
        |  (o) |     rolls down the slope
         \____/
   ______________________________  slope
   \ θ
    (the slope is tilted by the angle θ from the horizontal)

   Forces on the disc:
   - weight mg, acting down at the centre
   - normal force N, perpendicular to the slope
   - friction f, acting UP the slope at the contact point

Let the disc have mass m, radius R, and centre acceleration a along the slope. Take down-slope as positive. Friction f acts up the slope. Two equations follow.

  1. Along the slope: m g sinθ − f = m a.
  2. About the centre: f R = IG α, with IG = (1/2)mR² for a solid disc.

Rolling without slipping gives α = a/R. Substituting: f R = (1/2) m R² (a/R) = (1/2) m a, so f = (1/2) m a. Put this into the first equation: m g sinθ − (1/2) m a = m a. Therefore m g sinθ = (3/2) m a, and

a = (2/3) g sinθ

Worked example

Take g = 10 m/s², θ = 30° (sin 30° = 0.5), and a disc of mass 2 kg. Then a = (2/3)(10)(0.5) = 3.33 m/s². The friction needed is f = (1/2)(2)(3.33) = 3.33 N, pointing up the slope. For comparison, a frictionless block would slide with a = g sinθ = 5 m/s².

import math
g = 10.0
theta = math.radians(30)
m = 2.0
a_roll = (2 / 3) * g * math.sin(theta)     # solid disc rolling
a_slide = g * math.sin(theta)              # frictionless sliding
f = 0.5 * m * a_roll                       # friction needed to roll
print(f"rolling a = {a_roll:.2f} m/s^2")
print(f"sliding a = {a_slide:.2f} m/s^2")
print(f"friction f = {f:.2f} N")
rolling a = 3.33 m/s^2
sliding a = 5.00 m/s^2
friction f = 3.33 N

GATE loves thisFor any rolling body: a = g sinθ / (1 + IG/(mR²)). Learn this one line and you can get the answer for disc, ring or sphere by plugging in the shape factor.

TrapFriction is not "reducing" the acceleration by itself. It is the force that provides the torque. Without friction, a disc would slide, and the acceleration would be bigger, g sinθ.

Which body rolls down an incline faster, a solid sphere or a ring with the same mass and radius?

The solid sphere. Its shape factor I/(mR²) is 2/5, while the ring's is 1. The sphere's acceleration is g sinθ/(1 + 2/5) = (5/7) g sinθ, and the ring's is (1/2) g sinθ. The sphere is faster.

Why does friction point up the slope for a disc rolling down it?

The contact point tends to slide down the slope relative to the surface. Friction opposes that tendency, so it acts up the slope. Its torque about the centre is what speeds up the spin.

D'Alembert's principle

Turning dynamics into statics

Many students find accelerations harder to handle than forces. D'Alembert's principle gives a trick. Add an imaginary inertia force −m aG at G and an imaginary inertia couple −IG α. With these added, the body is in equilibrium, so the usual statics equations hold: sum of forces equals zero and sum of moments equals zero.

  • Draw the free body diagram (real forces).
  • Add the inertia force −m aG and the inertia couple −IG α (the kinetic diagram).
  • Write ΣF = 0 and ΣM = 0 and solve.

The results are identical to the two Newton equations. The method is useful when many forces act, because it keeps your drawing organised.

Key ideaD'Alembert's principle is Newton's laws rearranged. It does not add new physics. It only helps you write the equations in a standard form.

What is the inertia couple for a body with angular acceleration α about G?

It is −IG α. Its direction opposes the angular acceleration, just as the inertia force −m aG opposes the acceleration of G.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original, written in the style of GATE numerical and multiple select questions. They are not past exam questions.

Moment of inertia of a solid disc

A solid disc has mass 2 kg and radius 0.1 m. What is its moment of inertia about an axis through its centre, perpendicular to its face, in kg·m²? (Numerical answer.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. I about the centre of a solid disc.
  2. Which idea applies. I = (1/2)mR² for a solid disc about its centre.
  3. Work it out. I = (1/2)(2)(0.1)² = (1/2)(2)(0.01) = 0.01 kg·m².
  4. Check. A ring of the same mass and radius would give 2 × 0.1² = 0.02. A solid disc should be smaller than the ring, and 0.01 is half of 0.02.

Answer: 0.01 kg·m².

Which statements about inertia are true

Select all that apply.

(A) The moment of inertia depends on the mass of the body.
(B) The moment of inertia depends on how the mass is spread from the axis.
(C) For a fixed axis, α = ΣMO/IO.
(D) The moment of inertia depends on the angular velocity.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements about moment of inertia are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. I = Σmr² depends on masses and their distances from the axis. The angular acceleration formula comes from ΣM = Iα.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true, since mass appears in I. (B) is true, since r appears squared. (C) is true, since rearranging ΣMO = IOα gives it. (D) is false, since I is a property of the body and axis, not of its motion.
  4. Check. A spinning disc and a still disc of the same shape have the same moment of inertia.

Answer: (A), (B) and (C).

Angular acceleration of a thin rod

A thin uniform rod of mass 6 kg and length 2 m is free to turn about its centre. A net torque of 12 N·m acts on it. What is its angular acceleration, in rad/s²? (Numerical answer.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. α for a rod turning about its centre.
  2. Which idea applies. ΣM = Iα, with I = (1/12)mL² for a rod about its centre.
  3. Work it out. I = (1/12)(6)(2)² = (1/12)(6)(4) = 2 kg·m². So α = 12/2 = 6 rad/s².
  4. Check. Units: N·m divided by kg·m² gives 1/s² in base units, which is rad/s². The number is plausible for a rod this size.

Answer: 6 rad/s².

Moment of inertia about the end of a rod

A thin uniform rod has mass 4 kg and length 0.5 m. What is its moment of inertia about an axis through one end, perpendicular to the rod, in kg·m²? Give your answer to two decimal places. (Numerical answer.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. I about an end of a thin rod.
  2. Which idea applies. I = (1/3)mL² for a rod about an end.
  3. Work it out. I = (1/3)(4)(0.5)² = (1/3)(4)(0.25) = (1/3)(1) = 0.333... kg·m².
  4. Check. The end value must be bigger than the centre value (1/12)(4)(0.25) = 0.083. Yes, 0.33 is four times bigger, which matches (1/3)/(1/12) = 4.
m, L = 4.0, 0.5
I_end = m * L ** 2 / 3
print(f"I about an end = {I_end:.2f} kg m^2")
I about an end = 0.33 kg m^2

Answer: 0.33 kg·m².

Solid sphere rolling down a slope

A solid sphere rolls without slipping down a slope of angle 30°. Take g = 10 m/s². What is the acceleration of its centre, in m/s²? Give your answer to two decimal places. (Numerical answer.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The centre acceleration of a rolling sphere.
  2. Which idea applies. a = g sinθ/(1 + IG/(mR²)), with IG/(mR²) = 2/5 for a solid sphere.
  3. Work it out. g sinθ = 10 × 0.5 = 5 m/s². The denominator is 1 + 2/5 = 7/5. So a = 5 × 5/7 = 25/7 = 3.571..., which is 3.57 m/s².
  4. Check. The sphere must be slower than the frictionless block (5 m/s²) and faster than a ring (2.5 m/s²). The value 3.57 lies between them.
import math
g, theta = 10.0, math.radians(30)
a = g * math.sin(theta) / (1 + 2 / 5)
print(f"a = {a:.2f} m/s^2")
a = 3.57 m/s^2

Answer: 3.57 m/s².

Minimum friction for a disc to roll

A solid disc rolls without slipping down a slope of angle 30°. The coefficient of static friction between disc and slope is μ. Find the smallest μ that stops the disc from slipping. Use the normal force N = mg cosθ and the friction f from the rolling analysis. Give your answer to two decimal places. (Numerical answer.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The minimum μ, which means the largest friction the disc needs must be allowed.
  2. Which idea applies. For a solid disc, f = (1/3) m g sinθ, derived from f = (1/2)ma and a = (2/3)g sinθ. Friction is limited by μN, so μ must be at least f/N.
  3. Work it out. f/N = (1/3) m g sinθ / (m g cosθ) = (1/3) tanθ. For θ = 30°, tan 30° = 0.577, so μmin = 0.577/3 = 0.19.
  4. Check. The answer is a pure number that is less than 1, which is reasonable for typical friction coefficients. The Python check below gives the same value.
import math
theta = math.radians(30)
mu_min = (1 / 3) * math.tan(theta)
print(f"mu_min = {mu_min:.2f}")
mu_min = 0.19

Answer: 0.19.

Statements about rolling and sliding

A body rolls without slipping down a slope. Select all that apply.

(A) A solid disc on a slope has acceleration g sinθ.
(B) A thin hollow ring rolling without slipping has acceleration (1/2) g sinθ.
(C) A frictionless block sliding down the same slope has acceleration g sinθ.
(D) For a disc rolling down the slope, friction acts up the slope.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Find every correct statement about rolling and sliding.
  2. Which idea applies. a = g sinθ/(1 + IG/(mR²)). For a ring, IG = mR², so the factor is 1 + 1 = 2.
  3. Work it out. (A) is false: the solid disc gives (2/3) g sinθ, since 1 + 1/2 = 3/2. (B) is true: the ring gives g sinθ/2. (C) is true: with no friction nothing turns the body, so it slides with g sinθ. (D) is true: friction acts at the contact point and opposes its tendency to slide down, so it acts up the slope and provides the torque.
  4. Check. Compare: the ring is slowest (1/2), the disc is between (2/3), and the frictionless block is fastest (1). The order is correct.

Answer: (B), (C) and (D).

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Rolling cylinder on an incline (NAT, 2 marks)

A solid cylinder rolls without slipping down a 30° incline. Take g = 9.81. Find its acceleration.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. a for rolling.
  2. Which idea applies. a = g sin θ/(1 + I/(mr²)) with I = mr²/2.
  3. Work it out. 4.905/1.5 = 3.27 m/s².
  4. Check. A sliding block (no friction) would have 4.905 m/s².
print("%.2f" % (9.81 * 0.5 / 1.5))

3.27

Answer: about 3.27 m/s².

Exam-level 2: Torque to accelerate a flywheel (NAT, 2 marks)

A flywheel has I = 2 kg·m². How much torque brings it from 0 to 300 rpm in 5 s?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. T = Iα.
  2. Which idea applies. α = Δω/t.
  3. Work it out. ω = 31.416 rad/s, α = 6.283 rad/s², T = 12.57 N·m.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
import math
print("%.2f" % (2 * 300 * 2 * math.pi / 60 / 5))

12.57

Answer: about 12.57 N·m.

Exam-level 3: Rotational kinetic energy (NAT, 2 marks)

A solid sphere of mass 4 kg rolls without slipping at 3 m/s. Find its total kinetic energy.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Translational plus rotational energy.
  2. Which idea applies. I = (2/5)mr², so KE = (1/2)mv²(1 + 2/5).
  3. Work it out. 0.5 · 4 · 9 · 1.4 = 25.2 J.
  4. Check. The radius cancels.

Answer: 25.2 J.

Remember these

  1. Use ΣF = m aG for the centre and ΣM = Iα for turning.
  2. The moment of inertia grows with the square of the distance from the axis.
  3. A ring is harder to turn than a solid disc of the same mass and radius.
  4. Use the parallel axis theorem when the axis is not through the centre.
  5. For a fixed axis, take moments about that axis, so reaction forces drop out.
  6. For a rolling body on a slope, a = g sinθ/(1 + IG/(mR²)).
  7. Friction on a rolling body acts up the slope and provides the torque.
  8. Check the answer against the frictionless case: rolling is always slower.

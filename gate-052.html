Probability axioms & conditional probability (recap)

Probability is the language of uncertainty. This recap covers the three axioms that every probability obeys, the addition rule, conditional probability, independence and Bayes theorem. Bayes theorem appears in many GATE DA and RA questions, so it gets the most attention here.

The idea in plain words

Probability is a number between 0 and 1 that tells you how likely an event is. Zero means impossible, one means certain, and 0.5 means as likely as not. The roll of a die, the result of a test and the fault in a machine can all be described this way.

Everyday picture: a forecast that says "70 percent chance of rain" does not say it will rain, and it does not say it will not. It describes how often rain happens in similar conditions.

Conditional probability is the same idea after you learn something new. If you already know it is a Tuesday, the chance of a train being late changes. Bayes theorem reverses a conditional: from "how likely is the evidence given a cause" to "how likely is the cause given the evidence". That reversal is what makes it so useful in robotics and data science.

Key terms

Experiment
Plain meaning: an action with an uncertain result, such as a die roll. Precise definition: a process whose outcome is not known in advance.
Sample space (S)
Plain meaning: the list of all possible results. Precise definition: the set of all outcomes of an experiment.
Event
Plain meaning: a result, or group of results, you care about. Precise definition: a subset of the sample space.
Complement (Ac)
Plain meaning: "not A". Precise definition: the outcomes in S that are not in A.
Union (A ∪ B)
Plain meaning: "A or B or both". Precise definition: the outcomes in A, in B, or in both.
Intersection (A ∩ B)
Plain meaning: "A and B together". Precise definition: the outcomes in both A and B.
Mutually exclusive
Plain meaning: cannot happen together. Precise definition: A ∩ B is empty, so P(A ∩ B) = 0.
Conditional probability P(A | B)
Plain meaning: the chance of A once B is known to have happened. Precise definition: P(A ∩ B)/P(B), when P(B) is greater than 0.
Independence
Plain meaning: knowing B does not change the chance of A. Precise definition: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B).
Partition
Plain meaning: separate cases that together cover everything. Precise definition: events A1, ..., An that are mutually exclusive and whose union is S.

Part 1: Axioms and the addition rule

Every probability model obeys three rules, called the axioms:

  1. Non-negativity. P(A) ≥ 0 for every event A.
  2. Certainty. P(S) = 1. Something in the sample space must happen.
  3. Additivity. If A and B are mutually exclusive, then P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B).

Two useful results follow:

P(Ac) = 1 − P(A)
P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)

The second is the addition rule. The subtraction removes the overlap, which would otherwise be counted twice. Example with a die: let A be "the roll is even", so A = {2, 4, 6}, and B be "the roll is greater than 3", so B = {4, 5, 6}. Then P(A) = 3/6, P(B) = 3/6 and A ∩ B = {4, 6}, so P(A ∩ B) = 2/6. The addition rule gives P(A ∪ B) = 3/6 + 3/6 − 2/6 = 4/6 = 2/3. Listing A ∪ B = {2, 4, 5, 6} confirms 4 out of 6.

from fractions import Fraction

S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}
A = {2, 4, 6}
B = {4, 5, 6}
def P(E):
    return Fraction(len(E), len(S))

print("P(A) =", P(A), " P(B) =", P(B), " P(A and B) =", P(A & B))
print("P(A or B) by the rule:", P(A) + P(B) - P(A & B))
print("P(A or B) by listing:", P(A | B))
print("P(not A) =", P(S - A))
P(A) = 1/2  P(B) = 1/2  P(A and B) = 1/3
P(A or B) by the rule: 2/3
P(A or B) by listing: 2/3
P(not A) = 1/2

TrapAdd P(A) + P(B) only when the events cannot happen together. Otherwise subtract the overlap.

If P(A) = 0.3, what is P(not A)?

P(not A) = 1 − 0.3 = 0.7.

Can P(A) be 1.2?

No. Probabilities lie between 0 and 1, so 1.2 would break the axioms.

Part 2: Conditional probability and independence

Conditional probability asks: "given that B has happened, how likely is A?" We shrink the sample space to B and look at the part of A that lies inside it.

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B), when P(B) is greater than 0
  • P(A | B) is read "the probability of A given B".
  • P(A ∩ B) is the chance that both happen.
  • P(B) is the new, smaller sample space we now live in.

Rearranging gives the multiplication rule, P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B) × P(B).

A worked example, every step shown

Two fair dice are rolled. Given that the first die shows 3, what is the probability that the total is at least 8?

Step 1: Events. A is "total at least 8". B is "first die is 3".

Step 2: Count B. There are 6 outcomes with a first die of 3, so P(B) = 6/36 = 1/6.

Step 3: Count both. The total is 3 plus the second die, so the second die must be 5 or 6. That gives 2 outcomes, so P(A ∩ B) = 2/36.

Step 4: Divide. P(A | B) = (2/36)/(6/36) = 2/6 = 1/3.

from fractions import Fraction
from itertools import product

outcomes = list(product(range(1, 7), repeat=2))   # 36 equally likely pairs
B = [o for o in outcomes if o[0] == 3]
AB = [o for o in B if sum(o) >= 8]
print("P(first die is 3) =", Fraction(len(B), len(outcomes)))
print("P(total >= 8 and first die is 3) =", Fraction(len(AB), len(outcomes)))
print("P(total >= 8 given first die is 3) =", Fraction(len(AB), len(B)))
P(first die is 3) = 1/6
P(total >= 8 and first die is 3) = 1/18
P(total >= 8 given first die is 3) = 1/3

Independence

Events A and B are independent when knowing B does not change the chance of A. In formulas, P(A | B) = P(A), which is the same as P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B). Two separate die rolls are independent: the second roll does not care what the first showed.

TrapMutually exclusive and independent are different ideas. If A and B are mutually exclusive and both have positive probability, they are not independent, because knowing A happened tells you B did not.

Key ideaIndependence is a claim you must check, not assume. Compare P(A ∩ B) with P(A) × P(B).

Two fair coins are tossed. Are "first is heads" and "second is heads" independent?

Yes. P(both) = 1/4 and P(first) × P(second) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4, so they are equal.

P(A) = 0.5, P(B) = 0.4, P(A ∩ B) = 0.2. Are A and B independent?

Yes. P(A) × P(B) = 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.2, which equals P(A ∩ B).

Part 3: Bayes theorem

Bayes theorem lets you reverse a conditional probability. Often we know how likely the evidence is when a cause is present, and we want the reverse: how likely the cause is, given the evidence.

The law of total probability

Suppose the causes A1 and A2 form a partition, so exactly one of them happens. Any event B can then be split into two parts:

P(B) = P(B | A1) P(A1) + P(B | A2) P(A2)

Bayes theorem

P(Ai | B) = P(B | Ai) P(Ai) / P(B)
  • P(Ai) is the prior: how likely the cause was before the evidence.
  • P(B | Ai) is the likelihood: how likely the evidence is if that cause is true.
  • P(B) is the total chance of the evidence, found from the law of total probability.
  • P(Ai | B) is the posterior: the updated chance of the cause.

A worked example, every step shown

A factory has two machines. Machine A makes 60 percent of the items, and 2 percent of its items are defective. Machine B makes 40 percent of the items, and 5 percent of its items are defective. An item is picked at random and is defective. What is the probability that it came from machine B?

Step 1: Name the events. A and B are the machines. D is "item is defective". We want P(B | D).

Step 2: Priors and likelihoods. P(A) = 0.6, P(B) = 0.4, P(D | A) = 0.02, P(D | B) = 0.05.

Step 3: Total probability. P(D) = 0.02 × 0.6 + 0.05 × 0.4 = 0.012 + 0.020 = 0.032.

Step 4: Bayes. P(B | D) = (0.05 × 0.4)/0.032 = 0.020/0.032 = 0.625 = 5/8.

Step 5: Check. Machine B makes only 40 percent of the items, but its defect rate is higher, so it has a bigger share of the defective items than its 40 percent. 0.625 is above 0.4, which makes sense.

from fractions import Fraction

pA, pB = Fraction(6, 10), Fraction(4, 10)
pD_A, pD_B = Fraction(2, 100), Fraction(5, 100)
pD = pD_A * pA + pD_B * pB
posterior_B = pD_B * pB / pD
print("P(defective) =", pD)
print("P(from B | defective) =", posterior_B, "=", float(posterior_B))
P(defective) = 4/125
P(from B | defective) = 5/8 = 0.625

GATE loves thisThe denominator P(B) is the total probability of the evidence. Forgetting to add up all the causes is the most common Bayes mistake in exams.

TrapP(B | A) is not the same as P(A | B). For example, the chance that a defective item came from B is not the chance that a machine-B item is defective.

What is the denominator in Bayes theorem called?

It is P(B), the total probability of the evidence, found by summing over all causes.

Why is 0.625 greater than 0.4 for machine B?

Because machine B has a higher defect rate, so defective items are over-represented among its output.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original, written in GATE style, and are not past exam questions. Try each one before reading the answer.

Addition with overlap

P(A) = 0.3, P(B) = 0.5 and P(A ∩ B) = 0.1. Find P(A ∪ B).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The probability that A or B or both happen.
  2. Which idea applies. The addition rule, which subtracts the overlap.
  3. Work it out. P(A ∪ B) = 0.3 + 0.5 − 0.1 = 0.7.
  4. Check. The answer is at least as large as the bigger of P(A) and P(B), which is 0.5, and it is at most 1.

Answer: 0.7.

At least one defect

Three items are checked. Each item is defective with probability 0.1, independently of the others. Find the probability that at least one item is defective.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. P(at least one defective).
  2. Which idea applies. Complement: P(at least one) = 1 − P(none).
  3. Work it out. P(none) = 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.729. So P(at least one) = 1 − 0.729 = 0.271.
  4. Check. The answer is between 0 and 1 and is roughly 3 × 0.1, which fits a small chance per item.

Answer: 0.271.

Which statements are correct?

P(A) = 0.5, P(B) = 0.4 and P(A ∩ B) = 0.2. Select all statements that are correct.

(A) A and B are independent.
(B) P(A | B) = 0.5.
(C) A and B are mutually exclusive.
(D) P(A ∪ B) = 0.7.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Test four claims.
  2. Which idea applies. Independence needs P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B). Conditional is P(A ∩ B)/P(B). Mutually exclusive needs P(A ∩ B) = 0. Addition rule for the union.
  3. Work it out. A: 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.2, which equals P(A ∩ B), so true. B: 0.2/0.4 = 0.5, so true. C: P(A ∩ B) = 0.2 is not 0, so false. D: 0.5 + 0.4 − 0.2 = 0.7, so true.
  4. Check. A and B are independent yet not mutually exclusive, which confirms that these are different ideas.

Answer: (A), (B) and (D).

Probability of two aces

Two cards are drawn from a standard 52-card deck without replacement. Find the probability that both are aces, correct to 4 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. P(both aces) for two draws without replacement.
  2. Which idea applies. Multiply the conditional chances: first draw an ace, then another ace from the remaining deck.
  3. Work it out. P(first ace) = 4/52. Given that, P(second ace) = 3/51. Product = (4/52) × (3/51) = 12/2652 = 1/221 ≈ 0.0045.
  4. Check. The second draw has one fewer card and one fewer ace, which is why 3/51 is used, not 4/52.

Answer: 0.0045.

Conditional on at least one head

Two fair coins are tossed. Given that at least one head appears, what is the probability that both are heads? Give the answer as a fraction.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. P(both heads | at least one head).
  2. Which idea applies. Conditional probability, P(A ∩ B)/P(B).
  3. Work it out. The outcomes are HH, HT, TH, TT, each with probability 1/4. P(at least one head) = 3/4. P(both heads and at least one head) = P(HH) = 1/4. So the conditional probability is (1/4)/(3/4) = 1/3.
  4. Check. Of the three equally likely outcomes with a head (HH, HT, TH), only one is HH, so 1/3 is right.

Answer: 1/3.

Which machine made the defective part?

Three machines make parts. Machine M1 makes 50 percent of the parts with a defect rate of 1 percent. Machine M2 makes 30 percent with a defect rate of 2 percent. Machine M3 makes 20 percent with a defect rate of 4 percent. A part is found defective. Find P(M3 | defective) as a fraction.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The chance that the defective part came from M3.
  2. Which idea applies. Bayes theorem with the law of total probability in the denominator.
  3. Work it out. Products: M1 gives 0.5 × 0.01 = 0.005, M2 gives 0.3 × 0.02 = 0.006, M3 gives 0.2 × 0.04 = 0.008. Total P(defective) = 0.019. Then P(M3 | defective) = 0.008/0.019 = 8/19.
  4. Check. The three machine shares of defectives, 0.005, 0.006 and 0.008, add to 0.019, the same denominator. 8/19 is about 0.42, which is larger than M3's 20 percent share, as expected.

Answer: 8/19.

A diagnostic test

A disease affects 1 percent of a population. A test is positive for 95 percent of people who have the disease, and positive for 5 percent of people who do not. A person tests positive. Find the probability that the person has the disease, correct to 3 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. P(disease | positive).
  2. Which idea applies. Bayes theorem, with P(positive) from total probability.
  3. Work it out. P(D) = 0.01, P(+ | D) = 0.95, P(+ | not D) = 0.05. P(+) = 0.95 × 0.01 + 0.05 × 0.99 = 0.0095 + 0.0495 = 0.059. So P(D | +) = 0.0095/0.059 ≈ 0.161.
  4. Check. The test is accurate, yet most positives are false alarms because the disease is rare. A value of 0.161 is well below 0.95, which shows this effect.

Answer: 0.161.

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Bayes for a medical test (NAT, 2 marks)

A disease affects 1% of people. A test detects it with probability 0.95 and gives a false positive with probability 0.05. A person tests positive. Find the probability they have the disease, correct to 3 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. P(D | +).
  2. Which idea applies. Bayes: P(D | +) = P(+ | D)P(D) / P(+).
  3. Work it out. P(+) = 0.95·0.01 + 0.05·0.99 = 0.0095 + 0.0495 = 0.059. P(D | +) = 0.0095/0.059 = 0.161.
  4. Check. Most positives come from the large healthy group, which is why the answer is low.
print("%.3f" % (0.95 * 0.01 / (0.95 * 0.01 + 0.05 * 0.99)))

0.161

Answer: about 0.161.

Exam-level 2: Independence versus disjointness (MSQ, 2 marks)

A and B are events with P(A) = 0.4, P(B) = 0.5. Which are TRUE? (A) If A and B are independent, P(A ∪ B) = 0.7. (B) If A and B are disjoint, they are independent. (C) If A and B are independent, A and Bᶜ are independent. (D) P(A ∩ B) ≤ 0.4.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements about union and independence hold.
  2. Which idea applies. Independence means P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B). Disjoint events with positive probability cannot be independent.
  3. Work it out. (A) 0.4 + 0.5 − 0.2 = 0.7. True. (B) P(A ∩ B) = 0 but P(A)P(B) = 0.2. False. (C) P(A ∩ Bᶜ) = 0.4 − 0.2 = 0.2 = 0.4·0.5. True. (D) A ∩ B is a subset of A. True.
  4. Check. Only (B) confuses two different ideas.

Answer: (A), (C) and (D).

Exam-level 3: Two boxes (NAT, 2 marks)

Box 1 has 3 red and 2 blue balls. Box 2 has 1 red and 4 blue. A fair coin picks a box and one ball is drawn. Given the ball is red, find the probability it came from box 1.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A posterior probability.
  2. Which idea applies. Bayes with two equally likely boxes.
  3. Work it out. P(red) = 0.5·3/5 + 0.5·1/5 = 0.4. P(box 1 | red) = 0.3/0.4 = 0.75.
  4. Check. Box 1 holds 3 of the 4 red balls and both boxes are equally likely, so 3/4 makes sense.

Answer: 0.75.

Summary

Sample space
The set of all possible outcomes.
Event
A subset of the sample space.
Axioms
P(A) ≥ 0, P(S) = 1, and additivity for mutually exclusive events.
Complement
P(Ac) = 1 − P(A).
Addition rule
P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B).
Mutually exclusive
Events with P(A ∩ B) = 0.
Conditional probability
P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B), for P(B) greater than 0.
Independence
P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B).
Partition
Mutually exclusive events that together cover the sample space.
Law of total probability
P(B) is the sum of P(B | Ai) P(Ai) over a partition.
Bayes theorem
P(Ai | B) = P(B | Ai) P(Ai)/P(B).
Prior
The probability of a cause before the evidence.
Posterior
The updated probability of a cause after the evidence.

Remember these

  1. Probabilities lie between 0 and 1, and the whole sample space has probability 1.
  2. For "A or B", add and subtract the overlap.
  3. For "at least one", use 1 minus the probability of none.
  4. Conditional probability divides the joint probability by the probability of the condition.
  5. Independence must be checked with P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B).
  6. In Bayes theorem, the denominator is the total probability of the evidence.
  7. P(A | B) and P(B | A) are different numbers.

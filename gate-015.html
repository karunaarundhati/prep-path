Quadratic forms

A quadratic form is a squared-type expression in several variables, written with a symmetric matrix. Whether it is always positive, always negative or mixed decides the shape of a bowl, a hill or a saddle, and it is the key test for optimisation and the Hessian in data science.

The idea in plain words

Think of a landscape described by a height function. Stand at the origin, which is the lowest point of a bowl. Every step away from the origin raises you. That shape is a bowl, and the height function is positive definite. Turn the bowl upside down and every step lowers you: that is a hill, or negative definite. A saddle, like a horse saddle, goes up in one direction and down in another. That is indefinite.

The height functions in these pictures are quadratic forms. A quadratic form is an expression where every term has total degree 2, such as 2x² + 6xy + 3y². Each one can be written compactly as xᵀAx, where A is a symmetric matrix. The shape of the bowl, hill or saddle is hidden in the numbers of A.

Why does GATE care? Quadratic forms are the core of optimisation in data science. The Hessian matrix, which holds the second derivatives of a cost function, is a symmetric matrix. Testing whether it is positive definite tells you if a point is a minimum. Many GATE DA questions ask exactly this.

Key terms

Quadratic form
An expression q(x) = xᵀAx, where every term has degree 2 in the entries of x.
Symmetric matrix
A square matrix equal to its transpose: A = Aᵀ. Its off-diagonal entries are mirror images.
Positive definite (PD)
xᵀAx is greater than 0 for every non-zero x.
Negative definite (ND)
xᵀAx is less than 0 for every non-zero x.
Positive semidefinite (PSD)
xᵀAx is at least 0 for every x, and equals 0 for some non-zero x.
Negative semidefinite (NSD)
xᵀAx is at most 0 for every x, and equals 0 for some non-zero x.
Indefinite
The form takes both positive and negative values.
Eigenvalue
A number λ with Av = λv for some non-zero v. Symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues.
Leading principal minor
The determinant of the top-left k × k block of A, for k = 1, 2, ..., n.
Hessian
The matrix of second partial derivatives of a function. It is symmetric.
Conic section
An ellipse, parabola or hyperbola, the shapes obtained by slicing a cone.

Step 1: From a quadratic expression to a symmetric matrix

Start with a vector x = (x, y), written as a column. The product xᵀAx is a number. For a 2 × 2 symmetric matrix A = [[a, b], [b, c]]:

xᵀAx = [x y] [[a, b], [b, c]] [x; y] = ax² + 2bxy + cy²

Notice that the off-diagonal entry b appears twice, once for each of the two cross terms. That is why the matrix must be symmetric: the cross term 2bxy is split evenly between the two off-diagonal positions.

Worked example. Write q(x, y) = 2x² + 6xy + 3y² as xᵀAx.

  1. The coefficient of x² is 2, so a = 2.
  2. The coefficient of y² is 3, so c = 3.
  3. The cross term 6xy must equal 2bxy, so 2b = 6 and b = 3.
  4. So A = [[2, 3], [3, 3]].
  5. Check: [x y] [[2, 3], [3, 3]] [x; y] = 2x² + 3xy + 3xy + 3y² = 2x² + 6xy + 3y². Correct.

Rule of thumb: the diagonal entries are the coefficients of the squared terms, and each off-diagonal entry is half the coefficient of the cross term.

TrapDo not put the full cross coefficient 6 in the matrix. That gives [[2, 6], [6, 3]], which has the wrong form. The entries must be half the cross coefficient.

Question: what symmetric matrix represents q = x² − 4xy + y²?

[[1, −2], [−2, 1]]. The cross coefficient −4 is split into two entries of −2.

Question: evaluate xᵀAx for A = [[1, 0], [0, 1]] and x = (3, 4).

x² + y² = 9 + 16 = 25. The identity matrix gives the squared length of x.

Step 2: Definite, semidefinite and indefinite forms

A quadratic form is classified by the sign of its value at every non-zero x. Here are the four main types with simple examples:

  • Positive definite: q = x² + y². It is positive at every point except the origin. This is the bowl.
  • Negative definite: q = −x² − y². It is negative everywhere except the origin. This is the hill.
  • Positive semidefinite: q = x². It is zero along the whole y-axis, and never negative. It is a flat trough.
  • Indefinite: q = x² − y². It is positive along the x-axis and negative along the y-axis. This is the saddle.

The zero vector always gives q = 0. The classification concerns non-zero x only, because including x = 0 would make every form look semidefinite.

Key ideaDefinite means the sign never changes except at the origin. Indefinite means the sign changes. Semidefinite means the sign never changes, but there is a non-zero direction where the form gives zero.

Take the form q = 2x² + 6xy + 3y² from Step 1. Try x = (1, 0): q = 2. Try x = (0, 1): q = 3. Try x = (1, −1): q = 2 − 6 + 3 = −1. The value is negative, so q takes both signs. It is indefinite. That matches the saddle shape.

Question: is the form x² + 2xy + y² positive definite?

No. It equals (x + y)², which is zero along the line x = −y. So it is positive semidefinite, not positive definite.

Step 3: Testing with eigenvalues

For a symmetric matrix, the classification is controlled by its eigenvalues. The spectral theorem says that a real symmetric matrix can be written as A = QΛQᵀ, with Q orthogonal and Λ diagonal, holding the eigenvalues. Substituting y = Qᵀx turns the form into a sum of squares:

xᵀAx = λ1y1² + λ2y2² + ... + λnyn²

Each square has a fixed sign, so the signs of the eigenvalues decide everything:

  • Positive definite: all eigenvalues are greater than 0.
  • Negative definite: all eigenvalues are less than 0.
  • Positive semidefinite: all eigenvalues are at least 0, with at least one equal to 0.
  • Negative semidefinite: all eigenvalues are at most 0, with at least one equal to 0.
  • Indefinite: some eigenvalue is positive and some is negative.

For a 2 × 2 symmetric matrix [[a, b], [b, c]], the eigenvalues come from λ² − (a + c)λ + (ac − b²) = 0. The code below computes them for three matrices.

import math

def eig_sym2(a, b, c):
    tr = a + c
    det = a * c - b * b
    r = math.sqrt(tr * tr - 4 * det)
    return (tr + r) / 2, (tr - r) / 2

for name, (a, b, c) in [("[[2,1],[1,2]]", (2, 1, 2)), ("[[2,3],[3,3]]", (2, 3, 3)), ("[[1,2],[2,1]]", (1, 2, 1))]:
    hi, lo = eig_sym2(a, b, c)
    print(name, "eigenvalues:", round(hi, 6), round(lo, 6))
[[2,1],[1,2]] eigenvalues: 3.0 1.0
[[2,3],[3,3]] eigenvalues: 5.541381 -0.541381
[[1,2],[2,1]] eigenvalues: 3.0 -1.0

The first matrix has both eigenvalues positive, so it is positive definite. The second has one positive and one negative eigenvalue, so it is indefinite, which matches the sign change found in Step 2. The third is also indefinite.

GATE loves thisIf a question gives the eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix, the classification is immediate: look only at their signs.

Question: a symmetric matrix has eigenvalues 4 and 0. What type is it?

Positive semidefinite, because all eigenvalues are at least 0 and one of them is 0.

Step 4: Sylvester's criterion with leading minors

Eigenvalues need a calculation. Sylvester's criterion gives a test using only determinants. Compute the leading principal minors D1, D2, ..., Dn, where Dk is the determinant of the top-left k × k block of A. Then:

  • Positive definite if and only if D1 > 0, D2 > 0, ..., Dn > 0.
  • Negative definite if and only if the signs alternate, starting with negative: D1 < 0, D2 > 0, D3 < 0, and so on.

For a 2 × 2 matrix [[a, b], [b, c]], these become simple: PD if a > 0 and ac − b² > 0, and ND if a < 0 and ac − b² > 0.

Worked example. Test A = [[2, 3], [3, 3]].

  1. D1 = 2, which is positive.
  2. D2 = 2 · 3 − 3 · 3 = 6 − 9 = −3, which is negative.
  3. Not PD, because D2 must be positive. Not ND, because D1 must be negative.
  4. So A is indefinite. This agrees with the eigenvalue result in Step 3.

TrapSylvester's criterion with leading minors proves positive or negative definiteness only. For semidefinite forms, you need all principal minors, not just the leading ones. The matrix [[0, 0], [0, −1]] has leading minors 0 and 0, which settle nothing, yet it is negative semidefinite. Its eigenvalues are 0 and −1.

A useful check for semidefinite forms: for NSD, every 1 × 1 principal minor (each diagonal entry) must be at most 0, and every 2 × 2 principal minor must be at least 0. For [[0, 0], [0, −1]], the diagonal entries are 0 and −1, both at most 0, and the determinant is 0. So it is NSD.

Question: is [[5, 2], [2, 1]] positive definite?

D1 = 5 > 0 and D2 = 5 − 4 = 1 > 0. Both are positive, so yes, it is positive definite.

Question: is [[−2, 1], [1, −3]] negative definite?

D1 = −2 < 0 and D2 = 6 − 1 = 5 > 0. The signs alternate, so yes, it is negative definite.

Step 5: Conic sections and the Hessian

Set a quadratic form equal to 1 and you get a curve. Its shape tells you the type of the form:

  • Positive definite gives an ellipse. For example, x² + 4y² = 1 is an ellipse with semi-axes 1 and 1/2.
  • Indefinite gives a hyperbola. For example, x² − y² = 1 is a hyperbola with two branches.

This is the same story as the bowl and saddle in the introduction. Ellipses are closed bowls, and hyperbolas open out in two directions.

The most important application in data science is the Hessian. Near a point where the slope of a function is zero, the function behaves like a quadratic form in the small change h. The matrix H of second derivatives controls that form. So:

  • If H is positive definite, the point is a strict local minimum.
  • If H is negative definite, the point is a strict local maximum.
  • If H is indefinite, the point is a saddle.

Worked example. Let f(x, y) = x² + xy + y². The second derivatives are fxx = 2, fxy = 1 and fyy = 2, so H = [[2, 1], [1, 2]]. Then D1 = 2 > 0 and D2 = 4 − 1 = 3 > 0. H is positive definite, so the origin is a strict local minimum. Indeed, f(0, 0) = 0 and f is positive everywhere else.

Key ideaThe Hessian is a symmetric matrix, so every test in this topic applies to it: eigenvalues, leading minors and definiteness.

Question: why does positive definiteness of the Hessian give a minimum rather than a maximum?

Positive definiteness means the function rises in every direction away from the critical point, like the bottom of a bowl. Rising in all directions means the critical point is the lowest nearby point, which is a minimum.

Solved GATE-style problems

Try each problem before reading the answer. Every problem here is a GATE-style practice problem.

Matrix of a quadratic form

Write q(x, y) = x² + 4xy + 2y² as xᵀAx, where A is symmetric.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A symmetric 2 × 2 matrix A.
  2. Which idea applies. Diagonal entries are the squared coefficients. Off-diagonal entries are half the cross coefficient.
  3. Work it out. The x² coefficient is 1, so a = 1. The y² coefficient is 2, so c = 2. The cross coefficient is 4, so each off-diagonal entry is 4/2 = 2. So A = [[1, 2], [2, 2]].
  4. Check. [x y][[1, 2], [2, 2]][x; y] = x² + 2xy + 2xy + 2y² = x² + 4xy + 2y².

Answer: A = [[1, 2], [2, 2]].

Positive definite?

Is the quadratic form 3x² − 2xy + 3y² positive definite? Justify with a determinant test.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A yes or no, with a reason.
  2. Which idea applies. Sylvester's criterion: D1 and D2 must both be positive.
  3. Work it out. The matrix is [[3, −1], [−1, 3]]. D1 = 3 > 0. D2 = 9 − 1 = 8 > 0.
  4. Check. The eigenvalues are 3 ± 1, which are 4 and 2, both positive. Consistent.

Answer: yes, it is positive definite.

Which forms are positive semidefinite? (MSQ)

Select all matrices that are positive semidefinite.

(A) [[1, 0], [0, 0]]   (B) [[1, 2], [2, 1]]   (C) [[2, 0], [0, 5]]   (D) [[0, 0], [0, −1]]

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which matrices have all eigenvalues at least 0.
  2. Which idea applies. Use the eigenvalues, which are easy for diagonal matrices and 2 × 2 matrices.
  3. Work it out. (A) is diagonal with entries 1 and 0, so the eigenvalues are 1 and 0. PSD. (B) has eigenvalues 3 and −1, so it is indefinite. (C) is diagonal with entries 2 and 5, so PD, which implies PSD. (D) has eigenvalues 0 and −1, so it is negative semidefinite, not positive.
  4. Check. A quick sanity check on (D): the form is −y², which is never positive.

Answer: (A) and (C).

Counting integer values of k (NAT)

For how many integer values of k is the matrix [[1, k], [k, 4]] positive definite? Give the count as an integer.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The number of integers k that make the matrix PD.
  2. Which idea applies. Sylvester's criterion: D1 > 0 and D2 > 0.
  3. Work it out. D1 = 1 is always positive. D2 = 4 − k² must be positive, so k² < 4, which means −2 < k < 2. The integers are −1, 0 and 1.
  4. Check. At k = 2, D2 = 0, so the matrix is only PSD, and k = 2 is excluded. The count is therefore 3.

Answer: 3.

Value of a quadratic form (NAT)

Let A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]]. Evaluate xᵀAx at x = (1, −1). Give the answer as an integer.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The number xᵀAx at a specific vector.
  2. Which idea applies. Expand the form as 2x² + 2xy + 2y², then substitute.
  3. Work it out. With x = 1 and y = −1: 2(1)² + 2(1)(−1) + 2(−1)² = 2 − 2 + 2 = 2.
  4. Check. Compute Ax first: Ax = (2 − 1, 1 − 2) = (1, −1). Then xᵀ(Ax) = 1·1 + (−1)(−1) = 2. Same answer.

Answer: 2.

Classify from eigenvalues

A symmetric matrix has eigenvalues 0, 2 and 5. Is it positive definite, positive semidefinite, or neither? Justify.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The strongest type that fits.
  2. Which idea applies. Look at the signs of the eigenvalues. PD needs all strictly positive.
  3. Work it out. All eigenvalues are at least 0, so the matrix is PSD. But one eigenvalue is 0, so it is not PD. The determinant is 0 times 2 times 5 = 0, which confirms it is singular.
  4. Check. There is a non-zero vector (an eigenvector for 0) on which the form gives zero, which is exactly the defining feature of PSD but not PD.

Answer: positive semidefinite, but not positive definite.

For which k is the matrix positive definite? (NAT)

Let A = [[2, 0, 1], [0, 2, 0], [1, 0, k]]. For which real k is A positive definite? Give the boundary value k0, as a fraction, such that A is positive definite exactly when k > k0.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The condition on k, expressed through the boundary value.
  2. Which idea applies. Sylvester's criterion: D1, D2, D3 must all be positive.
  3. Work it out. D1 = 2 > 0. D2 = 2·2 − 0 = 4 > 0. D3 = det A. Expanding along the second row, det A = 2 · det[[2, 1], [1, k]] = 2(2k − 1) = 4k − 2. D3 > 0 requires k > 1/2. So the boundary is k = 1/2, which is excluded.
  4. Check. The code below evaluates det A at four values of k. The value is zero at k = 1/2, negative below it and positive above it.
def det3(m):
    a, b, c = m[0]
    d, e, f = m[1]
    g, h, i = m[2]
    return a * (e * i - f * h) - b * (d * i - f * g) + c * (d * h - e * g)

for k in [0, 0.5, 1, 2]:
    A = [[2, 0, 1], [0, 2, 0], [1, 0, k]]
    print("k =", k, "D3 =", det3(A))
k = 0 D3 = -2
k = 0.5 D3 = 0.0
k = 1 D3 = 2
k = 2 D3 = 6

Answer: k0 = 1/2. A is positive definite exactly when k > 1/2.

Hessian of a cost function

Let f(x, y) = x² − xy + 2y². The origin is a critical point of f. What kind of point is it? Use the Hessian.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Minimum, maximum or saddle at the origin.
  2. Which idea applies. The Hessian H of second derivatives, then Sylvester's criterion.
  3. Work it out. fxx = 2, fxy = −1, fyy = 4. So H = [[2, −1], [−1, 4]]. D1 = 2 > 0 and D2 = 8 − 1 = 7 > 0. H is positive definite.
  4. Check. Completing the square gives f = (x − y/2)² + (7/4)y². This is never negative and is zero only at the origin, which confirms a strict minimum.

Answer: a strict local minimum.

Which values make the matrix positive definite? (MSQ)

Let A = [[a, 1], [1, a]]. Select all values of a for which A is positive definite.

(A) a = 0   (B) a = 1   (C) a = 2   (D) a = 3

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which of four specific values of a give PD.
  2. Which idea applies. Sylvester's criterion: a > 0 and a² − 1 > 0.
  3. Work it out. (A) a = 0 gives D1 = 0, which is not positive, so not PD. (B) a = 1 gives D2 = 1 − 1 = 0, so only PSD. (C) a = 2 gives D1 = 2 and D2 = 4 − 1 = 3, both positive: PD. (D) a = 3 gives D1 = 3 and D2 = 9 − 1 = 8: PD.
  4. Check. The eigenvalues are a + 1 and a − 1. For a = 2 they are 3 and 1, and for a = 3 they are 4 and 2, all positive.

Answer: (C) and (D).

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Positive definite range (NAT, 2 marks)

Consider the quadratic form Q(x, y, z) = x² + 2y² + 3z² + 2axy. Find the sum of all integers a for which Q is positive definite.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The integer values of a that keep Q positive definite, then their sum.
  2. Which idea applies. The matrix is [[1, a, 0], [a, 2, 0], [0, 0, 3]]. Use Sylvester's criterion: all leading principal minors positive.
  3. Work it out. Minors: 1 > 0, 2 − a² > 0, 3(2 − a²) > 0. So a² < 2, giving integers a = −1, 0, 1. Sum = 0.
  4. Check. The code tests each integer with the minors.
ok = [a for a in range(-5, 6) if 1 > 0 and 2 - a * a > 0 and 3 * (2 - a * a) > 0]
print(ok, "sum =", sum(ok))

[-1, 0, 1] sum = 0

Answer: 0 (a = −1, 0, 1).

Exam-level 2: Minimum on the unit circle (NAT, 2 marks)

Find the minimum value of Q(x, y) = 5x² + 4xy + 2y² subject to x² + y² = 1.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The smallest value of the form on the unit circle.
  2. Which idea applies. By the Rayleigh quotient, the minimum is the smallest eigenvalue of the symmetric matrix [[5, 2], [2, 2]].
  3. Work it out. λ² − 7λ + 6 = 0 gives λ = 1 and 6. Minimum = 1.
  4. Check. The code samples the circle.
import math
print("min = %.4f" % min(5*math.cos(t)**2 + 4*math.cos(t)*math.sin(t) + 2*math.sin(t)**2 for t in [k * 2 * math.pi / 100000 for k in range(100000)]))

min = 1.0000

Answer: 1.

Exam-level 3: Classifying a form (MCQ, 2 marks)

The form Q = x² + y² + z² + 4xy + 4yz + 4xz is (A) positive definite (B) negative definite (C) indefinite (D) positive semidefinite.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The sign pattern of the eigenvalues of its matrix.
  2. Which idea applies. The matrix is I + 2(J − I) = 2J − I where J is the all ones 3 × 3 matrix. J has eigenvalues 3, 0, 0.
  3. Work it out. Eigenvalues: 2·3 − 1 = 5 and 2·0 − 1 = −1 (twice). Mixed signs, so indefinite.
  4. Check. Q(1, −1, 0) = 1 + 1 − 4 = −2 and Q(1, 1, 1) = 15.

Answer: (C).

Summary

Quadratic form
xᵀAx, an expression of degree 2 in the entries of x.
Symmetric matrix
A square matrix equal to its transpose. Quadratic forms are always written with one.
Positive definite
xᵀAx > 0 for every non-zero x. A bowl.
Negative definite
xᵀAx < 0 for every non-zero x. A hill.
Positive semidefinite
xᵀAx ≥ 0 for every x, with some non-zero x giving zero.
Negative semidefinite
xᵀAx ≤ 0 for every x, with some non-zero x giving zero.
Indefinite
The form takes both signs. A saddle.
Eigenvalue test
All eigenvalues positive gives PD. All negative gives ND. Mixed signs give indefinite.
Leading principal minor
The determinant of a top-left block of A.
Sylvester's criterion
PD exactly when all leading principal minors are positive.
Spectral theorem
A symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors.
Hessian
The symmetric matrix of second derivatives of a function.
Ellipse
The level curve q = 1 of a positive definite form.
Hyperbola
The level curve q = 1 of an indefinite form.
Strict local minimum
A critical point where the Hessian is positive definite.
Saddle point
A critical point where the Hessian is indefinite.

Remember these

  1. Off-diagonal entries of the matrix are half the cross coefficients.
  2. Definiteness is about the sign of xᵀAx for non-zero x.
  3. Sylvester's criterion proves positive or negative definiteness using leading minors.
  4. For semidefinite forms, leading minors are not enough. Check all principal minors or use eigenvalues.
  5. Positive definite gives an ellipse, indefinite gives a hyperbola.
  6. A positive definite Hessian at a critical point means a strict local minimum.

Rotation matrices in 2D and 3D

A rotation matrix is a grid of numbers that turns a point or an arrow about the origin without stretching it. Robots use rotation matrices to describe how each link is turned relative to the next. This topic builds the 2D and 3D rotation matrices, shows their properties, and explains why the order of rotations matters.

The idea in plain words

Imagine an arrow drawn on a sheet of paper, with its tail at the centre of the page. Turn the paper by 30 degrees. The arrow turns with it, but its length stays the same. A rotation keeps lengths and angles, and only changes direction. A rotation matrix is a small table of numbers that does this turning when we multiply it by the coordinates of a point.

Why use a table of numbers at all? A computer can multiply numbers quickly and reliably. A robot may need to turn points thousands of times a second, so a matrix is the natural tool. Once we know the matrix, we can also chain several turns together in one step.

Everyday analogy. Think of a dance. Each dancer turns on the spot, and the turn is the same for each person in the group. A rotation matrix is like the instruction "turn left by 90 degrees" written as numbers, so a computer can apply it to every dancer at once.

Why it matters. Each joint of a robot arm turns one link relative to the previous one. Rotation matrices give the orientation of every link and of the tool. Without them, we could not describe where the gripper is pointing.

Where it appears in GATE. In the Robotics and Automation (RA) paper you may be asked for the rotated coordinates of a point, for a property of a rotation matrix, or for the effect of two rotations in a given order. These questions reward careful, step-by-step arithmetic.

Key terms

Rotation matrix
A square array of numbers that rotates a vector about the origin, keeping its length. Plain meaning: a turning instruction written as a table.
Matrix
A rectangular array of numbers arranged in rows and columns. A 2 by 2 matrix has two rows and two columns.
Vector
A quantity with a size and a direction, written as a list of numbers such as (x, y) or (x, y, z).
Angle θ
The amount of turning, in degrees or radians. The conversion is radians = degrees × π/180.
Counterclockwise positive
The convention that a turn in the counterclockwise direction is a positive angle when seen from above the axis.
Orthogonal matrix
A matrix whose transpose is its inverse. Rotation matrices are orthogonal.
Transpose (AT)
The matrix formed by swapping rows and columns. Plain meaning: flip the table over its diagonal.
Identity matrix (I)
A square matrix with 1 on the diagonal and 0 elsewhere. Multiplying by I changes nothing.
Determinant (det)
A single number computed from a square matrix. For a 2 by 2 matrix [[a, b], [c, d]] it equals ad − bc.
Matrix product (composition)
Multiplying two rotation matrices gives a single rotation equal to doing both one after the other.
Euler angles (roll, pitch, yaw)
Three angles that describe an orientation as three successive rotations about the X, Y and Z axes.

Rotating a point in 2D

Starting from a picture

Take a point at (x, y), and draw the arrow from the origin to it. Let the arrow have length r and make angle φ with the x-axis. Then x = r cos φ and y = r sin φ. Now rotate the arrow counterclockwise by an angle θ. The new arrow has length r and angle φ + θ, so its new coordinates are

x′ = r cos(φ + θ) = x cos θ − y sin θ
y′ = r sin(φ + θ) = x sin θ + y cos θ

The second form comes from the sine and cosine addition rules applied to r cos(φ + θ) and r sin(φ + θ). The two lines can be written as one multiplication. Here x′ and y′ are the new coordinates, and θ is the counterclockwise angle.

[ x′ ]   [ cos θ  −sin θ ] [ x ]
[ y′ ] = [ sin θ   cos θ ] [ y ]

The square array is the 2D rotation matrix R(θ). Its rows are written left to right, and the column of (x, y) is multiplied by it.

Try the picture below. Look at the orange square grid as it turns. Then drag the column vectors and notice how the grid stays square and never stretches.

A check with 90 degrees

For θ = 90°, cos θ = 0 and sin θ = 1. So R(90°) = [[0, −1], [1, 0]]. Apply it to (1, 0): x′ = 0 × 1 + (−1) × 0 = 0 and y′ = 1 × 1 + 0 × 0 = 1. The point (1, 0) moves to (0, 1), a quarter turn counterclockwise. The check works.

Worked example

Rotate the point (2, 1) by θ = 30°. Use cos 30° = 0.866 and sin 30° = 0.5.

  1. x′ = x cos θ − y sin θ = 2 × 0.866 − 1 × 0.5 = 1.732 − 0.5 = 1.232.
  2. y′ = x sin θ + y cos θ = 2 × 0.5 + 1 × 0.866 = 1 + 0.866 = 1.866.
  3. The new point is (1.23, 1.87), to two decimals.
import math
t = math.radians(30)
R = [[math.cos(t), -math.sin(t)],
     [math.sin(t),  math.cos(t)]]
x, y = 2.0, 1.0
xp = R[0][0] * x + R[0][1] * y
yp = R[1][0] * x + R[1][1] * y
print(f"rotated (2,1) = ({xp:.2f}, {yp:.2f})")
rotated (2,1) = (1.23, 1.87)

TrapThe sign of the sine term is easy to get wrong. In the first row it is minus, and in the second row it is plus. A quick check with 90 degrees, as above, catches the error.

What are the coordinates of (1, 0) after a counterclockwise rotation of 90°?

(0, 1). The point moves a quarter turn up to the y-axis.

What is the 2D rotation matrix for θ = 180°?

cos 180° = −1 and sin 180° = 0, so R = [[−1, 0], [0, −1]], which is −I. Every point is reflected through the origin.

Properties of rotation matrices

Why these properties matter

Rotation matrices have special properties that let us check our work and speed up calculations. Three properties are used most often in GATE questions.

  • Lengths are preserved. A rotation keeps the size of a vector. So |R v| = |v|.
  • Determinant is 1. A proper rotation has det R = +1. A reflection would have det R = −1, and it is not a rotation.
  • Inverse equals transpose. R−1 = RT. Undoing a rotation is the same as rotating the other way, and the transpose gives that.
RT R = I, det R = 1, R−1 = RT

For the 2D matrix R(θ) = [[cos θ, −sin θ], [sin θ, cos θ]], the determinant is cos²θ + sin²θ = 1. Its transpose is [[cos θ, sin θ], [−sin θ, cos θ]], which is R(−θ).

import math
t = math.radians(30)
R = [[math.cos(t), -math.sin(t)],
     [math.sin(t),  math.cos(t)]]
det = R[0][0] * R[1][1] - R[0][1] * R[1][0]
x, y = 2.0, 1.0
xp = R[0][0] * x + R[0][1] * y
yp = R[1][0] * x + R[1][1] * y
print(f"det = {det:.2f}")
print(f"length before = {math.hypot(x, y):.4f}, after = {math.hypot(xp, yp):.4f}")
det = 1.00
length before = 2.2361, after = 2.2361

The first line confirms the determinant is 1. The second line confirms that the length is kept. Both checks are useful in GATE, where a wrong matrix often fails one of them.

In the next picture, try the 30 degree rotation. Watch what happens to the square grid, and try to drag the column vectors so that the determinant changes sign. The shape flips rather than turns, which is what a reflection does and not a rotation.

Key ideaA rotation matrix is orthogonal: its columns are unit length, and they are at right angles to each other. This is why its inverse is just its transpose.

What is the inverse of R(30°)?

Its transpose, which is R(−30°). Undoing a 30 degree turn is a 30 degree turn in the other direction.

Does a rotation matrix ever have determinant −1?

No. A determinant of −1 means the matrix reflects the plane, which is not a pure rotation. Proper rotations always have determinant +1.

Rotations about the X, Y and Z axes in 3D

Turning about one axis

In 3D, a rotation needs an axis. A rotation about the Z axis keeps the z-coordinate unchanged and turns the x and y coordinates in the plane. That is exactly the 2D rotation, with z added as a spectator. Rotations about X and Y work the same way, but each one keeps a different coordinate fixed.

Rz(θ) = [[cos θ, −sin θ, 0], [sin θ, cos θ, 0], [0, 0, 1]]
Rx(θ) = [[1, 0, 0], [0, cos θ, −sin θ], [0, sin θ, cos θ]]
Ry(θ) = [[cos θ, 0, sin θ], [0, 1, 0], [−sin θ, 0, cos θ]]

Read them with care. In Rz, the third row and column are (0, 0, 1), so z is kept. In Rx, the first row and column are (1, 0, 0), so x is kept. In Ry, the middle row and column are (0, 1, 0), so y is kept. The signs of the sines change position in each matrix, which is a common source of mistakes.

  Rotation about Z (seen from above, looking down the Z axis)

            y
            |
            |      x-y plane turns counterclockwise by θ
            |     /
            |   /
            | /
   ---------+---------- x

  z is unchanged: a point above the plane stays at the same height.

Worked example

Rotate the point (1, 0, 0) by 90° about Z. Use Rz(90°) = [[0, −1, 0], [1, 0, 0], [0, 0, 1]].

  1. x′ = 0 × 1 + (−1) × 0 + 0 × 0 = 0.
  2. y′ = 1 × 1 + 0 × 0 + 0 × 0 = 1.
  3. z′ = 0 × 1 + 0 × 0 + 1 × 0 = 0.
  4. The result is (0, 1, 0). The point moved from the x-axis to the y-axis, and stayed in the plane z = 0.
import math

def rot_z(t):
    c, s = math.cos(t), math.sin(t)
    return [[c, -s, 0], [s, c, 0], [0, 0, 1]]

def apply(R, p):
    return [sum(R[i][k] * p[k] for k in range(3)) for i in range(3)]

def clean(v):
    return [round(x, 6) + 0.0 for x in v]

print("Rz(90) applied to (1,0,0):", clean(apply(rot_z(math.pi / 2), [1, 0, 0])))
Rz(90) applied to (1,0,0): [0.0, 1.0, 0.0]

TrapRotation about Z does not change the z-coordinate. Students sometimes write a rotation about X that changes x, which is wrong. Rotation about X keeps x fixed.

Which coordinate does Rx(θ) keep unchanged?

The x-coordinate. The first row and column of Rx are (1, 0, 0).

What is Ry(0°)?

The identity matrix, because cos 0° = 1 and sin 0° = 0. No turning means no change.

Composing rotations and why order matters

Two turns in a row

Suppose a point is first rotated by a matrix A, and then by a matrix B. The final point is B(A p), which we can write as (BA) p. The product BA is a single matrix that does both turns in one step. Notice the order: the matrix on the left is applied last.

In 2D, two rotations about the origin commute, which means the order does not matter. R(θ) R(φ) = R(θ + φ) = R(φ) R(θ). In 3D this is not true. Rotations about different axes do not commute, so the order changes the answer.

R1 R2 ≠ R2 R1 in general (3D)

A worked example of order

Let A = Rx(90°) and B = Rz(90°). Apply the point p = (1, 0, 0) in two orders.

  1. Order 1, Rz first then Rx. Rz(90°) takes (1, 0, 0) to (0, 1, 0). Then Rx(90°) takes (0, 1, 0) to (0, 0, 1).
  2. Order 2, Rx first then Rz. Rx(90°) keeps (1, 0, 0) unchanged, because it lies on the x-axis. Then Rz(90°) takes (1, 0, 0) to (0, 1, 0).
  3. The two answers are (0, 0, 1) and (0, 1, 0). They are different, so the order matters.
import math

def rot_x(t):
    c, s = math.cos(t), math.sin(t)
    return [[1, 0, 0], [0, c, -s], [0, s, c]]

def rot_z(t):
    c, s = math.cos(t), math.sin(t)
    return [[c, -s, 0], [s, c, 0], [0, 0, 1]]

def matmul(A, B):
    return [[sum(A[i][k] * B[k][j] for k in range(3)) for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]

def apply(R, p):
    return [sum(R[i][k] * p[k] for k in range(3)) for i in range(3)]

def clean(v):
    return [round(x, 6) + 0.0 for x in v]

half_pi = math.pi / 2
p = [1, 0, 0]
print("Rz first, then Rx:", clean(apply(rot_x(half_pi), apply(rot_z(half_pi), p))))
print("Rx first, then Rz:", clean(apply(rot_z(half_pi), apply(rot_x(half_pi), p))))
print("matrix Rx*Rz applied:", clean(apply(matmul(rot_x(half_pi), rot_z(half_pi)), p)))
Rz first, then Rx: [0.0, 0.0, 1.0]
Rx first, then Rz: [0.0, 1.0, 0.0]
matrix Rx*Rz applied: [0.0, 0.0, 1.0]

TrapWhen you multiply matrices, the rightmost matrix acts first on the point. The product Rx·Rz therefore means "Rz first, then Rx". Read the product from right to left when you apply it to a point.

GATE loves thisRotations in 3D do not commute. A question that swaps two rotations is testing this exact fact. Always check whether the order changes the final point.

In 2D, does R(30°) R(60°) equal R(60°) R(30°)?

Yes. Both equal R(90°), because rotations about the same point in 2D commute.

For the product M = Rz(90°) Rx(90°), which rotation acts first on a point?

Rx(90°), because it is the rightmost matrix.

Roll, pitch and yaw (Euler angles)

Three angles for one orientation

Any orientation in 3D can be made by three successive rotations about the axes. Engineers usually name them after the motion of a vehicle or an aircraft. Roll is the turn about the X axis (the direction of travel), pitch is the turn about the Y axis (the nose up or down), and yaw is the turn about the Z axis (the nose left or right).

A common convention is the ZYX order. The overall rotation is

R = Rz(yaw) Ry(pitch) Rx(roll)

Read it from right to left when applying it to a point. First roll is applied, then pitch, and finally yaw. Other books use other orders, so always check which convention a question uses.

Worked example

Take yaw = 90°, pitch = 90°, roll = 0°. Apply R to the point (1, 0, 0).

  1. Roll is zero, so Rx(0°) is the identity and the point stays (1, 0, 0).
  2. Pitch of 90° about Y: Ry(90°) takes (1, 0, 0) to (0, 0, −1).
  3. Yaw of 90° about Z: Rz(90°) keeps the z-coordinate, so (0, 0, −1) stays (0, 0, −1).
  4. The final point is (0, 0, −1).
import math

def rot_x(t):
    c, s = math.cos(t), math.sin(t)
    return [[1, 0, 0], [0, c, -s], [0, s, c]]

def rot_y(t):
    c, s = math.cos(t), math.sin(t)
    return [[c, 0, s], [0, 1, 0], [-s, 0, c]]

def rot_z(t):
    c, s = math.cos(t), math.sin(t)
    return [[c, -s, 0], [s, c, 0], [0, 0, 1]]

def matmul(A, B):
    return [[sum(A[i][k] * B[k][j] for k in range(3)) for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]

def apply(R, p):
    return [sum(R[i][k] * p[k] for k in range(3)) for i in range(3)]

def clean(v):
    return [round(x, 6) + 0.0 for x in v]

q = math.pi / 2
R = matmul(rot_z(q), matmul(rot_y(q), rot_x(0.0)))
print("Euler ZYX applied to (1,0,0):", clean(apply(R, [1, 0, 0])))
Euler ZYX applied to (1,0,0): [0.0, 0.0, -1.0]

TipEuler angles are easy to misread. If a question gives the three angles, first state the convention it uses. Then build the matrix in that order, and only then apply it to the point.

In the ZYX convention, which rotation is applied first to a point?

The roll, which is Rx(roll), because it is the rightmost matrix.

What does a yaw of 90° do to the point (1, 0, 0) if pitch and roll are zero?

It turns the point to (0, 1, 0), a quarter turn about the Z axis, with z unchanged.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original, written in the style of GATE multiple select and numerical questions. They are not past exam questions.

Rotate a point by 90 degrees

The point (1, 0) is rotated counterclockwise by 90° about the origin. What is the y-coordinate of the new point? (Numerical answer.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Only the y-coordinate after the turn.
  2. Which idea applies. y′ = x sin θ + y cos θ.
  3. Work it out. y′ = 1 × sin 90° + 0 × cos 90° = 1 × 1 + 0 = 1.
  4. Check. A quarter turn of (1, 0) lands on (0, 1), so the y-coordinate is 1.

Answer: 1.

Properties of rotation matrices

Select all that apply. Which statements are true for a 2D rotation matrix R(θ)?

(A) det R(θ) = 1.
(B) The inverse of R(θ) equals its transpose.
(C) R(θ) R(φ) = R(θ − φ).
(D) R(θ) preserves the length of every vector.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which properties hold for a 2D rotation.
  2. Which idea applies. Rotation matrices have determinant 1, are orthogonal, and preserve length. Two rotations about the origin add their angles.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true, since cos² + sin² = 1. (B) is true, since R is orthogonal. (C) is false, since the angles add, giving R(θ + φ). (D) is true.
  4. Check. Test (C) with θ = φ = 90°. R(90°)R(90°) = R(180°) = −I, while R(0°) = I. So (C) is wrong.

Answer: (A), (B) and (D).

The x-coordinate after a 90 degree turn

The point (3, 4) is rotated counterclockwise by 90° about the origin. What is the x-coordinate of the new point? (Numerical answer.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The new x-coordinate.
  2. Which idea applies. x′ = x cos θ − y sin θ.
  3. Work it out. x′ = 3 × 0 − 4 × 1 = −4.
  4. Check. A quarter turn sends the point (3, 4) to (−4, 3). The length is √25 = 5 before and after, as it should be.

Answer: −4.

Rotate about Z, then about X

The point (1, 0, 0) is first rotated by 90° about the Z axis, and then by 90° about the X axis. What is the z-coordinate of the final point? (Numerical answer.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The z-coordinate after two rotations in the given order.
  2. Which idea applies. Apply Rz(90°) first, then Rx(90°).
  3. Work it out. Rz(90°) takes (1, 0, 0) to (0, 1, 0). Then Rx(90°) takes (0, 1, 0) to (0, 0, 1), since z′ = y sin θ + z cos θ = 1 × 1 + 0 = 1. The z-coordinate is 1.
  4. Check. The Python check below gives the same final point.
import math
def rot_x(t):
    c, s = math.cos(t), math.sin(t)
    return [[1, 0, 0], [0, c, -s], [0, s, c]]
def rot_z(t):
    c, s = math.cos(t), math.sin(t)
    return [[c, -s, 0], [s, c, 0], [0, 0, 1]]
def apply(R, p):
    return [sum(R[i][k] * p[k] for k in range(3)) for i in range(3)]
q = math.pi / 2
p = apply(rot_x(q), apply(rot_z(q), [1, 0, 0]))
print(f"z = {round(p[2], 6) + 0.0:.1f}")
z = 1.0

Answer: 1.

Does the order of rotations matter

Let A = Rx(90°) and B = Rz(90°). Select all that apply.

(A) A and B commute, so AB and BA are the same matrix.
(B) Applying BA to (1, 0, 0) gives (0, 1, 0).
(C) Applying AB to (1, 0, 0) gives (0, 0, 1).
(D) Rx(30°) Rx(60°) = Rx(90°).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements about the order of these rotations are true.
  2. Which idea applies. The rightmost matrix acts first. Rotations about the same axis add their angles, but rotations about different axes do not commute in 3D.
  3. Work it out. (A) is false, since A and B do not commute. (B) is true: BA applies A first, which leaves (1, 0, 0) unchanged, then B gives (0, 1, 0). (C) is true: AB applies B first, giving (0, 1, 0), then A gives (0, 0, 1). (D) is true, since both are about X and the angles add.
  4. Check. The results in (B) and (C) are different points, which confirms that the order matters.

Answer: (B), (C) and (D).

Rotate about X, then about Y

The point (0, 0, 1) is first rotated by 90° about the X axis, and then by 90° about the Y axis. What is the y-coordinate of the final point? (Numerical answer.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The y-coordinate after Ry(90°) Rx(90°) is applied to the point.
  2. Which idea applies. The rightmost matrix, Rx(90°), acts first.
  3. Work it out. Rx(90°) takes (0, 0, 1) to (0, −1, 0), since y′ = −z sin θ = −1 and z′ = z cos θ = 0. Then Ry(90°) keeps y, so the y-coordinate stays −1.
  4. Check. The Python check below gives the same result.
import math
def rot_x(t):
    c, s = math.cos(t), math.sin(t)
    return [[1, 0, 0], [0, c, -s], [0, s, c]]
def rot_y(t):
    c, s = math.cos(t), math.sin(t)
    return [[c, 0, s], [0, 1, 0], [-s, 0, c]]
def apply(R, p):
    return [sum(R[i][k] * p[k] for k in range(3)) for i in range(3)]
q = math.pi / 2
p = apply(rot_y(q), apply(rot_x(q), [0, 0, 1]))
print(f"y = {round(p[1], 6) + 0.0:.1f}")
y = -1.0

Answer: −1.

Statements about rotation matrices

Select all that apply.

(A) The columns of a 2D rotation matrix are unit vectors at right angles.
(B) R(180°) = −I in 2D.
(C) Rz(θ) changes the z-coordinate of every point.
(D) In 2D, R(a) R(b) = R(a + b).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements about rotation matrices are true.
  2. Which idea applies. The columns of R(θ) are (cos θ, sin θ) and (−sin θ, cos θ), which are unit vectors at right angles. Two 2D rotations add their angles.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true. (B) is true, since cos 180° = −1 and sin 180° = 0. (C) is false, since Rz keeps z fixed. (D) is true.
  4. Check. The statement in (C) fails the test with the third row of Rz, which is (0, 0, 1). It leaves z unchanged.

Answer: (A), (B) and (D).

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Composite rotation (NAT, 2 marks)

R = Rz(90°)Rx(90°). Find R applied to the vector (0, 1, 0).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The rotated vector.
  2. Which idea applies. Apply Rx first, then Rz.
  3. Work it out. Rx(90°) sends (0, 1, 0) to (0, 0, 1). Rz(90°) leaves (0, 0, 1) unchanged. Result (0, 0, 1).
  4. Check. The code multiplies the matrices.
Rz = [[0, -1, 0], [1, 0, 0], [0, 0, 1]]; Rx = [[1, 0, 0], [0, 0, -1], [0, 1, 0]]
mv = lambda M, v: [sum(M[i][j] * v[j] for j in range(3)) for i in range(3)]
print(mv(Rz, mv(Rx, [0, 1, 0])))

[0, 0, 1]

Answer: (0, 0, 1).

Exam-level 2: Rotation angle from trace (NAT, 2 marks)

A rotation matrix has trace 2. Find the rotation angle in degrees.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. θ from tr R = 1 + 2cos θ.
  2. Which idea applies. Every 3D rotation has eigenvalues 1, eiθ, e−iθ.
  3. Work it out. 1 + 2cos θ = 2, cos θ = 1/2, θ = 60°.
  4. Check. The identity has trace 3, θ = 0.

Answer: 60°.

Exam-level 3: Rotation facts (MSQ, 2 marks)

Which are TRUE for 3 × 3 rotation matrices? (A) Rᵀ = R−1. (B) det R = 1. (C) R1R2 = R2R1 always. (D) Each column has unit length.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Properties of SO(3).
  2. Which idea applies. Rotations in 3D do not commute in general.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) True. (C) False. (D) True.
  4. Check. Rx(90°)Rz(90°) differs from Rz(90°)Rx(90°).

Answer: (A), (B) and (D).

A reflection has determinant −1, which is not a rotation.

Remember these

  1. A rotation matrix keeps lengths and has determinant +1.
  2. The inverse of a rotation matrix is its transpose.
  3. In 2D, rotations about the origin add their angles and commute.
  4. In 3D, rotations generally do not commute, so the order matters.
  5. The rightmost matrix in a product acts first on the point.
  6. Rotation about Z keeps z, rotation about X keeps x, and rotation about Y keeps y.
  7. Always state the Euler angle convention before you build the matrix.

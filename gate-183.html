Design for static loading

Design for static loading means choosing the size of a machine part so that a steady load never makes it fail. This page builds the ideas step by step: stress, factor of safety, allowable stress, the failure theories that tell us when yield happens under combined stresses, and the design equations for rods, pins and shafts, with GATE-style numbers.

The idea in plain words

Suppose you hang a bag of shopping from a thin string. The string holds for a while, then one day it snaps. A designer wants to prevent this. The question is how thick the string must be so that it never breaks in normal use, even if the load is a little more than expected.

This is the job of design for static loading. A static load is one that is applied slowly and stays the same. We must find the size of a rod, pin, shaft or bracket so that the stress it feels stays safely below the strength of its material. The safety margin is called the factor of safety.

This is the first step of machine design, and it leads to the fatigue topic. In GATE, the Robotics and Automation (RA) paper uses these ideas for robot links, shafts and fasteners.

Key terms

Read this list once, then use it as a reference. Each term gives the plain meaning first.

Force (F)
A push or pull, in newtons (N). Plain meaning: how hard something is pulled or pushed.
Area (A)
The size of the surface that carries the force, in mm² when the stress is in N/mm².
Normal stress (σ)
Force per unit area acting at right angles to the surface, σ = F/A. Plain meaning: the pull or push spread over the cross section.
Shear stress (τ)
Force per unit area acting along the surface, τ = F/A for a sliding force. Plain meaning: a sliding or cutting stress.
Stress unit
N/mm² is the same as MPa (megapascal). A stress of 250 MPa means 250 N per square millimetre.
Yield strength (Sy)
The stress at which a ductile material starts to deform permanently.
Ultimate strength (Sut)
The largest stress a material can carry in a tension test before fracture.
Ductile material
A material such as mild steel that stretches a lot before breaking, and shows a clear yield point.
Brittle material
A material such as cast iron or glass that breaks with little stretching.
Factor of safety (n)
The ratio of the strength to the working stress: n = Sy/σ for ductile parts, n = Sut/σ for brittle parts.
Allowable stress (σall)
The highest working stress permitted, σall = Sy/n.
Principal stresses (σ1, σ2)
The largest and smallest normal stresses at a point, found when the shear stress on the plane is zero.
Mohr's circle
A circle drawing that shows the normal and shear stress on all planes at a point.
Failure theory
A rule that predicts when a material fails under a combined stress. Examples: Tresca and von Mises.
Second moment of area (I) and polar second moment (J)
Shape numbers for bending and torsion. For a solid shaft of diameter d, J = πd⁴/32 and the radius is d/2.
Torque (T)
A turning force on a shaft, in N m. In stress calculations we convert it to N mm.

TrapAlways convert lengths to millimetres when stress is in N/mm². A torque of 200 N m is 200 000 N mm, and a diameter of 0.02 m is 20 mm. Forgetting this factor of a thousand is one of the most common errors.

Stress, strength and the factor of safety

Why stress, not force, matters

A rope that carries 1 kN will fail sooner if it is thin than if it is thick. The force is the same, but the thin rope spreads it over a smaller area. Stress is the force divided by the area it spreads over. Materials fail at a particular stress, not at a particular force, so we compare stress with strength.

σ = F / A (normal stress) and τ = F / A (shear stress)

Here F is the force in newtons and A is the area in mm². The result is in N/mm², which is written MPa. Steel used in machines typically has a yield strength of a few hundred MPa.

The factor of safety

Machines never see exactly the load that was calculated. Loads can be higher than expected, the material may have flaws, and the manufacturing may vary. So we design for a margin. The factor of safety n is

n = Sy / σworking (ductile material, yield basis)

A factor of safety of 1 means the part is on the edge of yield. Typical design values are in the range 1.5 to 4, chosen by the designer according to the risk, the uncertainty and the consequence of failure. GATE questions usually give the factor of safety directly.

Key ideaA factor of safety compares the material's strength with the actual working stress. Larger n means more margin and a heavier part.

Worked example: finding the factor of safety

A flat steel bar 20 mm wide and 10 mm thick carries an axial tensile force of 40 kN. The yield strength of the steel is 400 MPa. Find the working stress and the factor of safety.

Step 1. Area: A = 20 × 10 = 200 mm².

Step 2. Working stress: σ = 40 000 N/200 mm² = 200 MPa.

Step 3. Factor of safety: n = 400/200 = 2.

Step 4. Check: the stress is half the yield strength, so the bar has a factor of safety of 2. This is a sensible margin.

F = 40000.0          # N
A = 20.0 * 10.0      # mm^2
Sy = 400.0           # MPa = N/mm^2

sigma = F / A
n = Sy / sigma
print(f"working stress = {sigma:.0f} MPa")
print(f"factor of safety = {n:.1f}")
working stress = 200 MPa
factor of safety = 2.0
Self-check: if the same bar is made 40 mm wide, what is the factor of safety?

The area doubles to 400 mm², so the stress is 100 MPa and the factor of safety is 4.

Self-check: why is the factor of safety defined with yield strength for ductile materials?

Yield is the point where the part is no longer usable, because it stays permanently deformed. Designers keep the working stress below this point.

Allowable stress and the design of simple members

From factor of safety to size

Designing a part is the reverse of checking it. We know the force, the material and the factor of safety, and we want the size. First find the allowable stress:

σall = Sy / n

Then the area must be big enough that the working stress does not exceed the allowable stress:

A ≥ F / σall = F n / Sy

For a round rod of diameter d, the area is A = πd²/4, so the diameter is

d = √(4 F n / (π Sy))

Worked example: a tie rod

A round tie rod must carry a tensile force of 50 kN. The material has yield strength 250 MPa, and the factor of safety is 2.5. Find the minimum diameter.

Step 1. Allowable stress: σall = 250/2.5 = 100 MPa.

Step 2. Minimum area: A = 50 000/100 = 500 mm².

Step 3. Diameter: d = √(4 × 500/π) = √636.6 = 25.2 mm.

Step 4. Check: the area of a 25.2 mm circle is π × 25.2²/4 = 498.5 mm², which is close to the 500 mm² needed. In practice we round up, to 26 mm or the next standard size.

import math

F = 50000.0        # N
Sy = 250.0         # MPa
n = 2.5

sigma_all = Sy / n
A = F / sigma_all
d = math.sqrt(4 * A / math.pi)
print(f"allowable stress = {sigma_all:.0f} MPa")
print(f"area = {A:.0f} mm^2")
print(f"diameter = {d:.1f} mm")
allowable stress = 100 MPa
area = 500 mm^2
diameter = 25.2 mm

TrapCheck which area the stress acts on. A pin in a clevis is in shear across its cross section, while a tie rod is in tension across its cross section. The same force gives different stresses in different cases.

Shear in a pin

A pin that carries a force across a joint is in shear. If it is in single shear, the shear area is one circle, A = πd²/4. In double shear, the force is shared across two surfaces, so the area is 2 × πd²/4. The design equation is then

τ = F/A, with τall = Ssy/n

where Ssy is the shear yield strength. For ductile steel, Tresca's theory gives Ssy = 0.5Sy and von Mises gives Ssy = 0.577Sy. Section C shows where these come from.

Self-check: a rod of diameter 25.2 mm is used at a factor of safety of 2.5. What is the stress?

The stress is about 100 MPa, since the area is about 500 mm² and the force is 50 kN. This is the allowable stress.

Self-check: a pin is in double shear instead of single shear. For the same force, what happens to the required area?

It halves. Two shear planes share the load, so each carries half, and the required total shear area is halved.

Shear stress and the design of shafts in torsion

What torsion does

Twisting a shaft produces shear stress that is zero at the centre and largest at the surface.

      cross section of a solid shaft, diameter d

           ___________
         /   stress   \       shear stress tau is zero at the centre
        |   rises to   |      and largest at the outer surface (radius d/2)
        |  maximum at  |
         \  surface    /
           -----------
   torque T twists the section; the outer fibres shear the most

The shear stress at radius r in a solid shaft is τ = Tr/J. The largest value occurs at the surface, where r = d/2 and J = πd⁴/32. This gives the maximum shear stress:

τmax = 16T / (π d³)

Read each symbol: T is the torque in N mm, d is the shaft diameter in mm, and τmax is in N/mm² (MPa).

Designing a shaft for torque alone

For pure torsion, the simple design uses the shear yield strength. Set τmax equal to the allowable shear stress Ssy/n and solve for d:

d = [16 n T / (π Ssy)]1/3

Worked example

A solid shaft transmits a torque of 200 N m. The material has yield strength 300 MPa, and the factor of safety is 2. Use the Tresca shear yield, Ssy = 0.5 × 300 = 150 MPa. Find the minimum diameter.

Step 1. Convert the torque: T = 200 N m = 200 000 N mm.

Step 2. Substitute: d³ = 16 × 2 × 200 000/(π × 150) = 6 400 000/471.24 = 13 580.

Step 3. Cube root: d = 13 580^(1/3) = 23.9 mm.

Step 4. Round up to a standard size, such as 24 mm, and check the stress: 16 × 200 000/(π × 24³) = 3.2 × 10⁶/43 429 = 73.7 MPa, which is below 150 MPa.

import math

T = 200.0 * 1000.0       # N mm
Sy, n = 300.0, 2.0
Ssy = 0.5 * Sy           # Tresca shear yield
d = (16 * n * T / (math.pi * Ssy)) ** (1 / 3)
print(f"diameter = {d:.1f} mm")
print(f"stress at 24 mm = {16 * T / (math.pi * 24 ** 3):.1f} MPa")
diameter = 23.9 mm
stress at 24 mm = 73.7 MPa

GATE loves thisWhen a question says "design for torque only" and gives the shear yield or the ultimate shear, use that value directly. If it gives only Sy, use Ssy = 0.5Sy for Tresca or 0.577Sy for von Mises.

Self-check: the torque is doubled and the other values are fixed. By what factor does the diameter change?

The diameter changes by 21/3 = 1.26, so it increases by about 26 per cent.

Self-check: where in a solid shaft is the shear stress zero?

At the centre axis, where r = 0.

Combined stresses and Mohr's circle

Why stresses combine

Most parts carry several stresses at once, described at a point by σx, σy and τxy. To check failure we need the largest normal and shear stresses at that point. Mohr's circle gives both.

The formulas for principal stresses

Mohr's circle has a centre and a radius. The centre is at the average normal stress, and the radius is the largest shear stress in the plane. The principal stresses are

σ1,2 = (σx + σy)/2 ± √[((σx - σy)/2)² + τxy²]

The maximum in-plane shear stress is the radius of the circle:

τmax = √[((σx - σy)/2)² + τxy²] = (σ1 - σ2)/2

Here the sign convention is that tension is positive and compression is negative. The "+" gives the larger principal stress σ1, and the "-" gives σ2.

Key ideaPrincipal stresses are the normal stresses on the planes where the shear is zero. Failure theories use the principal stresses, not the original x and y stresses.

Worked example

At a point, σx = 80 MPa, σy = -40 MPa (compression) and τxy = 30 MPa. Find the principal stresses and the maximum shear stress.

Step 1. Centre: (80 + (-40))/2 = 20 MPa.

Step 2. Radius: √[((80 - (-40))/2)² + 30²] = √[60² + 30²] = √4500 = 67.08 MPa.

Step 3. Principal stresses: σ1 = 20 + 67.08 = 87.08 MPa and σ2 = 20 - 67.08 = -47.08 MPa.

Step 4. Maximum shear: τmax = 67.08 MPa, which equals (87.08 - (-47.08))/2 = 67.08. The two values agree.

import math

sx, sy, txy = 80.0, -40.0, 30.0
centre = (sx + sy) / 2
radius = math.sqrt(((sx - sy) / 2) ** 2 + txy ** 2)
s1, s2 = centre + radius, centre - radius
print(f"s1 = {s1:.2f} MPa")
print(f"s2 = {s2:.2f} MPa")
print(f"tau max = {radius:.2f} MPa")
print(f"check = {(s1 - s2) / 2:.2f} MPa")
s1 = 87.08 MPa
s2 = -47.08 MPa
tau max = 67.08 MPa
check = 67.08 MPa
Self-check: if τxy is zero, what are the principal stresses?

They are simply σx and σy, because the shear is zero on the x and y planes already.

Failure theories: maximum shear stress and distortion energy

Why we need failure theories

A tension test gives the yield strength in one direction only. A failure theory says when a combined stress state reaches yield, using only that strength. For ductile machine parts, two theories are common.

Maximum shear stress theory (Tresca)

Tresca says that yield starts when the largest shear stress in the material reaches the shear stress at yield in a simple tension test. In a tension test, the maximum shear is Sy/2. So the rule is

σ1 - σ3 = Sy/n, where σ3 is the smallest principal stress

For plane stress (where one principal stress is zero), Tresca gives the test: the largest of |σ1|, |σ2| and |σ1 - σ2| must be at most Sy/n.

Maximum distortion energy theory (von Mises)

Von Mises says that yield starts when the energy that changes the shape of the material reaches the value at yield in a simple tension test. The result is an equivalent stress, which is compared with Sy/n. For plane stress:

σ' = √(σx² - σxσy + σy² + 3τxy²) ≤ Sy/n

For a simple case, with only a normal stress σ and a shear τ, this becomes σ' = √(σ² + 3τ²). Each term is in MPa, and the square root is taken of the whole sum.

Key ideaTresca is simpler and always a little more conservative. Von Mises is less conservative and is most often used for ductile machine parts.

Comparing the two in pure shear

Consider a pure shear state τ with no normal stress. Tresca says yield at τ = Sy/2 = 0.5 Sy. Von Mises gives σ' = √(3τ²) = √3 τ, so yield at τ = Sy/√3 = 0.577 Sy. Von Mises predicts a higher shear yield strength, which is why it is less conservative.

Worked example

Use the stresses from the previous section: σx = 80, σy = -40 and τxy = 30 MPa. The material has Sy = 300 MPa, and the factor of safety is 2. Check the design with both theories.

Step 1. Von Mises: σ' = √(80² - 80 × (-40) + (-40)² + 3 × 30²) = √(6400 + 3200 + 1600 + 2700) = √13 900 = 117.9 MPa.

Step 2. Allowable: Sy/n = 300/2 = 150 MPa. Since 117.9 is less than 150, the design is safe by von Mises.

Step 3. Tresca: σ1 - σ2 = 87.08 - (-47.08) = 134.2 MPa. Compare with 150 MPa. Since 134.2 is less than 150, the design is also safe by Tresca.

Step 4. Check: von Mises gave a smaller equivalent stress (117.9) than Tresca's difference (134.2), which is the usual pattern.

import math

sx, sy, txy = 80.0, -40.0, 30.0
Sy, n = 300.0, 2.0
vm = math.sqrt(sx ** 2 - sx * sy + sy ** 2 + 3 * txy ** 2)
centre = (sx + sy) / 2
radius = math.sqrt(((sx - sy) / 2) ** 2 + txy ** 2)
tresca = 2 * radius
print(f"von Mises stress = {vm:.1f} MPa")
print(f"Tresca stress = {tresca:.1f} MPa")
print(f"allowable = {Sy / n:.0f} MPa")
von Mises stress = 117.9 MPa
Tresca stress = 134.2 MPa
allowable = 150 MPa

TrapFor Tresca, the comparison is σ1 - σ3 against Sy/n, not against Sy/(2n). Students who halve the stress by mistake reach the wrong answer.

Self-check: a pure tension of 100 MPa is applied. What is the von Mises equivalent stress?

σ' = 100 MPa. With no shear, von Mises reduces to the tensile stress itself.

Self-check: which theory gives the higher allowable shear stress for a ductile steel in pure shear?

Von Mises, with 0.577 Sy, which is higher than Tresca's 0.5 Sy.

Combined bending and torsion of shafts

The real shaft

A gearbox shaft carries a bending moment M and a torque T. At the critical section, the bending gives σ = 32M/(πd³) and the torque gives τ = 16T/(πd³), both at the outer surface.

σ = 32M/(πd³), τ = 16T/(πd³)

Here M and T are in N mm and d is in mm.

Von Mises design equation

The von Mises equivalent stress for the bending normal stress σ and the shear τ is σ' = √(σ² + 3τ²). Substituting the two expressions gives

σ' = (16/(πd³)) √(4M² + 3T²)

Set σ' equal to the allowable stress Sy/n and solve for the diameter:

d³ = 16n √(4M² + 3T²) / (π Sy)

Tresca design equation

For Tresca, the maximum shear stress is the radius of Mohr's circle. For the shaft, this gives τmax = (16/(πd³)) √(M² + T²). Setting τmax = Ssy/n with Ssy = Sy/2 gives

d³ = 32n √(M² + T²) / (π Sy)

Notice that the Tresca formula uses 32 where the von Mises formula uses 16 with the factor 4M². For a pure torque the two agree.

Worked example

A shaft carries a bending moment M = 200 N m and a torque T = 150 N m. The material has Sy = 300 MPa and the factor of safety is 2. Find the minimum diameter by von Mises and by Tresca.

Step 1. Convert: M = 200 000 N mm and T = 150 000 N mm.

Step 2. Von Mises: √(4M² + 3T²) = √(4 × 4 × 10¹⁰ + 3 × 2.25 × 10¹⁰) = √(2.275 × 10¹¹) = 476 970 N mm. Then d³ = 16 × 2 × 476 970/(π × 300) = 16 195, so d = 25.3 mm.

Step 3. Tresca: √(M² + T²) = √(4 × 10¹⁰ + 2.25 × 10¹⁰) = 250 000 N mm. Then d³ = 32 × 2 × 250 000/(π × 300) = 16 977, so d = 25.7 mm.

Step 4. Check: the Tresca diameter is slightly larger, which is the expected conservative result. Round up to a standard size, such as 26 mm.

import math

M, T = 200000.0, 150000.0      # N mm
Sy, n = 300.0, 2.0

d_vm = (16 * n * math.sqrt(4 * M ** 2 + 3 * T ** 2) / (math.pi * Sy)) ** (1 / 3)
d_tr = (32 * n * math.sqrt(M ** 2 + T ** 2) / (math.pi * Sy)) ** (1 / 3)
print(f"von Mises diameter = {d_vm:.1f} mm")
print(f"Tresca diameter = {d_tr:.1f} mm")
von Mises diameter = 25.3 mm
Tresca diameter = 25.7 mm

TrapThe bending stress uses 32M/(πd³), but the shear stress uses 16T/(πd³). If you use 16 for both, the bending term is too small by a factor of 2, which gives an unsafe shaft.

Self-check: with M = 0, does the von Mises formula reduce to the torsion formula?

Yes. d³ = 16n√(3)T/(π Sy), which equals the torsion result with Ssy = Sy/√3.

Self-check: a shaft has zero torque. Which formula applies, and why is the result a bending design?

Either formula reduces to d³ = 16n(2M)/(π Sy) or the Tresca equivalent, which is a pure bending design. The bending stress alone sets the size.

Brittle materials and the maximum normal stress theory

Why brittle materials are different

Cast iron and ceramics break with little yielding, so their factor of safety uses the ultimate strength.

Maximum normal stress theory

The maximum normal stress theory says that failure occurs when the largest principal stress reaches the ultimate tensile strength. The design rule is

n = Sut / σ1, where σ1 is the largest principal stress

TipIf a question gives a brittle material and asks for the factor of safety, use the ultimate strength and the largest principal stress. Do not use the yield strength for cast iron.

Worked example

A cast iron bracket has a largest principal stress of 60 MPa and an ultimate tensile strength of 200 MPa. Find the factor of safety by maximum normal stress.

Step 1. Largest principal stress: σ1 = 60 MPa.

Step 2. Factor of safety: n = 200/60 = 3.33.

Step 3. Check: the bracket is well within its tensile strength, which is consistent with a safe design.

Self-check: why do we use ultimate strength rather than yield for cast iron?

Cast iron does not show a clear yield point. It fractures at about the ultimate strength, so that is the limiting value.

Self-check: a brittle part has σ1 = 0 and σ2 = -100 MPa. Is the maximum normal stress theory useful here?

Not directly. The largest principal stress is zero, so this theory gives an infinite factor of safety, which is not realistic. Brittle materials in compression need a different theory that uses the compression strength.

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original GATE-style problems for practice, not real past paper questions. Work each one on paper before reading the answer.

Factor of safety of a bar

A rectangular steel bar of cross section 25 mm × 8 mm carries an axial tensile load of 30 kN. The yield strength is 400 MPa. Find the factor of safety (numerical answer, one decimal place).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. n, using yield strength.
  2. Which idea applies. σ = F/A, then n = Sy/σ.
  3. Work it out. A = 25 × 8 = 200 mm². σ = 30 000/200 = 150 MPa. n = 400/150 = 2.67.
  4. Check. Stress is less than yield, so n is greater than 1, as required.
F, A, Sy = 30000.0, 25.0 * 8.0, 400.0
print(f"n = {Sy / (F / A):.2f}")
n = 2.67

Answer: n = 2.7 (2.67).

Size of a tie rod

A solid round rod must carry 20 kN in tension. The material has yield strength 300 MPa and the factor of safety is 2. Find the minimum diameter in mm (numerical answer, one decimal place).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The minimum diameter.
  2. Which idea applies. σall = Sy/n, A = F/σall, d = √(4A/π).
  3. Work it out. σall = 150 MPa. A = 20 000/150 = 133.3 mm². d = √(4 × 133.3/π) = √169.7 = 13.0 mm.
  4. Check. The area of a 13 mm circle is π × 13²/4 = 132.7 mm², very close to 133.3 mm².

Answer: about 13.0 mm.

Which statements are true?

Consider these statements about static design.

(A) In pure shear, von Mises predicts a higher shear yield strength than Tresca for a ductile material.
(B) For brittle materials the factor of safety is based on the ultimate strength.
(C) For a state of pure tension, Tresca and von Mises give the same yield condition.
(D) Stress concentration can always be ignored in static design of ductile parts.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true (multiple selection).
  2. Which idea applies. Pure shear: Tresca gives 0.5 Sy, von Mises gives 0.577 Sy. Brittle parts use the ultimate. Pure tension gives the same result for both theories.
  3. Work it out. (A) true. (B) true. (C) true, since with no shear both theories give σ = Sy. (D) false: stress concentration is often ignored for ductile parts under static load, but "always" is too strong and brittle parts need it.
  4. Check. Compare numbers: 0.577 > 0.5 confirms (A).

Answer: (A), (B) and (C).

Diameter of a torque-only shaft

A solid shaft transmits a torque of 200 N m. The material has yield strength 300 MPa, and the factor of safety is 2. Use Tresca with Ssy = 0.5 Sy. Find the minimum diameter in mm (numerical answer, one decimal place).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The minimum diameter in mm.
  2. Which idea applies. d = [16nT/(πSsy)]1/3 with T in N mm.
  3. Work it out. T = 200 000 N mm, Ssy = 150 MPa. d³ = 16 × 2 × 200 000/(π × 150) = 13 580. d = 23.9 mm.
  4. Check. A 24 mm shaft gives a shear stress of 73.7 MPa, below the 150 MPa allowable.

Answer: 23.9 mm.

Principal stress of a biaxial state

At a point of a machine part, σx = 80 MPa, σy = -40 MPa and τxy = 30 MPa. Find the larger principal stress σ1 in MPa (numerical answer, two decimal places).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. σ1 in MPa.
  2. Which idea applies. Mohr's circle: σ1 = centre + radius.
  3. Work it out. Centre = 20 MPa. Radius = √(60² + 30²) = 67.08 MPa. σ1 = 87.08 MPa.
  4. Check. σ1 must be larger than both σx and σy, and 87.08 is larger than 80.
import math

s1 = (sx + sy) / 2 + math.sqrt(((sx - sy) / 2) ** 2 + txy ** 2)
print(f"s1 = {s1:.2f} MPa")
s1 = 87.08 MPa

Shaft under bending and torsion

A solid shaft of diameter 40 mm carries a bending moment of 500 N m and a torque of 300 N m at the critical section. The yield strength is 350 MPa. Using the von Mises criterion, find the factor of safety (numerical answer, two decimal places).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The factor of safety n by von Mises.
  2. Which idea applies. σ' = (16/(πd³))√(4M² + 3T²), then n = Sy/σ'.
  3. Work it out. M = 500 000 N mm, T = 300 000 N mm. √(4M² + 3T²) = √(1.0 × 10¹² + 2.7 × 10¹¹) = √(1.27 × 10¹²) = 1.1269 × 10⁶ N mm. With d = 40 mm: 16/(π × 64 000) = 7.958 × 10⁻⁵. σ' = 7.958 × 10⁻⁵ × 1.1269 × 10⁶ = 89.7 MPa. n = 350/89.7 = 3.90.
  4. Check. The stress is much less than yield, so a factor of about 4 is consistent.
import math

M, T, d, Sy = 500000.0, 300000.0, 40.0, 350.0
sigma_vm = 16 / (math.pi * d ** 3) * math.sqrt(4 * M ** 2 + 3 * T ** 2)
print(f"von Mises stress = {sigma_vm:.2f} MPa")
print(f"factor of safety = {Sy / sigma_vm:.2f}")
von Mises stress = 89.68 MPa
factor of safety = 3.90

Answer: n = 3.90.

Brittle bracket

A cast iron bracket has a largest principal stress of 60 MPa and an ultimate tensile strength of 200 MPa. The smallest principal stress is -20 MPa. Using the maximum normal stress theory, find the factor of safety, and say which value is compared with the ultimate strength.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The factor of safety and the stress that is compared.
  2. Which idea applies. For brittle materials, n = Sut/σ1, using only the largest principal stress.
  3. Work it out. σ1 = 60 MPa, and n = 200/60 = 3.33. The value -20 MPa is not used in the maximum normal stress theory.
  4. Check. The compressive stress is small compared with the tensile strength, so it does not change the answer here.

Answer: n = 3.33. The largest principal stress, 60 MPa, is compared with the ultimate tensile strength.

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Factor of safety (NAT, 2 marks)

A rod of diameter 20 mm carries 40 kN tension. The yield strength is 300 MPa. Find the factor of safety, correct to 2 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. FOS = Sy/σ.
  2. Which idea applies. σ = P/A.
  3. Work it out. A = 314.16 mm². σ = 127.32 MPa. FOS = 2.36.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
import math
s = 40e3 / (math.pi * 10 ** 2)
print("%.2f MPa, FOS = %.2f" % (s, 300 / s))

127.32 MPa, FOS = 2.36

Answer: about 2.36.

Exam-level 2: Stress concentration (NAT, 2 marks)

A plate with a hole has a nominal stress of 60 MPa and Kt = 2.5. The material is brittle with ultimate strength 200 MPa. Find the factor of safety.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. FOS using the peak stress.
  2. Which idea applies. Brittle materials feel the full stress concentration.
  3. Work it out. Peak 150 MPa. FOS = 200/150 = 1.33.
  4. Check. A ductile part under static load would usually ignore Kt.

Answer: about 1.33.

Exam-level 3: Combined loading (NAT, 2 marks)

A shaft of diameter 50 mm has M = 1 kN·m and T = 1.5 kN·m. Find the maximum shear stress by τ = 16√(M² + T²)/(πd³), in MPa.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The combined shear stress.
  2. Which idea applies. Equivalent torque √(M² + T²).
  3. Work it out. √(1 + 2.25) = 1.803 kN·m. τ = 16 · 1.803e6/(π · 125000) = 73.45 MPa.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
import math
print("%.2f MPa" % (16 * math.hypot(1e6, 1.5e6) / (math.pi * 50 ** 3)))

73.45 MPa

Answer: about 73.45 MPa.

Summary

Normal stress
σ = F/A, force per area acting at right angles to the surface.
Shear stress
τ = F/A, force per area acting along the surface.
Factor of safety
n = strength divided by working stress.
Yield strength
The stress at which a ductile material starts to deform permanently.
Ultimate strength
The largest stress before fracture, used for brittle materials.
Allowable stress
σall = Sy/n, the highest permitted working stress.
Tie rod diameter
d = √(4Fn/(πSy)) for a round rod in tension.
Torsional shear
τmax = 16T/(πd³) at the surface of a solid shaft.
Principal stresses
The largest and smallest normal stresses at a point, from Mohr's circle.
Maximum shear stress
The radius of Mohr's circle, equal to (σ1 - σ2)/2.
Tresca criterion
σ1 - σ3 must not exceed Sy/n.
Von Mises equivalent stress
σ' = √(σx² - σxσy + σy² + 3τxy²) for plane stress.
Shaft design, von Mises
d³ = 16n√(4M² + 3T²)/(πSy).
Shaft design, Tresca
d³ = 32n√(M² + T²)/(πSy).
Maximum normal stress
For brittle parts, n = Sut/σ1.

Remember these

  • Convert to mm and N before using N/mm² (MPa).
  • Ductile parts use yield strength, brittle parts use ultimate strength.
  • Shear yield is 0.5 Sy (Tresca) or 0.577 Sy (von Mises).
  • The bending stress uses 32M/(πd³) and the torsional stress uses 16T/(πd³).
  • Von Mises is less conservative than Tresca, and Tresca gives the larger diameter.
  • Check the answer by putting the stress back and comparing it with the allowable value.

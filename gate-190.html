Brakes and clutches

Brakes stop a moving machine by turning its motion into heat through friction, and clutches connect or disconnect a drive by the same friction. This page explains the main brake and clutch types, how to find the torque they can carry, and how much energy they must absorb, with GATE style numericals.

The idea in plain words

Welcome. Every time you rub your hands together, they warm up. That warmth is friction turning motion into heat. Brakes and clutches use exactly the same effect, but on purpose. A brake slows or stops a moving part by pressing a friction surface against a rotating drum or disc. The kinetic energy of the moving part is turned into heat in the friction material. A clutch connects a driving shaft to a driven shaft by pressing two friction surfaces together, so that the torque can pass from one to the other.

Think of a bicycle. Squeezing the brake lever presses rubber blocks onto the wheel rim, and the wheel slows. Heat is produced at the rim, and that is why the rim gets warm after a long downhill ride. A car's clutch works in the same way, except that the friction plate is pressed onto a flywheel, and the engine's torque is passed on to the gearbox only as long as the plate holds.

Why does this matter for GATE? In the Machine Design part of the Robotics and Automation paper, questions on brakes and clutches are short and standard. They ask for the braking torque of a block or band brake, the torque capacity of a single or multi plate clutch, or the energy a brake must absorb in a given stop. Each question is a simple formula with a clear meaning, once you know the geometry.

We will go slowly. The key idea, friction torque, comes first. Then each device is built from that idea.

Key terms

Read this list once now, and come back to it when a word feels unfamiliar.

Brake
A device that slows or stops a moving part by friction. Precisely: a device that dissipates kinetic energy as heat through a friction contact.
Clutch
A device that connects or disconnects two rotating shafts by friction. Precisely: a torque-transmitting coupling that slips when the friction capacity is exceeded.
Coefficient of friction (mu)
The ratio of the friction force to the normal force between two surfaces. Precisely: a dimensionless number, typically about 0.2 to 0.5 for brake and clutch linings.
Normal force (N or F)
The force pressing the two friction surfaces together. Precisely: the force perpendicular to the contact surfaces, in newtons.
Friction torque (T)
The torque from friction acting at a radius. Precisely: friction force times the radius at which it acts, in N m or N mm.
Mean radius (rm)
The effective radius at which the friction force acts on an annular pad or plate. Precisely: an average radius between the inner and outer radii of the friction surface.
Uniform pressure theory
A model that assumes the pressure is the same everywhere on the friction surface. Precisely: the mean radius formula rm = (2/3)(R3 - r3)/(R2 - r2).
Uniform wear theory
A model that assumes the wear is the same everywhere, which makes the pressure higher near the inside. Precisely: the mean radius rm = (R + r)/2.
Band brake
A brake in which a flexible band wraps around a drum and is tightened. Precisely: a brake whose torque comes from the difference between two band tensions.
Tension ratio
The ratio of the tight side to the slack side of a belt or band. Precisely: F1/F2 = emu theta, with theta the wrap angle in radians.
Wrap angle (theta)
The angle through which the band touches the drum. Precisely: the arc of contact, in radians.
Centrifugal clutch
A clutch whose shoes are pressed outward by a spinning mass. Precisely: a clutch that engages automatically above a set speed, because the centrifugal force on the shoes grows with the square of speed.
Heat dissipation
The removal of heat from a brake or clutch to the surroundings. Precisely: the rate at which heat leaves the friction surfaces by conduction, convection and radiation.

Friction as the common idea

Before we look at each device, we need one basic idea: friction torque. Imagine pressing a flat pad against a spinning disc. The pad pushes with a normal force N. Where the two surfaces rub, a friction force acts, and its size is mu times N. This friction force acts at some distance from the axis, which we call the radius r. The torque from friction is the force times that radius.

Friction force = mu N, and friction torque = mu N r

Here mu is the coefficient of friction, N is the normal force in newtons, and r is the radius in metres or millimetres. The torque is in newton metres (N m) when r is in metres. Every brake and clutch formula in this page is built from this idea, with extra factors for the number of friction surfaces and the shape of the pad.

Worked example. A pad with mu = 0.3 is pressed with N = 1000 N at a radius r = 0.1 m.

  1. Friction force: mu N = 0.3 times 1000 = 300 N.
  2. Friction torque: T = 300 times 0.1 = 30 N m.

Key ideaThe torque a friction contact can carry is set by three things: the friction coefficient, the normal force, and the radius. Double any one of them and the torque doubles.

If the friction coefficient is halved, what happens to the friction torque?

It is halved, because torque is proportional to mu.

Why does the radius matter?

A friction force acting farther from the axis has a longer lever arm, so it produces a larger torque for the same force.

Brakes: what they do and where the energy goes

A moving vehicle has kinetic energy. Its size depends on the mass m and the speed v.

Kinetic energy E = (1/2) m v2

Here m is the mass in kg, v is the speed in m/s, and E is the energy in joules. When a brake stops the vehicle, this energy must go somewhere. Almost all of it turns into heat in the brake linings and drum or disc. The heating is the main design problem for brakes, because hot linings wear quickly, lose friction (this is called fade), and can even be damaged.

The average power a brake must absorb during a stop is the energy divided by the time taken.

Average power P = E/t

Worked example. A car has mass m = 1200 kg and speed v = 20 m/s. It stops in t = 8 s.

  1. Kinetic energy: E = (1/2) times 1200 times 202 = 0.5 times 1200 times 400 = 240000 J = 240 kJ.
  2. Average power: P = 240000/8 = 30000 W = 30 kW.
  3. So the brakes must absorb about 30 kW on average during this stop, all of it as heat.

TrapKinetic energy depends on the square of the speed. Doubling the speed makes the energy four times as large, and the brake must absorb four times as much heat. Many students forget this square when a question changes the speed.

GATE loves thisWhen a question asks how much energy a brake absorbs, the answer is the change in kinetic energy, and not the work done by the brake force on the wheel. Use (1/2) m (v12 - v22) when the speed changes from v1 to v2.

A vehicle of mass 1000 kg slows from 10 m/s to 0. How much energy is absorbed?

(1/2) times 1000 times 102 = 50000 J = 50 kJ.

Why do brakes on a long downhill road get very hot?

Gravity keeps adding energy to the vehicle as it descends, and the brakes must remove that energy as heat over a long time. The average power is high and the heat cannot escape quickly enough.

Block and band brakes

A block brake is the simplest type. A short shoe, or block, is pressed against the rim of a rotating drum by a force. The friction on the block produces a torque about the drum axis. For a short block with the normal force N acting on the drum at radius r, the braking torque is simply mu N r.

Block brake torque: T = mu N r

Worked example, block brake. A block with mu = 0.35 presses on a drum of radius r = 0.15 m with a normal force N = 1000 N.

  1. Write the formula: T = mu N r.
  2. Substitute: T = 0.35 times 1000 times 0.15.
  3. Multiply: T = 52.5 N m.

A band brake uses a flexible steel band wrapped around the drum. One end of the band is fixed, and the other end is pulled. The band tension is higher on the tight side (F1) than on the slack side (F2). The difference in tension creates the braking torque. The ratio of the two tensions depends on the friction and on the wrap angle. This is the same rule as the capstan equation, which you may have seen for ropes around posts.

Tension ratio: F1/F2 = emu theta
Band brake torque: T = (F1 - F2) r

Here theta is the wrap angle in radians, e is the base of natural logarithms (about 2.718), and r is the drum radius. Remember that theta must be in radians. A half turn is pi radians, not 180.

Worked example, band brake. A band with mu = 0.3 wraps half way round a drum, so theta = pi radians. The tight side is F1 = 2000 N and the drum radius is r = 0.2 m.

  1. Tension ratio: e0.3 pi = e0.9425 = 2.5663.
  2. Slack side: F2 = 2000/2.5663 = 779.3 N.
  3. Torque: T = (2000 - 779.3) times 0.2 = 1220.7 times 0.2 = 244.1 N m.

The Python block at the end of this page computes these values as a check.

TrapDo not use degrees in the exponent. Convert the wrap angle to radians first. An angle of 180 degrees gives the exponent pi, which is about 3.14, while 180 in place of pi would give a meaningless huge number.

Key ideaA band brake gains a large torque from a small pull, because the friction in the wrap multiplies the tension difference. More wrap angle and more friction give a bigger ratio.

What happens to the tension ratio of a band brake if the wrap angle doubles?

The ratio becomes e2 mu theta, which is the square of the original ratio. The ratio grows very quickly with the wrap angle.

Which side of the band is the tight side?

The side that is being pulled, which carries the larger tension F1. The other side, F2, is the slack side.

Disc brakes and the mean radius

A disc brake has a flat rotor and pads that clamp its faces. Each pad is an annular ring, with an inner radius r and an outer radius R. The friction force does not act at a single radius, because it is spread across the whole pad. We therefore use a mean radius, rm, at which the friction can be treated as acting.

Two models are used for the mean radius. The first is the uniform pressure model, where the pressure is the same everywhere on the pad. The second is the uniform wear model, where the pad wears evenly. A new pad with a flat surface is close to uniform pressure, and a worn pad is close to uniform wear. GATE questions usually say which model to use.

Uniform pressure: rm = (2/3)(R3 - r3)/(R2 - r2)
Uniform wear: rm = (R + r)/2

The torque for a disc with nf friction surfaces, each with axial clamping force Fa, is then the following.

T = nf mu Fa rm

Here nf is the number of friction surfaces in contact. A disc with pads on both faces has two friction surfaces, so nf = 2.

Worked example. A disc brake has an inner pad radius r = 60 mm and an outer radius R = 100 mm. Find the mean radius by both models, and the torque for mu = 0.3, Fa = 1000 N and nf = 2.

  1. Uniform wear: rm = (100 + 60)/2 = 80 mm.
  2. Uniform pressure: rm = (2/3)(1003 - 603)/(1002 - 602) = (2/3)(1000000 - 216000)/(10000 - 3600) = (2/3)(784000)/6400 = (2/3)(122.5) = 81.67 mm.
  3. Torque, uniform wear: T = 2 times 0.3 times 1000 times 80 = 48000 N mm = 48 N m.
  4. Torque, uniform pressure: T = 2 times 0.3 times 1000 times 81.67 = 49000 N mm = 49.0 N m.

The two models differ by only about 2 percent here, which is common when the ratio of radii is moderate.

TipWrite the mean radius in the same unit as the force, then convert the final torque to N m by dividing by 1000 when the radius was in mm.

TrapDo not use the outer radius R as the mean radius. It overstates the torque. The mean radius lies between r and R.

For a pad with r = 0 (a solid disc), what is the uniform pressure mean radius?

rm = (2/3) R3/R2 = (2/3) R. That is two thirds of the outer radius.

Why are two models used?

Because a new pad wears little and its pressure is nearly even (uniform pressure), while a pad that has run in and worn is nearly even in wear (uniform wear). The two give slightly different answers, and the question tells you which to use.

Friction clutches: torque capacity

A friction clutch has a driving member and a driven member, and a friction lining between them. When the clamping force is applied, the two members turn together. The clutch can carry a maximum torque, its capacity, before the lining slips. The capacity is the same formula as a disc brake.

Clutch torque capacity: T = n mu F rm

Here n is the number of friction surfaces, mu is the coefficient of friction, F is the axial clamping force, and rm is the mean radius. For a single plate clutch, the plate has two faces, so n = 2. A multi plate clutch has several plates with friction faces on both sides, and n equals the total number of friction faces in contact. Count every friction face that is in contact, including both faces of each middle plate.

Worked example. A single plate clutch has lining radii R = 120 mm and r = 80 mm. The clamping force is F = 800 N and mu = 0.25. Using uniform wear, find the torque capacity.

  1. Mean radius (uniform wear): rm = (120 + 80)/2 = 100 mm.
  2. Number of faces: n = 2.
  3. Torque: T = 2 times 0.25 times 800 times 100 = 40000 N mm = 40 N m.

The clutch can therefore transmit up to 40 N m before it slips. A design should give a margin above the engine torque, so that the clutch does not slip under normal use.

GATE loves thisFor a clutch, the torque capacity uses the number of friction faces, not the number of plates. A question that says "three plates" with friction on both sides of each plate may have four or six faces, so read the figure or statement carefully.

Why does a multi plate clutch carry more torque than a single plate clutch of the same size?

It has more friction faces (n is larger), so the friction force is shared over more surfaces, and the capacity grows in proportion to n.

What happens if the clutch is asked to carry more than its capacity?

The friction lining slips. This produces heat and wear, and the output speed is lower than the input speed, which is the basic reason for designing a margin.

Centrifugal clutches

A centrifugal clutch engages by itself when the driving shaft speeds up. Inside the clutch, weighted shoes are hinged to the driving member. As the shaft spins, each shoe tries to move outward, pulled by centrifugal force. When the speed is high enough, the shoes press against a drum and the drive is connected. A small motor, a chainsaw or a go-kart often uses this type, because it engages without a lever or a pedal.

The centrifugal force on a shoe of mass m whose centre of mass is at radius r, rotating at angular speed omega, is found from the familiar rule for circular motion.

Centrifugal force Fc = m r omega2

Here omega is the angular speed in rad/s. Because the force grows with the square of the speed, the clutch engages suddenly once a threshold is passed. The pressing force on the drum is what gives the torque capacity, so the capacity also grows with omega squared.

Worked example. A shoe of mass 0.5 kg has its centre of mass at r = 0.1 m and rotates at omega = 100 rad/s. Find the centrifugal force.

  1. Write the formula: Fc = m r omega2.
  2. Square omega: 1002 = 10000.
  3. Multiply: Fc = 0.5 times 0.1 times 10000 = 500 N.

TrapSpeed in rev/min must be converted to rad/s before you use the formula. One revolution per second equals 2 pi rad/s, which is about 6.28 rad/s.

If the speed of a centrifugal clutch doubles, how does the centrifugal force change?

It increases by a factor of 2 squared = 4, because the force is proportional to omega squared.

What is the main advantage of a centrifugal clutch?

It engages automatically at a set speed, with no pedal or manual lever, which makes it simple and convenient.

Design: heat, wear and energy

Designing a brake or clutch is more than finding a torque. The designer must check three things, and a good answer to a design question names them.

  • Heat. The energy absorbed in each stop, and the average power, must be carried away by the surfaces and the surroundings. If the temperature gets too high, the lining fades, glazes or burns. Large discs, fins and ventilation are used to remove heat.
  • Wear. The lining slowly wears away with every use, so the clamping force falls over time. Designers choose materials that wear slowly, and they use the uniform wear model to predict the life of the pad.
  • Torque capacity and margin. The capacity must be larger than the largest torque the system will see, with a safety factor. For a clutch, this avoids slipping. For a brake, it ensures the stopping force is enough.

A quick design approach has four steps: find the energy or torque required, choose the friction material and the mean radius, compute the capacity with the formula, and check the heat and the wear.

Key ideaHeat is the main limit for brakes, and torque capacity is the main limit for clutches. Both use the same friction formula.

Which two quantities must a brake designer check besides the torque?

The heat generated (energy and average power, which must be removed) and the wear of the lining over its life.

Why does a larger disc help with braking?

A larger disc has a larger surface area to shed heat, and a larger mean radius that increases the torque for the same clamping force.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original, written in the GATE style, and they are not copied from any real exam paper. Try the "How to think about it" steps yourself before reading the answer.

Band brake torque from two tensions

A band brake has a tight side tension F1 = 1000 N and a slack side tension F2 = 500 N, and the drum radius is 0.1 m. What is the braking torque, in N m? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The braking torque T.
  2. Which idea applies. T = (F1 - F2) r.
  3. Work it out. F1 - F2 = 500 N. Then T = 500 times 0.1 = 50 N m.
  4. Check. The tension difference is 500 N and the radius is 0.1 m, so the torque is 50 N m. The units match because radius is in metres.

Answer: 50 N m.

Energy absorbed in a stop

A vehicle of mass 1000 kg slows from 10 m/s to rest. How much kinetic energy must the brakes absorb, in kJ? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The energy absorbed, in kJ.
  2. Which idea applies. E = (1/2) m v2, the change in kinetic energy.
  3. Work it out. E = (1/2) times 1000 times 102 = 50000 J = 50 kJ.
  4. Check. The speed is 10 m/s, so the energy is a few tens of kilojoules for a car of this mass, which is a reasonable size.

Answer: 50 kJ.

Statements about brakes and clutches

Which of the following statements about brakes and clutches are correct? Select all that apply. (MSQ)

  • (A) A band brake torque grows with the wrap angle, through the tension ratio.
  • (B) Kinetic energy absorbed in a stop is proportional to the speed, not the square of the speed.
  • (C) The uniform wear mean radius is the average of the inner and outer radii.
  • (D) The torque capacity of a clutch depends on the number of friction faces.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true. More than one may be correct.
  2. Which idea applies. The tension ratio emu theta, the kinetic energy formula, the uniform wear mean radius, and the clutch capacity formula with n.
  3. Work it out. (A) True: a larger theta raises the ratio. (B) False: energy goes as v squared. (C) True: (R + r)/2. (D) True: T = n mu F rm.
  4. Check. Statement (B) contradicts the square in the kinetic energy formula, so it must be false.

Answer: (A), (C) and (D) are correct.

Clutch torque with uniform wear

A single plate clutch has lining outer radius R = 120 mm and inner radius r = 80 mm. The axial clamping force is F = 800 N and mu = 0.25. Using the uniform wear model, what is the torque capacity, in N m? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The torque capacity in N m.
  2. Which idea applies. rm = (R + r)/2 for uniform wear, and T = n mu F rm with n = 2 for a single plate.
  3. Work it out. rm = 100 mm. T = 2 times 0.25 times 800 times 100 = 40000 N mm = 40 N m.
  4. Check. The answer is in N m after dividing by 1000, and it is a sensible size for a small clutch.

Answer: 40 N m.

Band brake with a half turn of wrap

A band brake has mu = 0.25 and the band wraps half way round the drum (theta = pi radians). The tight side tension is F1 = 2000 N and the drum radius is 0.15 m. What is the braking torque, in N m, to one decimal place? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The torque T.
  2. Which idea applies. F1/F2 = emu theta, then T = (F1 - F2) r.
  3. Work it out. e0.25 pi = e0.7854 = 2.1933. Then F2 = 2000/2.1933 = 911.9 N. The difference is 2000 - 911.9 = 1088.1 N, and T = 1088.1 times 0.15 = 163.2 N m.
  4. Check. The slack side is less than the tight side, as it should be. The torque is positive and in a sensible range.

Answer: 163.2 N m.

Clamping force for a required clutch capacity

A single plate clutch has R = 100 mm and r = 60 mm, mu = 0.3, and two friction faces. The clutch must carry 45 N m with uniform pressure. What is the axial clamping force required, in newtons, to one decimal place? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The clamping force F.
  2. Which idea applies. Uniform pressure mean radius, then T = n mu F rm, rearranged to F = T/(n mu rm).
  3. Work it out. rm = (2/3)(1003 - 603)/(1002 - 602) = 81.67 mm. Then F = 45000 N mm/(2 times 0.3 times 81.67) = 45000/49.0 = 918.4 N.
  4. Check. Substitute back: T = 2 times 0.3 times 918.4 times 81.67 = 45000 N mm = 45 N m. The check works.

Answer: 918.4 N.

Average braking power on a steep descent

A vehicle of mass 1500 kg is travelling at 30 m/s and brings itself to rest in 10 s with the brakes. What is the average power the brakes absorb, in kW, to one decimal place? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The average power in kW.
  2. Which idea applies. Energy E = (1/2) m v2, then average power P = E/t.
  3. Work it out. E = 0.5 times 1500 times 302 = 0.5 times 1500 times 900 = 675000 J = 675 kJ. P = 675000/10 = 67500 W = 67.5 kW.
  4. Check. The speed is three times that of the earlier example, so the energy is nine times larger, and the time is also longer, so the power is larger but not nine times larger. The value 67.5 kW is consistent with that.

Answer: 67.5 kW.

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Single plate clutch (NAT, 2 marks)

A single plate clutch with two friction surfaces has inner and outer diameters 100 mm and 200 mm, μ = 0.3 and axial force 4 kN. Using uniform wear, find the torque in N·m.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. T = nμF rm, with rm = (ro + ri)/2.
  2. Which idea applies. Uniform wear mean radius.
  3. Work it out. rm = 75 mm. T = 2 · 0.3 · 4000 · 0.075 = 180 N·m.
  4. Check. Uniform pressure would give a slightly larger mean radius (77.8 mm).

Answer: 180 N·m.

Exam-level 2: Band brake (NAT, 2 marks)

A simple band brake has wrap 270° and μ = 0.25. The slack side tension is 500 N. Find the braking torque on a drum of radius 0.2 m, correct to 1 decimal place.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. T = (T1 − T2)r.
  2. Which idea applies. T1/T2 = eμθ.
  3. Work it out. e0.25 · 4.712 = 3.248. T1 = 1624.1 N. Torque = 1124.1 · 0.2 = 224.8 N·m.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
import math
T1 = 500 * math.exp(0.25 * 1.5 * math.pi)
print("%.1f N, torque %.1f N m" % (T1, (T1 - 500) * 0.2))

1624.1 N, torque 224.8 N m

Answer: about 224.8 N·m.

Exam-level 3: Brake energy (NAT, 2 marks)

A 1200 kg car stops from 72 km/h. Find the energy absorbed by the brakes in kJ.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Kinetic energy.
  2. Which idea applies. 72 km/h = 20 m/s.
  3. Work it out. 0.5 · 1200 · 400 = 240 kJ.
  4. Check. This heat must be dissipated by the brake discs.

Answer: 240 kJ.

Summary

Here are the one-line meanings of the main ideas on this page.

Brake
A device that absorbs kinetic energy as heat through friction.
Clutch
A friction coupling that connects or disconnects two shafts.
Coefficient of friction mu
The ratio of friction force to normal force, typically 0.2 to 0.5 for linings.
Friction torque
Friction force times the radius at which it acts.
Kinetic energy
One half m v squared, the energy a brake must absorb.
Average braking power
Energy absorbed divided by the time of the stop.
Block brake
A short shoe pressed on a drum, with torque mu N r.
Band brake
A flexible band wrapped round a drum, with torque from the tension difference.
Tension ratio
F1/F2 = e to the power mu theta, with theta in radians.
Mean radius
The effective radius of an annular friction surface.
Uniform pressure
The model with equal pressure on the pad, giving the cubic mean radius formula.
Uniform wear
The model with equal wear, giving the average of the two radii.
Torque capacity
n mu F rm for a disc brake or friction clutch.
Centrifugal clutch
A clutch that engages automatically when the speed is high enough.
Centrifugal force
m r omega squared, which grows with the square of the speed.
Fade
Loss of friction in a brake when the lining becomes too hot.

Remember these

  1. Friction torque is mu N r, and every brake and clutch formula is built from it.
  2. The energy a brake absorbs is one half m v squared, so it grows with the square of the speed.
  3. Band brake tension ratio is e to the power mu theta, with theta in radians.
  4. Use the mean radius, never the outer radius, for a disc or clutch.
  5. Clutch capacity is n mu F rm, where n counts friction faces.
  6. Brakes are limited by heat, and clutches by torque capacity.

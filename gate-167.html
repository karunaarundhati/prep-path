Torsion of circular shafts

A shaft is a rod that carries a turning force, such as the drive shaft of a motor or the joint of a robot arm. When it is twisted, shear stress grows from the centre to the outside, and the shaft turns through a small angle. This page explains the torsion equation, the polar moment of inertia and the power formula, all of which are common GATE Robotics and Automation questions.

The idea in plain words

Welcome. Take a stick of chalk and hold one end in each hand. Twist one hand while holding the other still. The chalk resists, and at some point it snaps. Twist a rubber tube in the same way and it turns through an angle, and the rubber near the outside surface stretches more than the rubber near the middle. This is torsion: a twisting load applied about the axis of a shaft. A shaft is a long round bar that carries that twisting load from one place to another. The motor in a washing machine turns a drum through a shaft. A robot joint turns its link through a shaft.

Here is the key picture to hold in your mind. Imagine a bundle of thin straws glued side by side, forming a round rod. Twist the bundle. Each straw slides a little relative to its neighbours. The straws on the outside slide the most, and the straw at the very centre hardly slides at all. Shear stress follows the same pattern: it is zero at the centre and largest at the outer surface. This is the single most important fact about torsion.

Why does this matter for GATE? Torsion is a standard part of GATE Robotics and Automation (RA) mechanics. Questions ask for the shear stress at the surface, the angle of twist of a shaft of given length, the diameter needed for a given torque, or the power transmitted at a given speed. All of these use the same small set of formulas, which are built on the picture above. Learn the formulas and their units, and most torsion questions become a matter of careful substitution.

We will go slowly. Every formula is explained first with a picture, then given with symbols, then used on a small example with every step shown.

Key terms

Read this list once now and return to it whenever a word feels new.

Shaft
A long, usually round, rod that transmits power by turning. Plain meaning: a turning rod.
Torsion
Twisting of a shaft about its own axis by a torque. Plain meaning: wringing.
Torque (T)
The turning effect of a force about the axis of the shaft. Plain meaning: how hard the shaft is being twisted. Unit: N mm or N m.
Shear stress (tau)
The sliding stress in the shaft caused by torsion. Unit: MPa (N/mm2).
Radius (r)
The distance from the centre of the shaft to the point where the stress is wanted. Unit: mm.
Polar moment of inertia (J)
A property of the cross-section that measures how well the shape resists twisting. Plain meaning: the torsional stiffness of the shape. Unit: mm4.
Modulus of rigidity (G)
The shear modulus of the material, which links shear stress to shear strain. Unit: MPa or GPa. Plain meaning: how stiff the material is in shear.
Angle of twist (theta)
The angle through which one end of the shaft turns relative to the other, in radians. Plain meaning: how far the shaft unwinds.
Length (L)
The length of the shaft over which the twist is measured. Unit: mm.
Solid shaft
A shaft with no hole through its centre. Plain meaning: a full round bar.
Hollow shaft
A shaft with a hole through its centre, usually a tube. Plain meaning: a pipe.
Outer diameter (D) and inner diameter (d)
The outside and inside diameters of a hollow shaft. For a solid shaft, only the outer diameter is used.
Speed (N)
The rotation rate of the shaft in revolutions per minute (rpm).
Power (P)
The rate at which work is done by the torque. Unit: watt (W), or kW.
Rigid body rotation
Motion where the whole body turns without deforming. Plain meaning: turning as one piece.

What torsion is and why shafts twist

The twisting load

A torque is a turning effect. You feel a torque when you turn a tap, a bicycle pedal or a screwdriver. The torque is the force times the distance from the axis: T = F x r. If a 20 N force acts at a radius of 0.5 m from the axis, the torque is 20 x 0.5 = 10 N m. In a shaft, the torque is carried along the length and resisted by the material inside.

When a shaft carries a constant torque from one end to the other, it does not accelerate. The torque applied at one end is balanced by the torque at the other end. Between the two ends, every thin slice of the shaft carries the same torque T. This is an important assumption, and it holds when the shaft is straight, round, and the torque acts along its axis.

                  torque T applied here
                       |
                       v
     ==================================  <- shaft, length L
     |                                |
     fixed end                        torque T at the other end
     (holds the shaft still)          (balances the first)

   Each cross-section carries the same torque T.
   The outer fibres twist the most, the centre the least.

Key ideaTorsion makes shear stress, not normal stress, inside a round shaft. This is why a twisted chalk stick breaks along a slanted line, at 45 degrees to its axis, where the normal stress is largest.

A 30 N force acts at a radius of 0.2 m from the axis of a shaft. What is the torque?

T = F x r = 30 x 0.2 = 6 N m.

Does the torque change from one end of a straight shaft to the other, when no torque is applied along the length?

No. The torque stays the same along the shaft, and it is balanced at the two ends.

The torsion equation T/J = tau/r = G theta/L

Three ratios that are equal

The whole torsion theory for a round shaft is captured in one line that links four quantities. The shear stress tau at a radius r, the torque T, the polar moment J, the modulus G, the angle of twist theta and the length L are related by:

T / J = tau / r = G theta / L

Read this carefully. The three ratios are equal, so any two can be used to find a third. The first ratio, T/J, links the torque to the shape. The second, tau/r, gives the shear stress at radius r. The third, G theta/L, links the material and the twist.

Using the first and second ratios

From T/J = tau/r, the shear stress at radius r is:

tau = T r / J

Here tau is the shear stress in MPa, T is the torque in N mm, r is the radius in mm, and J is the polar moment in mm4. The unit check works: N mm times mm, divided by mm4, gives N/mm2, which is MPa. The largest stress is at the outer surface, where r = R (the outer radius). So:

tau max = T R / J

TrapUse the radius, not the diameter, in tau = Tr/J. A common error is to use the diameter, which gives a stress twice as large as the correct value.

Worked example: shear stress at the surface

A solid shaft has diameter d = 50 mm and carries a torque T = 2 kN m = 2 x 106 N mm. Find the maximum shear stress.

  • Step 1: radius R = d/2 = 25 mm.
  • Step 2: polar moment J = pi d4/32 = pi x 504/32 = pi x 6250000/32 = 613592.3 mm4 (the next section explains where this comes from).
  • Step 3: tau max = T R/J = 2 x 106 x 25/613592.3 = 50 x 106/613592.3 = 81.49 MPa.

The Python check at the end of the next section repeats these numbers.

Why is the shear stress tau = Tr/J proportional to r?

Because the shear strain grows in a straight line from the centre to the surface, and the stress follows the strain. At twice the radius the stress is twice as large.

Which radius is used for the largest stress in a solid shaft of radius 25 mm?

The outer radius, 25 mm, because the stress is largest at the outer surface.

Polar moment of inertia for solid and hollow shafts

What J measures

The polar moment of inertia J measures how the cross-section resists twisting. It is built up from the area and its distance from the centre. Think of each tiny ring of area in the section. A ring far from the centre is harder to twist than a ring near the centre, because it has a longer lever arm. J adds up these contributions, with the distance squared, over the whole section. The bigger the J, the stiffer the shaft is in torsion.

Solid shaft

J = pi d4 / 32

Here d is the diameter of the solid shaft. The power 4 is very important. Doubling the diameter multiplies J by 16. A shaft twice as thick is sixteen times stiffer in torsion. This is why shaft diameter is so sensitive in design.

Hollow shaft

J = pi (D4 - d4) / 32

Here D is the outer diameter and d is the inner (hole) diameter. A hollow shaft has the same outer diameter as a solid one, but it is lighter, because the material near the centre contributes little to J. The hollow shaft gives up only a small amount of stiffness for a large saving of weight. This is why many shafts are hollow.

Worked example: hollow shaft

A hollow shaft has outer diameter D = 60 mm and inner diameter d = 40 mm. Find J.

  • Step 1: D4 = 604 = 12960000 mm4.
  • Step 2: d4 = 404 = 2560000 mm4.
  • Step 3: the difference is 12960000 - 2560000 = 10400000.
  • Step 4: J = pi x 10400000/32 = 1021017.6 mm4.
  • Step 5: the shear stress at the outer surface for T = 2 x 106 N mm is tau = T (D/2)/J = 2 x 106 x 30/1021017.6 = 58.76 MPa.
import math
T = 2.0e6
d = 50.0
J = math.pi * d ** 4 / 32
r = d / 2
tau = T * r / J
print("J =", round(J, 1), "mm^4")
print("tau max =", round(tau, 2), "MPa")
print("tau at r = 10 mm:", round(tau * 10 / r, 2), "MPa")
Jh = math.pi * (60 ** 4 - 40 ** 4) / 32
print("hollow J =", round(Jh, 1), "mm^4")
print("hollow tau at outer surface:", round(T * 30 / Jh, 2), "MPa")
J = 613592.3 mm^4
tau max = 81.49 MPa
tau at r = 10 mm: 32.59 MPa
hollow J = 1021017.6 mm^4
hollow tau at outer surface: 58.76 MPa

GATE loves thisA question may ask whether a hollow shaft or a solid shaft of the same outer diameter has a larger J. The solid one has the larger J, because it has more material near the centre. The hollow shaft is chosen for its lower weight, not for its stiffness.

What is J for a solid shaft of diameter 20 mm?

J = pi x 204/32 = pi x 160000/32 = 15707.96 mm4.

If the diameter of a solid shaft is doubled, by what factor does J change?

By 16, because J is proportional to d4.

Shear stress distribution across a shaft

A straight line from the centre

Put the formula tau = Tr/J together with the picture of the straw bundle. Since tau is proportional to r, the shear stress grows in a straight line from zero at the centre to its maximum at the outer surface. Halfway out, the stress is half the maximum. At one-fifth of the radius, it is one-fifth of the maximum. This linear pattern is the reason the centre of a solid shaft carries so little torque.

Shear stress tau across a solid shaft (cross-section):

   tau
    ^
    |                              /
    |                           /
    |                        /
    |                     /
    |                  /
    |               /
    |            /
    |         /
    |      /
    |   /
    +--+----------------------------> r
       0          R (outside)

   tau = 0 at the centre, tau max at the surface.

Hollow shafts

For a hollow shaft, the stress is still linear with radius, but it does not start at zero. At the inner radius r = d/2 it is smaller, and it rises to the maximum at the outer radius. Because the inside is empty, the stress there is not zero, and that is not a problem, because the inside is no longer part of the shaft.

TipIn a question about the stress at a particular radius, use tau = Tr/J with that radius. Do not use the outer radius by mistake.

What is the shear stress at half the outer radius in a solid shaft, as a fraction of the maximum?

One half of the maximum, because the stress is proportional to r.

Where does a solid shaft carry most of its torque?

Near the outer surface, because the stress and the lever arm are both largest there.

Angle of twist

How far does the shaft turn?

When a shaft is twisted, one end turns relative to the other. The angle through which this happens is the angle of twist, theta, in radians. From the third ratio of the torsion equation, G theta/L = tau/r, we find theta:

theta = T L / (G J)

Here theta is the angle of twist in radians, T is the torque, L is the length, G is the modulus of rigidity and J is the polar moment. The formula shows three things. A longer shaft twists more. A stiffer material (larger G) and a stiffer shape (larger J) twist less. A larger torque twists more. Each of these is the direction you would expect.

Angles in these formulas are in radians. To convert to degrees, multiply by 180/pi, which is about 57.3. A full turn is 2 pi radians, which is 360 degrees.

Worked example

The solid shaft from the earlier example has J = 613592.3 mm4, torque T = 2 x 106 N mm and length L = 1000 mm. The modulus of rigidity is G = 80000 MPa (steel). Find the angle of twist.

  • Step 1: T L = 2 x 106 x 1000 = 2 x 109.
  • Step 2: G J = 80000 x 613592.3 = 4.9087 x 1010.
  • Step 3: theta = 2 x 109/4.9087 x 1010 = 0.04074 rad.
  • Step 4: in degrees, 0.04074 x 57.3 = 2.33 degrees.
import math
T = 2.0e6
L = 1000.0
G = 80000.0
d = 50.0
J = math.pi * d ** 4 / 32
theta = T * L / (G * J)
print("angle of twist =", round(theta, 5), "rad")
print("angle in degrees =", round(math.degrees(theta), 2))
angle of twist = 0.04074 rad
angle in degrees = 2.33

TrapKeep the units consistent: N mm, mm, MPa and mm4. If the length is given in metres, convert it to mm before you divide.

If the length of a shaft doubles and everything else stays the same, what happens to the angle of twist?

It doubles, because theta is proportional to L.

A shaft twists by 0.1 rad. Roughly how many degrees is this?

0.1 x 57.3 = 5.73 degrees.

Power transmitted by a shaft

Power is torque times speed

A motor does not only twist a shaft. It transmits power, which is the rate at which the torque does work. A shaft turning at a constant speed carries power, and the power depends on the torque and the speed. In SI units, with the speed N in revolutions per minute (rpm) and the torque in N m:

P = 2 pi N T / 60

Here P is the power in watts, N is the speed in rpm, and T is the torque in N m. The factor 2 pi N/60 converts the speed from revolutions per minute into radians per second. Each revolution is 2 pi radians, and each minute has 60 seconds. So multiplying torque (N m) by angular speed (rad/s) gives watts (N m/s).

Worked example

A shaft turns at N = 600 rpm and carries a torque of T = 1000 N m. Find the power in kW.

  • Step 1: angular speed = 2 pi x 600/60 = 62.83 rad/s.
  • Step 2: power = T x angular speed = 1000 x 62.83 = 62830 W.
  • Step 3: in kilowatts, 62830/1000 = 62.83 kW.
import math
N = 600.0
T = 1000.0
P = 2 * math.pi * N * T / 60
print("power =", round(P / 1000, 2), "kW")
power = 62.83 kW

Finding the diameter from a stress limit

A common design task is the reverse. Given the torque and an allowed shear stress, find the diameter. Put tau max = T R/J with R = d/2 and J = pi d4/32. After simplifying, the diameter is:

d = cube root of (16 T / (pi tau))

Here the torque T is in N mm and the allowed stress tau is in MPa. The cube root means the number that, multiplied by itself three times, gives the value inside. This formula is valid for a solid shaft.

TipIf a question asks for a diameter, check your answer by putting it back into tau = Tr/J. You should get the allowed stress.

A shaft turns at 300 rpm with a torque of 500 N m. What is the power in kW, to the nearest 0.1?

P = 2 pi x 300 x 500/60 = 15708 W, which is 15.7 kW.

Why do we convert rpm to radians per second?

Because power in watts is torque in N m times angular speed in rad/s. The factor 2 pi/60 does the conversion.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original, written in the style of GATE. They are not real past questions. Try each one before you read the solution.

Polar moment of a small shaft

Find the polar moment of inertia of a solid shaft of diameter 20 mm, in mm4, to two decimal places. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. J in mm4.
  2. Which idea applies. J = pi d4/32 for a solid shaft.
  3. Work it out. d4 = 204 = 160000. J = pi x 160000/32 = 15707.96 mm4.
  4. Check. A shaft of diameter 40 mm has 16 times this J, as the d4 rule predicts.

Answer: 15707.96 mm4.

Torque from power

A shaft transmits 10 kW at 300 rpm. Find the torque in N m, to two decimal places. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. T in N m.
  2. Which idea applies. P = 2 pi N T/60, so T = 60 P/(2 pi N).
  3. Work it out. P = 10000 W. T = 60 x 10000/(2 pi x 300) = 600000/1884.96 = 318.31 N m.
  4. Check. Multiply back: 2 pi x 300 x 318.31/60 = 10000 W. The check works.

Answer: 318.31 N m.

Shear stress at the surface (NAT)

A solid shaft of diameter 40 mm carries a torque of 1 kN m. Find the maximum shear stress in MPa, to two decimal places. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. tau max in MPa.
  2. Which idea applies. tau max = T R/J, with R = 20 mm and J = pi d4/32.
  3. Work it out. J = pi x 404/32 = 251327.41 mm4. T = 106 N mm. tau max = 106 x 20/251327.41 = 79.58 MPa.
  4. Check. Using the diameter by mistake would give 159.15 MPa, which is twice the answer. The radius gives the right size.

Answer: 79.58 MPa.

Which statements are correct? (MSQ)

For a straight circular shaft in pure torsion, which of the following statements are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) The shear stress is zero at the centre of a solid shaft
(B) The angle of twist is proportional to the length of the shaft
(C) The torque is proportional to the modulus of rigidity G
(D) The maximum shear stress in a solid shaft occurs at its centre

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Every correct statement.
  2. Which idea applies. tau = Tr/J, theta = TL/(GJ), and the fact that T does not depend on G.
  3. Work it out. (A) is correct, because tau = 0 when r = 0. (B) is correct, since theta = TL/(GJ) is proportional to L. (C) is wrong: T is the applied torque, which does not depend on G. (D) is wrong: the maximum is at the outer surface.
  4. Check. Two correct statements. The two wrong ones confuse what depends on the material and where the stress is largest.

Answer: (A) and (B).

Diameter for an allowed stress (NAT)

A solid shaft must carry a torque of 1 kN m with an allowed shear stress of 50 MPa. Find the minimum diameter in mm, to two decimal places. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The smallest diameter d in mm, to one decimal place.
  2. Which idea applies. d = cube root of (16 T/(pi tau)), with T in N mm.
  3. Work it out. 16 x 106/(pi x 50) = 16 x 106/157.08 = 101859.16. Its cube root is 46.70 mm, to two decimal places.
  4. Check. With d = 46.70 mm, J = pi x 46.704/32 = 466947 mm4 (about), and tau = 106 x 23.35/466947 = 50.0 MPa, which matches the allowed stress.

Answer: 46.70 mm.

Angle of twist in degrees (NAT)

A solid steel shaft of diameter 50 mm and length 1 m carries a torque of 2 kN m. Take G = 80 GPa. Find the angle of twist in degrees, to two decimal places. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The twist in degrees.
  2. Which idea applies. theta = TL/(GJ) in radians, then multiply by 180/pi.
  3. Work it out. J = 613592.3 mm4, T = 2 x 106 N mm, L = 1000 mm, G = 80000 MPa. theta = 0.04074 rad, which is 2.33 degrees. The Python check in the chapter gives the same value.
  4. Check. A twist of about 0.04 rad is a small angle, so the result is plausible.

Answer: 2.33 degrees.

Stress at the outer surface of a hollow shaft (NAT)

A hollow shaft has outer diameter 60 mm and inner diameter 40 mm. It carries a torque of 2 kN m. Find the maximum shear stress in MPa, to two decimal places. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. tau at the outer surface of the hollow shaft.
  2. Which idea applies. tau max = T R/J with R = 30 mm and J = pi (D4 - d4)/32.
  3. Work it out. J = 1021017.6 mm4. tau max = 2 x 106 x 30/1021017.6 = 58.76 MPa.
  4. Check. The solid shaft of diameter 60 mm would give a smaller stress, because its J is larger, so the hollow shaft is more highly stressed for the same torque. This is consistent with the lighter weight of the hollow shaft.

Answer: 58.76 MPa.

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Shaft stress (NAT, 2 marks)

A solid shaft of 50 mm diameter transmits 2 kN·m. Find the maximum shear stress in MPa, correct to 2 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. τ = 16T/(πd³).
  2. Which idea applies. Torsion formula.
  3. Work it out. 16 · 2e6/(π · 125000) = 81.49 MPa.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
import math
print("%.2f" % (16 * 2e6 / (math.pi * 50 ** 3)))

81.49

Answer: about 81.49 MPa.

Exam-level 2: Power and torque (NAT, 2 marks)

A shaft transmits 30 kW at 600 rpm. Find the torque in N·m, correct to 1 decimal place.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. T = P/ω.
  2. Which idea applies. ω = 2πN/60.
  3. Work it out. 30000/62.83 = 477.5 N·m.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
import math

Exam-level 3: Hollow versus solid (NAT, 2 marks)

A hollow shaft has outer diameter D and inner diameter 0.5D. What is the ratio of its torque capacity to that of a solid shaft of diameter D at the same allowable stress?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The ratio of polar section moduli.
  2. Which idea applies. Zp ∝ (D⁴ − d⁴)/D.
  3. Work it out. 1 − 0.5⁴ = 0.9375.
  4. Check. It keeps 93.75% of the strength with 75% of the material.

Answer: 0.9375.

  1. Use the radius, not the diameter, in tau = Tr/J.
  2. Shear stress is largest at the outer surface of a shaft.
  3. J for a solid shaft grows with the fourth power of the diameter.
  4. The angle of twist is proportional to the length and inversely proportional to G J.

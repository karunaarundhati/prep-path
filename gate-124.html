Thevenin's & Norton's theorems

Thevenin's and Norton's theorems let you replace a complicated circuit, seen from two terminals, with one simple source and one resistor. The load then becomes easy to analyse. These two theorems are among the most used tools in GATE network questions.

The idea in plain words

Imagine a sealed black box with two terminals sticking out. Inside the box is a messy network of sources and resistors. You are not allowed to open the box. You are allowed to connect a load to the two terminals and measure what happens. Could you describe the box with something much simpler, one that behaves the same way from the outside?

Thevenin's theorem says yes, for any linear circuit. The box is equivalent to a single voltage source Vth in series with a single resistor Rth. Norton's theorem says the box is also equivalent to a single current source IN in parallel with a single resistor RN. The two descriptions are two views of the same box.

Why does this matter? Once the box is replaced, the load is part of a tiny circuit that you can solve in one or two lines. This is the fastest way to find the current, voltage or power in one load, especially when the load is changed many times. GATE RA and DA questions use this idea very often.

Key ideaFrom the load's point of view, any linear network is just one voltage source in series with one resistor, or one current source in parallel with one resistor.

Key terms

Each term has a plain meaning first, then a precise definition.

Two-terminal network
A circuit with only two connection points for the outside world. Precisely: the box that the theorem replaces.
Load
The part you connect to the terminals. Precisely: the element whose current or voltage you want.
Open circuit
A break with no path for current. Precisely: zero current, any voltage.
Short circuit
A plain wire with zero resistance. Precisely: zero voltage, any current.
Open-circuit voltage Voc
The voltage across the terminals with nothing connected. Precisely: it is Vth.
Short-circuit current Isc
The current when the terminals are joined by a wire. Precisely: it is IN.
Thevenin voltage Vth
The voltage of the Thevenin source. Precisely: equal to Voc.
Thevenin resistance Rth
The resistance in series with Vth. Precisely: the resistance seen from the terminals with independent sources deactivated.
Norton current IN
The current of the Norton source. Precisely: equal to Isc.
Norton resistance RN
The resistance in parallel with IN. Precisely: equal to Rth.
Deactivate a source
Switch a source off for a resistance calculation. Precisely: voltage sources become short circuits and current sources become open circuits.
Current divider
A rule that splits a current between two parallel branches in inverse proportion to their resistances.

Thevenin's theorem

What the theorem says

Thevenin's theorem states that a linear two-terminal network can be replaced by a voltage source Vth in series with a resistance Rth. The load connects to the same two terminals as before, and it sees the same voltage and current as it did with the original network.

   original box                  Thevenin equivalent

  +-----------+                  R_th
  | network   |--a          a o--/\/\/--+
  |  inside   |                |        |
  +-----------+--b          V_th        [ load ]
                                |        |
                             b o--------+

The steps

  1. Remove the load. Disconnect the load from terminals a and b. The load is not part of the box.
  2. Find Vth. Find the open-circuit voltage between a and b, using any method you know. This is Vth.
  3. Find Rth. Deactivate the independent sources, then find the resistance seen from a and b. The next part shows this in detail.
  4. Draw the equivalent. Draw Vth in series with Rth, and connect the load back.
  5. Solve the simple circuit. The load current is I = Vth/(Rth + RL), and the load voltage follows from it.

Worked example 1: a divider with a load

A 12 V ideal source is in series with a 4 ohm resistor. The output terminals a and b are across a 2 ohm resistor. A load RL = 4 ohm is connected across a and b. Find the load current and the load voltage.

   12 V (+ at top) --[ 4 ohm ]--+--- a
                                |
                              [ 2 ohm ]
                                |
                                +--- b  (bottom wire joins the source's -)
  1. Remove the load. With a and b open, the 4 ohm and 2 ohm resistors still form a loop with the source. The loop current is 12/(4 + 2) = 2 A, and that current flows through the 2 ohm resistor.
  2. Find Vth. The 12 V source and the 4 ohm and 2 ohm resistors form a simple divider. The open-circuit voltage across the 2 ohm is Vth = 12 × 2/(4 + 2) = 4 V.
  3. Find Rth. Deactivate the 12 V source by replacing it with a short. The 4 ohm and 2 ohm resistors are then in parallel between a and b. Rth = 4 × 2/(4 + 2) = 4/3 ohm.
  4. Draw the equivalent. A 4 V source, a 4/3 ohm resistor in series, and the 4 ohm load.
  5. Solve. IL = 4/(4/3 + 4) = 4/(16/3) = 3/4 = 0.75 A. The load voltage is 4 ohm × 0.75 A = 3 V.
from fractions import Fraction as F
Vth = F(12 * 2, 4 + 2)
Rth = F(4 * 2, 4 + 2)
RL = F(4)
IL = Vth / (Rth + RL)
VL = IL * RL
print("Vth =", Vth, "V, Rth =", Rth, "ohm")
print("load current =", IL, "A")
print("load voltage =", VL, "V")
# direct check on the original circuit: 4 ohm load in parallel with 2 ohm
Rpar = F(4) * 2 / (4 + 2)
Itot = F(12) / (F(4) + Rpar)
print("direct check of load voltage =", Itot * Rpar, "V")
Vth = 4 V, Rth = 4/3 ohm
load current = 3/4 A
load voltage = 3 V
direct check of load voltage = 3 V

Self-check

Why must the load be removed before finding Vth?

The load is not part of the network. If it stays connected, it draws current and changes the voltage across the terminals. Vth must be the open-circuit voltage, with nothing connected.

In Worked example 1, what happens to the load voltage if RL is changed to 2 ohm?

IL = 4/(4/3 + 2) = 4/(10/3) = 6/5 = 1.2 A, and VL = 1.2 × 2 = 2.4 V. The load voltage falls, because a smaller load takes a larger share of the source's internal drop.

Finding the Thevenin resistance

Method 1: deactivate and look in

This is the usual method. Deactivate every independent source. Voltage sources become short circuits, and current sources become open circuits. Then find the resistance between the two terminals, using series and parallel rules. The result is Rth.

Why does this work? With the sources off, the network is just resistors. The voltage across the terminals, divided by the current, is the resistance. With the sources back on, the Thevenin source supplies the voltage.

Method 2: Voc divided by Isc

Find the open-circuit voltage Voc, as before. Then join the terminals with a wire and find the short-circuit current Isc, with the sources still on. Then:

Rth = Voc / Isc

This method is useful when the network has a dependent source, because you never have to switch the dependent source off. It is also a good check when you have done Method 1.

Worked example 2: both methods on the divider

Use the same network as Worked example 1. We already have Voc = 4 V. Now find the short-circuit current. Join a and b with a wire. The 2 ohm resistor is now shorted, so all the current from the 12 V source flows through the wire. The short-circuit current is Isc = 12/4 = 3 A.

Method 2 gives Rth = 4/3 ohm. Method 1 gave 4/3 ohm as well. The two methods agree.

from fractions import Fraction as F
Voc = F(12 * 2, 4 + 2)
Isc = F(12, 4)
Rth_method2 = Voc / Isc
Rth_method1 = F(4 * 2, 4 + 2)
print("Voc =", Voc, "V, Isc =", Isc, "A")
print("Rth by Voc/Isc =", Rth_method2, "ohm")
print("Rth by deactivation =", Rth_method1, "ohm")
print("methods agree:", Rth_method2 == Rth_method1)
Voc = 4 V, Isc = 3 A
Rth by Voc/Isc = 4/3 ohm
Rth by deactivation = 4/3 ohm
methods agree: True

TrapDeactivate only the independent sources. A dependent source is not switched off, because its value depends on the circuit. If you switch it off, Rth will be wrong. This is why Method 2 is safer for dependent sources.

Self-check

A network has Voc = 10 V and Isc = 2 A. What is Rth?

Rth = 10/2 = 5 ohm.

What is the short-circuit current of a network whose Vth is 8 V and Rth is 2 ohm?

Isc = Vth/Rth = 8/2 = 4 A. This is the same as the Norton current, which we meet next.

Norton's theorem

What the theorem says

Norton's theorem gives the same box a different picture. The network is equivalent to a current source IN in parallel with a resistance RN. The Norton current is the short-circuit current, and the Norton resistance equals the Thevenin resistance.

IN = Isc, RN = Rth

The Norton source is drawn as a circle with an arrow, with RN beside it in parallel. The load connects across the same two terminals.

        +-------+------+
        |       |      |
        v     I_N     R_N     [ load ]
        |       |      |
        +-------+------+

Converting between the two forms

The two forms are linked by source transformation, which you met on the electric network page. The relations are:

Vth = IN × Rth, IN = Vth/Rth

Example: a Thevenin source of 10 V with 5 ohm becomes a Norton source of 10/5 = 2 A with 5 ohm in parallel. Going back, 2 A × 5 ohm = 10 V. The outside behaviour is the same.

Worked example 3: the Norton form of the divider

Take the same network as Worked example 1. We have Isc = 3 A (from Worked example 2) and Rth = 4/3 ohm. So IN = 3 A and RN = 4/3 ohm. Check: Vth = IN × RN = 3 × 4/3 = 4 V, which matches.

Now find the load current with the 4 ohm load. The 3 A divides between the parallel branches of 4/3 ohm and the load of 4 ohm. Using the current divider:

  1. Current into the load. IL = IN × RN/(RN + RL) = 3 × (4/3)/(4/3 + 4).
  2. Simplify. (4/3)/(16/3) = 1/4. So IL = 3 × 1/4 = 0.75 A.
  3. Check. This matches the Thevenin answer of 0.75 A from Worked example 1.
from fractions import Fraction as F
IN = F(12, 4)
RN = F(4 * 2, 4 + 2)
RL = F(4)
IL = IN * RN / (RN + RL)
print("IN =", IN, "A, RN =", RN, "ohm")
print("load current by current divider =", IL, "A")
print("Thevenin voltage from IN*RN =", IN * RN, "V")
IN = 3 A, RN = 4/3 ohm
load current by current divider = 3/4 A
Thevenin voltage from IN*RN = 4 V

TipUse Thevenin when the load is in series with the source. Use Norton when the load is in parallel with the source, because then the current divider is the natural tool.

Self-check

A Norton source has IN = 6 A and RN = 3 ohm. What is the Thevenin equivalent?

Vth = IN × RN = 6 × 3 = 18 V, with Rth = 3 ohm.

Is the Norton resistance the same as the Thevenin resistance?

Yes. Both are found the same way, and the two equivalents share one resistance. Only the source type differs.

Traps with dependent sources and loads

The load must not be inside the network

A common mistake is to find Vth with the load still connected. That gives a voltage that the load itself changed. Always remove the load first.

Dependent sources

A dependent source is part of the network, so it stays in the circuit when you find Vth. When you find Rth, the dependent source is also kept on, because its value depends on the rest of the circuit. This is why Method 2 (Voc/Isc) is often safer. It uses the circuit as it really is, with every source still active.

TrapA resistance found by switching off a dependent source is not the Thevenin resistance. Switch off only the independent sources.

GATE loves thisMany GATE questions give a circuit with a dependent source and ask for Rth. The safest route is Voc divided by Isc, with every source left on.

The case with no independent sources

If the network contains only dependent sources and resistors, Vth is zero. The network then has no energy source of its own. Rth is found by connecting a test source of known voltage to the terminals, and dividing that voltage by the current it draws. In practice, most GATE questions avoid this case, but it is worth knowing.

Self-check

Why is Voc/Isc safer than deactivation when a dependent source is present?

It uses the circuit with every source still active, so the dependent source keeps its correct rule. Deactivation would change the dependent source's behaviour.

Should the load be connected when finding Vth?

No. Vth is the open-circuit voltage, so the load must be removed. A connected load changes the voltage.

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original problems in the GATE style, not past exam questions. Try each one on paper first.

Thevenin voltage of a divider

A 9 V ideal source is in series with a 2 ohm resistor. The output terminals are across a 1 ohm resistor. Find Vth across the output terminals, in volt.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The open-circuit voltage across the 1 ohm resistor.
  2. Which idea applies. With nothing connected to the terminals, the 9 V source and the two resistors form a divider.
  3. Work it out. Vth = 9 × 1/(2 + 1) = 3 V.
  4. Check. The current is 9/3 = 3 A, and 3 A through 1 ohm gives 3 V. Consistent.

Answer: 3 V.

from fractions import Fraction as F
print("Vth =", F(9 * 1, 2 + 1), "V")
Vth = 3 V

Norton equivalent from a Thevenin source

A network has a Thevenin equivalent of 10 V in series with 5 ohm. Find the Norton current IN in ampere.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The Norton current.
  2. Which idea applies. IN = Vth/Rth.
  3. Work it out. IN = 10/5 = 2 A.
  4. Check. RN = 5 ohm. Then Vth = 2 × 5 = 10 V, which returns the original value.

Answer: 2 A.

Statements about Thevenin and Norton (MSQ)

Select all that apply.

(A) Vth is the open-circuit voltage across the terminals.
(B) Rth can be found by deactivating the independent sources and finding the resistance seen from the terminals.
(C) IN is the open-circuit voltage.
(D) RN is generally different from Rth.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. The definitions of the Thevenin and Norton quantities.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) True. (C) False: IN is the short-circuit current. (D) False: RN = Rth.
  4. Check. Test (C) on the divider: Voc = 4 V, but IN = 3 A. They are different quantities.

Answer: (A) and (B).

Thevenin voltage across a resistor (NAT)

A 10 V ideal source is in series with a 1 ohm resistor. The output terminals are across a 4 ohm resistor. Find the open-circuit voltage across the output terminals, in volt.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Vth, the voltage across the 4 ohm with nothing else connected.
  2. Which idea applies. A voltage divider with the terminals open.
  3. Work it out. Vth = 10 × 4/(1 + 4) = 40/5 = 8 V.
  4. Check. Current = 10/5 = 2 A. Voltage across 4 ohm = 2 × 4 = 8 V.

Answer: 8 V.

from fractions import Fraction as F
print("Vth =", F(10 * 4, 1 + 4), "V")
Vth = 8 V

Thevenin resistance of two branches (NAT)

Two branches join the terminals a and b. Branch 1 is a 3 ohm resistor. Branch 2 is a 4 ohm resistor in series with a 2 ohm resistor and a 12 V ideal source. Find Rth between a and b, in ohm.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Rth, so the source is deactivated.
  2. Which idea applies. Deactivate the 12 V source (replace with a short), then combine resistors.
  3. Work it out. Branch 2 becomes 4 + 2 = 6 ohm. It is in parallel with 3 ohm, so Rth = 6 × 3/(6 + 3) = 18/9 = 2 ohm.
  4. Check. The parallel of 3 and 6 is smaller than either, which is what we expect.

Answer: 2 ohm.

from fractions import Fraction as F
Rth = F(3 * (4 + 2), 3 + (4 + 2))
print("Rth =", Rth, "ohm")
Rth = 2 ohm

Load current from Voc and Isc (NAT)

A network has Voc = 12 V and Isc = 3 A across its terminals. A 2 ohm load is connected. Find the load current in ampere.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The current through the 2 ohm load.
  2. Which idea applies. Rth = Voc/Isc, then the Thevenin load current formula.
  3. Work it out. Rth = 12/3 = 4 ohm. IL = 12/(4 + 2) = 2 A.
  4. Check. Load voltage = 2 × 2 = 4 V. Source drop = 2 × 4 = 8 V. Total 12 V, as required.

Answer: 2 A.

from fractions import Fraction as F
Rth = F(12) / F(3)
IL = F(12) / (Rth + 2)
print("Rth =", Rth, "ohm, IL =", IL, "A")
Rth = 4 ohm, IL = 2 A

Two sources and a load (NAT)

A 12 V source is in series with a 4 ohm resistor, feeding node a. Node a connects to ground through a 2 ohm resistor. A 1 A ideal current source also pushes current into node a from ground. A 4 ohm load is connected from node a to ground, in parallel with the 2 ohm resistor. Find the load voltage in volt. Use Thevenin's theorem with the load removed for Vth.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The voltage across the 4 ohm load.
  2. Which idea applies. Find Vth at node a with the load removed, find Rth with both sources deactivated, then apply the load formula.
  3. Work it out. Open-circuit node a: (12 − V)/4 + 1 = V/2. Multiply by 4: 12 − V + 4 = 2V, so 16 = 3V, and Vth = 16/3 V. Deactivate both sources: Rth = 4 × 2/(4 + 2) = 4/3 ohm. Then IL = (16/3)/(4/3 + 4) = (16/3)/(16/3) = 1 A, and VL = 1 × 4 = 4 V.
  4. Check. Direct check: the node voltage with the load in place is 4 V. KCL: (12 − 4)/4 + 1 = 2 + 1 = 3 A, and 4/2 = 2 A through the 2 ohm, plus 1 A through the load gives 3 A out. Balanced.

Answer: 4 V.

from fractions import Fraction as F
Vth = F(16, 3)
Rth = F(4 * 2, 4 + 2)
RL = F(4)
IL = Vth / (Rth + RL)
print("Vth =", Vth, "V, Rth =", Rth, "ohm")
print("load voltage =", IL * RL, "V")
Vnode = F(4)
print("KCL at node a holds:", (12 - Vnode) / 4 + 1 == Vnode / 2 + Vnode / 4)
Vth = 16/3 V, Rth = 4/3 ohm
load voltage = 4 V
KCL at node a holds: True

Statements about dependent sources (MSQ)

Select all that apply.

(A) For a network with a dependent source, Rth can be found as Voc/Isc with every source still active.
(B) IN = Vth/Rth for a Thevenin and Norton pair.
(C) When finding Rth, the dependent sources are deactivated along with the independent sources.
(D) Vth is found with the load connected to the terminals.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. The Voc/Isc method, the Norton relation, the rule for dependent sources, and the load-removal rule.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) True: IN = Vth/Rth. (C) False: dependent sources stay on. (D) False: the load is removed.
  4. Check. (A) and (B) are the two correct rules used throughout this page. (C) and (D) are the two traps.

Answer: (A) and (B).

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Thevenin equivalent (NAT, 2 marks)

A 12 V source in series with 4 Ω connects to terminals a-b, with 12 Ω across a-b. Find Vth and Rth.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The Thevenin pair seen from a-b.
  2. Which idea applies. Vth is the open circuit voltage. Rth is the resistance with the source shorted.
  3. Work it out. Vth = 12 · 12/16 = 9 V. Rth = 4 ∥ 12 = 3 Ω.
  4. Check. Norton current = 9/3 = 3 A, which equals the short circuit current 12/4.

Answer: Vth = 9 V and Rth = 3 Ω.

Exam-level 2: Load current from Thevenin (NAT, 2 marks)

With the Thevenin equivalent above, find the current in a 6 Ω load.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Load current.
  2. Which idea applies. I = Vth/(Rth + RL).
  3. Work it out. 9/9 = 1 A.
  4. Check. Directly: 12 ∥ 6 = 4 Ω, total 8 Ω, I = 1.5 A, and the 6 Ω share is 1.5 · 12/18 = 1 A.

Answer: 1 A.

Exam-level 3: Thevenin with a dependent source (NAT, 2 marks)

A network has no independent sources and contains a dependent source. When 1 A is injected at the terminals, the terminal voltage is 7.5 V. What is Rth?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Rth from a test source.
  2. Which idea applies. With only dependent sources, Vth = 0 and Rth = Vtest/Itest.
  3. Work it out. 7.5/1 = 7.5 Ω.
  4. Check. Dependent sources can even make Rth negative.

Answer: 7.5 Ω.

Summary

Thevenin's theorem
A linear two-terminal network is equivalent to Vth in series with Rth.
Norton's theorem
The same network is equivalent to IN in parallel with RN.
Open-circuit voltage
The voltage across the terminals with nothing connected. Equal to Vth.
Short-circuit current
The current when the terminals are joined by a wire. Equal to IN.
Thevenin resistance
Found by deactivating independent sources and looking in, or by Voc/Isc.
Norton resistance
Equal to Rth.
Deactivation
Voltage sources become short circuits, current sources become open circuits.
Dependent source
Stays active when finding Vth or Rth.
Load removal
The load is removed before finding Vth.
Thevenin load current
IL = Vth/(Rth + RL).
Norton load current
IL = IN × RN/(RN + RL), by the current divider.
Conversion
Vth = IN Rth and IN = Vth/Rth.
Current divider
Splits a current between parallel branches in inverse proportion to resistance.
Series form
Use Thevenin when the load is in series with the source.
Parallel form
Use Norton when the load is in parallel with the source.

Remember these

  1. Remove the load before finding Vth.
  2. Vth is the open-circuit voltage, and IN is the short-circuit current.
  3. Rth = Voc/Isc is safe with dependent sources.
  4. Deactivate only the independent sources when finding Rth.
  5. RN equals Rth.
  6. Use the form that matches how the load is connected.

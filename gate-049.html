Chi-squared test

The chi-squared test asks whether the counts we actually observed match the counts we expected. It works for a single fixed model (goodness of fit) and for two categories in a table (independence). It is a regular GATE DA topic because the arithmetic is short, but the details, especially degrees of freedom, are easy to get wrong.

The idea in plain words

Imagine a shop owner who believes that a customer is equally likely to pick any of six flavours of ice cream. She records 60 customers. The counts will never match the belief exactly, because real people are random. The real question is not "are the counts exactly equal?" but "are the differences so big that the belief is probably wrong?"

The chi-squared test answers this question. It measures how far the observed counts are from the expected counts with one number, and then asks whether that number is larger than ordinary random chance would give.

Everyday picture: a referee compares the real score of a match with the score a bookmaker predicted. A small gap is normal. A huge gap makes the referee suspicious. The chi-squared statistic is one number that adds up all the gaps, scaled so that they can be compared with a cut-off value from a table.

There are two common versions, and both use the same formula:

  • Goodness of fit. One set of categories and a model that predicts the count in each. Example: "a die is fair."
  • Test of independence. A table with rows and columns. Example: "is gender independent of the pass or fail result?"

Why it matters for GATE DA: questions give you observed counts, ask for expected counts, the statistic, the degrees of freedom and a decision. Marks come from careful bookkeeping, and the steps are always the same.

Key terms

Null hypothesis (H0)
Plain meaning: the "no surprise" belief we test. Precise definition: the statement that the model is correct, or that the two categories are independent.
Alternative hypothesis (H1)
Plain meaning: what we accept if the belief looks wrong. Precise definition: the statement that the model is not correct, or that the categories are related.
Observed count (O)
Plain meaning: what you actually counted. Precise definition: the number of sample items that fall into a category or cell.
Expected count (E)
Plain meaning: what the belief says you should have counted. Precise definition: the count predicted by H0 for a category or cell.
Test statistic
Plain meaning: one number summarising all the gaps. Precise definition: χ² = Σ (O − E)²/E.
Degrees of freedom (df)
Plain meaning: how many numbers are free to change once the totals are fixed. Precise definition: the quantity that picks the chi-squared distribution to use.
Contingency table
Plain meaning: a grid of counts with one variable across rows and another across columns. Precise definition: a table of frequencies cross-classified by two categorical variables.
Significance level (α)
Plain meaning: how rare a result must be before we reject H0. Precise definition: the chance of rejecting H0 when it is true, fixed in advance, usually 0.05 or 0.01.
Critical value
Plain meaning: the cut-off on the chi-squared scale. Precise definition: the value with upper-tail probability α for the given df.
p-value
Plain meaning: how surprising the result is if H0 is true. Precise definition: the probability, assuming H0, of a statistic at least as large as the one observed.
Independence
Plain meaning: knowing one thing tells you nothing about the other. Precise definition: events A and B are independent when P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).

Part 1: The goodness-of-fit test

A fair die shows each face with probability 1/6. Rolled 60 times, each face should appear about 10 times. The goodness-of-fit test tells us whether the differences are small enough to be random noise.

χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E
  • χ² (chi-squared) is the test statistic.
  • Σ (sigma) means "add up over all categories".
  • O is the observed count and E is the expected count for the same category.

Why square? A gap of +5 and a gap of −5 would cancel if we did not. Why divide by E? A gap of 5 is large when you expected 10, but tiny when you expected 1000.

A worked example, every step shown

A die is rolled 60 times and the counts for faces 1 to 6 are 5, 8, 9, 8, 10 and 20. Is the die fair?

Step 1: Hypotheses. H0: the die is fair. H1: it is not.

Step 2: Expected counts. Each face has probability 1/6, so E = 60 × 1/6 = 10 for every face. Check: 6 × 10 = 60, the total number of rolls.

Step 3: Terms. Face 1: (5 − 10)²/10 = 25/10 = 2.5. Face 2: (8 − 10)²/10 = 0.4. Face 3: (9 − 10)²/10 = 0.1. Face 4: (8 − 10)²/10 = 0.4. Face 5: (10 − 10)²/10 = 0. Face 6: (20 − 10)²/10 = 10.

Step 4: Sum. χ² = 2.5 + 0.4 + 0.1 + 0.4 + 0 + 10 = 13.4.

Step 5: Degrees of freedom. For goodness of fit, df = k − 1 − m. Here k = 6 faces and m = 0 parameters estimated from data, so df = 5.

Step 6: Compare. The critical value for df = 5 at α = 0.05 is 11.070. Since 13.4 is larger, reject H0. The die does not look fair at the 5 percent level.

observed = [5, 8, 9, 8, 10, 20]
expected = [10] * 6          # 60 rolls, each face has probability 1/6
chi2 = sum((o - e) ** 2 / e for o, e in zip(observed, expected))
print("Total of observed:", sum(observed))
print("Chi-squared statistic:", round(chi2, 4))
print("Degrees of freedom:", len(observed) - 1)
print("Reject H0 at 5 percent?", chi2 > 11.070)
Total of observed: 60
Chi-squared statistic: 13.4
Degrees of freedom: 5
Reject H0 at 5 percent? True

TrapAlways write the df before you open the table. A cut-off of 11.070 is for df = 5. For df = 1 at the same level, the cut-off is only 3.841.

Why do we divide by E and not by O?

The belief gives us E, so E is the natural yardstick for how big a gap is. Dividing by O would make categories with small observed counts look artificially important.

Can the chi-squared statistic be negative?

No. Each term is a squared number divided by a positive expected count, so it is zero or positive.

Part 2: The test of independence

Two categorical variables are independent when knowing one tells you nothing about the other. In probability terms, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). We do not need the probabilities themselves, only the counts. We work out what the counts would look like under independence, then compare them with what we saw.

E = (row total × column total) / N
  • N is the grand total of all observations in the table.
  • The row total times the column total, divided by N, is the probability rule written in counts.

A worked example, every step shown

Group A has 30 students who passed and 20 who failed. Group B has 20 who passed and 30 who failed. Is the result independent of the group?

Step 1: Hypotheses. H0: result and group are independent. H1: they are related.

Step 2: Totals. Row totals 50 and 50. Column totals 50 (Pass) and 50 (Fail). N = 100.

Step 3: Expected counts. Every cell: (50 × 50)/100 = 25.

Step 4: Terms. (30 − 25)²/25 = 1. (20 − 25)²/25 = 1. (20 − 25)²/25 = 1. (30 − 25)²/25 = 1.

Step 5: Sum. χ² = 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 4.

Step 6: Degrees of freedom. df = (r − 1)(c − 1) = (2 − 1)(2 − 1) = 1. Once the totals are fixed, only one cell is free.

Step 7: Compare. The critical value for df = 1 at 0.05 is 3.841. Since 4 is larger, reject H0.

import math

table = [[30, 20],
         [20, 30]]
row_totals = [sum(r) for r in table]
col_totals = [sum(c) for c in zip(*table)]
N = sum(row_totals)

chi2 = 0.0
for i, row in enumerate(table):
    for j, obs in enumerate(row):
        exp = row_totals[i] * col_totals[j] / N
        chi2 += (obs - exp) ** 2 / exp

df = (len(table) - 1) * (len(table[0]) - 1)
p_value = math.erfc(math.sqrt(chi2 / 2))   # valid for df = 1 only
print("Expected count in each cell:", row_totals[0] * col_totals[0] / N)
print("Chi-squared statistic:", round(chi2, 4))
print("Degrees of freedom:", df)
print("p-value:", round(p_value, 4))
Expected count in each cell: 25.0
Chi-squared statistic: 4.0
Degrees of freedom: 1
p-value: 0.0455

The p-value of 0.0455 is below 0.05, so we reject at the 5 percent level. It is not below 0.01, so at the 1 percent level we would not reject.

TrapThe expected counts come from the totals, never from the observed counts. Compute E for all four cells, not just one.

What is the degrees of freedom for a 3 × 4 table?

df = (3 − 1)(4 − 1) = 2 × 3 = 6.

A 2 × 2 table has row totals 40 and 60, column totals 30 and 70, and N = 100. What is the expected count in the top-left cell?

E = (40 × 30)/100 = 12.

Part 3: Degrees of freedom

Degrees of freedom counts how many numbers are free to change once the totals are fixed. Picture five friends splitting a bill of 100 rupees. The first four can pay any amounts, but the fifth must pay what is left. Four free amounts from five people: "one less than the number of pieces" is the pattern to remember.

Goodness of fit:

df = k − 1 − m
  • k is the number of categories.
  • m is the number of parameters estimated from the sample. If the model was fully given, m = 0.

Example: 4 categories, and a Poisson mean estimated from the same sample. m = 1, so df = 4 − 1 − 1 = 2.

Test of independence:

df = (r − 1)(c − 1)

Here r is the number of rows and c is the number of columns. The sample size N does not appear. A table with a million observations and a 2 × 2 layout still has df = 1.

Key ideaDegrees of freedom depends on the shape of the problem, not on the number of people. Count the constraints and subtract.

A goodness-of-fit test has 5 categories and both the mean and the variance are estimated from the data. What is df?

m = 2, so df = 5 − 1 − 2 = 2.

Part 4: Critical values, decisions and p-values

The statistic is compared with a cut-off, the critical value, which depends on the df and on the significance level α that we choose before looking at the data.

Reject H0 if χ² is greater than the critical value.

Equivalently, reject H0 when the p-value is at most α. The two methods always agree.

A coin example at two levels

A coin shows 62 heads and 38 tails in 100 tosses. Is it fair?

Step 1: Expected. 50 heads and 50 tails.

Step 2: Statistic. χ² = (62 − 50)²/50 + (38 − 50)²/50 = 144/50 + 144/50 = 5.76.

Step 3: df. 2 categories, nothing estimated, so df = 1.

Step 4: Compare. At 0.05 the cut-off is 3.841, and 5.76 is larger, so reject. At 0.01 the cut-off is 6.635, and 5.76 is smaller, so do not reject. The same data give different answers at different levels, which is why the level must be fixed in advance.

from fractions import Fraction

observed = [62, 38]
expected = [50, 50]
chi2 = sum(Fraction((o - e) ** 2, e) for o, e in zip(observed, expected))
print("Exact statistic:", chi2)
print("As a decimal:", float(chi2))
print("Reject at 0.05?", float(chi2) > 3.841)
print("Reject at 0.01?", float(chi2) > 6.635)
Exact statistic: 144/25
As a decimal: 5.76
Reject at 0.05? True
Reject at 0.01? False

TrapThe p-value is the chance of a result this extreme if H0 is true. It is not the chance that H0 is true. And not rejecting H0 does not prove it.

GATE loves thisA statistic close to the cut-off, such as 3.75 against 3.841, is a common trap. Compare exactly and do not round the statistic up.

A statistic is 9.0 with df = 4. Is it significant at 0.05?

The critical value is 9.488. Since 9.0 is smaller, do not reject at 5 percent.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original, written in GATE style, and are not past exam questions. Try each one before reading the answer.

Is the coin fair?

A coin is tossed 100 times and shows 40 heads and 60 tails. The null hypothesis is that the coin is fair. Using the chi-squared goodness-of-fit test, which of the following statements are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) The statistic is 4, with df = 1.
(B) The null hypothesis is rejected at α = 0.05.
(C) The null hypothesis is rejected at α = 0.01.
(D) The statistic is 10, with df = 2.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true. Each contains a statistic, a df or a decision.
  2. Which idea applies. A fair coin gives 50 heads and 50 tails.
  3. Work it out. χ² = (40 − 50)²/50 + (60 − 50)²/50 = 2 + 2 = 4. df = 2 − 1 = 1. The 0.05 cut-off is 3.841, so reject. The 0.01 cut-off is 6.635, so do not reject.
  4. Check. A and B are true. C is false. D has the wrong statistic and df.

Answer: (A) and (B).

Counting degrees of freedom

A test of independence is run on a table with 3 rows and 4 columns. A separate goodness-of-fit test has 5 categories and no parameter estimated from the data. Give the pair (independence df, goodness-of-fit df).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Two df values as a pair.
  2. Which idea applies. (r − 1)(c − 1) for independence, k − 1 − m for goodness of fit.
  3. Work it out. (3 − 1)(4 − 1) = 6. And 5 − 1 − 0 = 4.
  4. Check. A 3 × 4 table has 12 cells. Fixing 3 row totals and 4 column totals leaves 12 − 3 − 4 + 1 = 6 free cells, which matches.

Answer: (6, 4).

A NAT question on a die

A die is rolled 120 times. The observed counts for faces 1 to 6 are 15, 25, 20, 22, 18 and 20. The null hypothesis is that the die is fair. Find the chi-squared statistic to one decimal place.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The statistic, to one decimal place.
  2. Which idea applies. A fair die gives E = 120/6 = 20 for each face.
  3. Work it out. Squared gaps: 25, 25, 0, 4, 4, 0. Total 58. Divide by 20: 58/20 = 2.9.
  4. Check. The counts add to 120, matching the rolls.

Answer: 2.9. With df = 5 the cut-off is 11.070, so the die is not rejected as unfair.

An expected count in a larger table

A survey of 100 people is cross-classified into a 2 × 3 table. The row totals are 40 and 60. The column totals are 30, 40 and 30. Assuming independence, find the expected count in row 2, column 3.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. One expected count.
  2. Which idea applies. E = (row total × column total)/N.
  3. Work it out. E = (60 × 30)/100 = 18.
  4. Check. The row totals add to 100 and so do the column totals, and 18 is less than the row total 60.

Answer: 18.

Pass rates by gender

In a class of 200 students, 100 are male and 100 are female. Of the males, 60 passed. Of the females, 40 passed. A test of independence between gender and result is run. Which option is correct? (A) statistic 8, df 1, rejected at 0.01; (B) statistic 8, df 2, rejected at 0.01; (C) statistic 4, df 1, not rejected at 0.01; (D) statistic 16, df 1, rejected at 0.01.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The option with the correct statistic, df and decision at α = 0.01.
  2. Which idea applies. Build the 2 × 2 table, then E from the totals.
  3. Work it out. Table: Male Pass 60, Male Fail 40, Female Pass 40, Female Fail 60. Every expected count is (100 × 100)/200 = 50. Terms: (10)²/50 = 2 each, four cells, total 8. df = 1. Cut-off at 0.01 is 6.635, and 8 is larger, so reject.
  4. Check. Four equal terms of 2 give 8, and option A matches.

Answer: (A).

A close call with a 2 × 2 table

A 2 × 2 table records 120 people. Group A has 45 in column 1 and 15 in column 2. Group B has 35 in column 1 and 25 in column 2. Test independence at α = 0.05. Give the statistic, and state the decision.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The statistic and a decision.
  2. Which idea applies. Totals, then E for each cell.
  3. Work it out. Row totals 60 and 60. Column totals 80 and 40. N = 120. Expected: 40, 20, 40, 20. Terms: 25/40 = 0.625, 25/20 = 1.25, 25/40 = 0.625, 25/20 = 1.25. Sum = 3.75. df = 1, cut-off 3.841. Since 3.75 is smaller, do not reject.
  4. Check. Expected counts add to 120. The statistic is close to 3.841 but below it, so the decision is not to reject.

Answer: the statistic is 3.75, and H0 is not rejected at the 5 percent level.

Which statements about the test are correct?

Consider the chi-squared test. Select all statements that are correct.

(A) A small p-value is evidence against the null hypothesis.
(B) If the statistic is small, the null hypothesis has been proved true.
(C) For a fixed df, a larger statistic gives a smaller p-value.
(D) Expected counts can be replaced by observed counts in the formula.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Identify the true claims about meaning and formula.
  2. Which idea applies. The p-value is a tail probability under H0. Not rejecting is not proving. E comes from the model or the totals.
  3. Work it out. A: true. B: false, failing to reject is not proof. C: true, a larger statistic has less tail area beyond it. D: false.
  4. Check. Picture the tail: the further right the statistic, the less area beyond it.

Answer: (A) and (C).

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Chi-squared statistic for a die (NAT, 2 marks)

A die is rolled 60 times. The counts of faces 1 to 6 are 8, 9, 12, 11, 6, 14. Compute the chi-squared goodness of fit statistic for a fair die, correct to 2 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. χ² = Σ (O − E)²/E with E = 10 for each face.
  2. Which idea applies. Under the fair die hypothesis each face is expected 60/6 = 10 times.
  3. Work it out. Squared deviations: 4, 1, 4, 1, 16, 16, total 42. Divide by 10: χ² = 4.20.
  4. Check. The code recomputes the sum.
O = [8, 9, 12, 11, 6, 14]
print("chi2 = %.2f" % sum((o - 10) ** 2 / 10 for o in O))

chi2 = 4.20

Answer: 4.20.

Exam-level 2: Degrees of freedom of a contingency table (NAT, 1 mark)

A test of independence uses a 4 × 3 contingency table. How many degrees of freedom does the chi-squared statistic have?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The degrees of freedom for an r × c table.
  2. Which idea applies. df = (r − 1)(c − 1).
  3. Work it out. (4 − 1)(3 − 1) = 6.
  4. Check. Once the row and column totals are fixed, a 3 × 2 corner of the table determines the rest, which is 6 cells.

Answer: 6.

Exam-level 3: Expected count in a 2 × 2 table (NAT, 2 marks)

In a 2 × 2 table, row totals are 40 and 60, column totals are 30 and 70, grand total 100. Observed count in cell (1, 1) is 18. Find the chi-squared statistic (no continuity correction), correct to 2 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The full χ² for the table.
  2. Which idea applies. Eij = (row i total)(column j total)/N. Fill the other cells from the margins.
  3. Work it out. Observed: [[18, 22], [12, 48]]. Expected: [[12, 28], [18, 42]]. Contributions: 36/12 + 36/28 + 36/18 + 36/42 = 3 + 1.2857 + 2 + 0.8571 = 7.14.
  4. Check. The code fills the table and adds the terms.
O = [[18, 22], [12, 48]]
R = [sum(r) for r in O]; C = [sum(c) for c in zip(*O)]; N = sum(R)
print("chi2 = %.2f" % sum((O[i][j] - R[i] * C[j] / N) ** 2 / (R[i] * C[j] / N) for i in range(2) for j in range(2)))

chi2 = 7.14

Answer: 7.14.

Summary

Chi-squared test
Compares observed counts with expected counts using one summed statistic.
Test statistic
χ² = Σ (O − E)²/E, always zero or positive.
Goodness of fit
Tests whether one categorical variable follows a specified model.
Test of independence
Tests whether two categorical variables are unrelated, using a contingency table.
Expected count under independence
(row total × column total)/N for each cell.
df, goodness of fit
k − 1 − m, with k categories and m estimated parameters.
df, independence
(r − 1)(c − 1), with r rows and c columns.
Significance level (α)
The chosen risk of rejecting H0 when it is true.
Critical value
The cut-off from the table, found from df and α.
Decision rule
Reject H0 when χ² is greater than the critical value.
p-value
The probability, assuming H0, of a statistic at least this large.
Null hypothesis
The belief being tested, such as fairness or independence.
Contingency table
A grid of counts for two categorical variables.
Not rejecting H0
Means the data are not strong enough to reject it. It does not prove H0.

Remember these

  1. The statistic is the sum of (O − E)²/E over every category or cell.
  2. Expected counts come from the model or from (row total × column total)/N, never from the observed counts.
  3. For independence, df = (r − 1)(c − 1). Sample size does not change df.
  4. For goodness of fit, df = k − 1 − m.
  5. Reject H0 when the statistic is larger than the critical value for your df and α.
  6. A p-value is the chance of a result this extreme if H0 is true, not the chance that H0 is true.
  7. Not rejecting H0 does not prove it.

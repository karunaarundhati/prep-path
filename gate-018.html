Differentiability & Taylor series

Differentiability asks whether a function has a clear, single slope at a point. Taylor series rewrite a function as an infinite polynomial, which lets you approximate values and evaluate hard limits. Both ideas appear often in GATE DA and RA.

The idea in plain words

Imagine you are driving along a road drawn on a map. At each point the road has a direction. If you can point your car in one clear direction at a spot, the road is smooth there. If the road has a sharp corner, you cannot say which way to point, because it changes instantly. A function is differentiable at a point when its graph has one clear, sensible slope there.

Now imagine you want to describe a complicated curve near one point, using only simple pieces. A straight line is simple, and it matches the curve's slope at that point. A parabola matches even more. A Taylor series extends this idea. It builds a polynomial that copies the function's value, slope, bend and higher behaviour at one point. The more pieces you add, the better the copy.

Why does this matter for GATE? Differentiability is tested in quick true or false questions. Taylor series are used for approximations, for evaluating limits that look impossible, and for the second-order thinking behind optimisation and robotics. Both ideas rely on the derivative, so keep the previous topic close.

Key terms

Each term gets a plain meaning first, then a precise definition.

Derivative
Plain meaning: the slope of the graph at a point. Precise definition: f'(a) = lim (h approaches 0) of (f(a + h) minus f(a))/h, if this limit exists.
Differentiable at a
Plain meaning: the graph has one clear slope at a. Precise definition: the derivative f'(a) exists as a finite number.
Corner
Plain meaning: a sharp point where the direction changes suddenly. Precise definition: the left derivative and the right derivative both exist, but they are different.
Cusp
Plain meaning: a sharp point like the tip of a pencil. Precise definition: the slope tends to plus or minus infinity from the two sides, with opposite signs.
Vertical tangent
Plain meaning: a point where the graph is vertical. Precise definition: the slope tends to plus or minus infinity from both sides in the same direction.
Higher-order derivative
Plain meaning: the derivative of a derivative. Precise definition: f''(x) is the derivative of f'(x), and f'''(x) is the derivative of f''(x).
Factorial
Plain meaning: a product of counting numbers. Precise definition: n! = 1 times 2 times ... times n, with 0! = 1.
Taylor series
Plain meaning: a polynomial that copies a function near a point. Precise definition: the sum of f^(n)(a) times (x - a)^n divided by n!, over all n from 0 onwards.
Maclaurin series
Plain meaning: a Taylor series centred at 0. Precise definition: the Taylor series of f with a = 0.
Remainder
Plain meaning: the error left after using a finite number of Taylor terms. Precise definition: R_n(x) = f(x) minus the Taylor polynomial of degree n.

TipThe symbol f^(n) means the n-th derivative of f. So f^(0) is f itself, f^(1) is f', and f^(2) is f''.

Differentiability and its definition

To find the slope at a point a, we look at a tiny step h away from a. The rise of the graph over that step is f(a + h) minus f(a). Dividing the rise by the step gives the average slope over the step. Then we shrink the step towards zero. If the average slopes settle to one number, that number is the derivative.

f'(a) = lim (h approaches 0) of (f(a + h) minus f(a))/h

Each symbol means:

  • h is a small step away from a. It can be positive or negative.
  • f(a + h) minus f(a) is the change in height.
  • divided by h converts the change in height into a slope.

Worked example. Find f'(1) for f(x) = x squared.

  1. Step 1. Compute f(1 + h) = (1 + h) squared = 1 + 2h + h squared.
  2. Step 2. Compute f(1 + h) minus f(1) = (1 + 2h + h squared) minus 1 = 2h + h squared.
  3. Step 3. Divide by h: (2h + h squared)/h = 2 + h, for h not equal to 0.
  4. Step 4. Let h go to 0: 2 + h becomes 2.
  5. Answer. f'(1) = 2.

Key ideaDifferentiable at a means the limit above exists, and it must give the same value whether h comes from the left or from the right.

Quick check: what is f'(2) for f(x) = 3x squared?

The quotient is (3(2 + h) squared minus 12)/h = (12h + 3h squared)/h = 12 + 3h, which tends to 12. So f'(2) = 12.

Quick check: does a derivative need the limit to be the same from both sides?

Yes. The two-sided limit exists only when the left and right limits of the difference quotient are equal. If they differ, the function has a corner there and is not differentiable.

When the derivative fails

A derivative fails to exist in four standard situations. Learn to recognise each picture.

1. Corner. The graph bends sharply, like a folded piece of paper. The left slope and the right slope both exist, but they are different. The standard example is f(x) = |x| at 0. The left slope is minus 1 and the right slope is 1.

2. Cusp. The graph comes into a point and goes back out sharply, like the tip of a pencil. The slope tends to plus infinity on one side and minus infinity on the other. The standard example is f(x) = x to the power 2/3 at 0.

3. Vertical tangent. The graph is locally vertical, and the slope is infinite in the same direction from both sides. The standard example is f(x) = cube root of x at 0, where the slope tends to plus infinity from both sides.

4. Discontinuity. If the graph has a break at a, the derivative cannot exist there. This follows from the main theorem of the previous topic: differentiable implies continuous.

TrapA continuous function can still have no derivative at a corner, a cusp or a vertical tangent. But a function with a break can never be differentiable at that break.

Here is a numerical check of the cube root example. Its difference quotient grows without limit as h shrinks.

def cube_root(x):
    if x >= 0:
        return x ** (1 / 3)
    return -((-x) ** (1 / 3))

for h in [0.1, 0.01, 0.001]:
    quotient = (cube_root(0 + h) - cube_root(0)) / h
    print("h =", h, "quotient =", round(quotient, 2))
h = 0.1 quotient = 4.64
h = 0.01 quotient = 21.54
h = 0.001 quotient = 100.0

The quotient keeps growing, so there is no finite slope at 0. This is a vertical tangent.

Quick check: is f(x) = x squared differentiable at 0, given it is a smooth bowl with no corner?

Yes. The quotient (h squared)/h = h tends to 0, so f'(0) = 0.

Quick check: a function has a jump at a. Can it be differentiable at a?

No. A jump is a break, and a function with a break cannot be differentiable there.

Higher-order derivatives

The derivative tells you how the function changes. The derivative of the derivative tells you how that rate of change changes. This is the second derivative, and it describes the bend of the graph. Think of a car: position is f, speed is f', and acceleration is f''.

f'(x) = first derivative, f''(x) = second derivative, f'''(x) = third derivative

Worked example. Find f''(x) for f(x) = x to the power 4 minus 2x squared.

  1. Step 1. Differentiate once: f'(x) = 4x cubed minus 4x. The power 4 becomes 4 times x cubed, and the term 2x squared becomes 4x.
  2. Step 2. Differentiate again: f''(x) = 12x squared minus 4. The term 4x cubed becomes 12x squared, and 4x becomes 4.
  3. Step 3. Evaluate at x = 1: f''(1) = 12 times 1 minus 4 = 8.

GATE loves thisSecond derivatives decide whether a stationary point is a maximum or a minimum. Positive second derivative means a bowl, and negative means a hill. This is the link to optimisation later in this unit.

Quick check: what is f'''(x) for f(x) = x to the power 4 minus 2x squared?

From f''(x) = 12x squared minus 4, differentiate: f'''(x) = 24x. The constant term disappears.

Taylor and Maclaurin series

Suppose you know a function's height, slope, bend and so on at one point a. You can build a polynomial that matches all of them. That polynomial is the Taylor series. Think of a tailor who measures you at one spot and cuts a suit that fits that spot well. The more measurements, the better the fit.

f(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a) + f''(a)(x - a) squared / 2! + f'''(a)(x - a) cubed / 3! + ...

Each symbol means:

  • a is the centre of the expansion, the point where we match the function.
  • f^(n)(a) is the n-th derivative evaluated at a.
  • (x - a) to the power n is a power of the distance from a.
  • n! is the factorial, which cancels the extra factors from repeated differentiation.

When a = 0 we get the Maclaurin series. The four standard expansions to memorise are:

e^x = 1 + x + x squared/2! + x cubed/3! + ...
sin(x) = x - x cubed/3! + x to the power 5/5! - ...
cos(x) = 1 - x squared/2! + x to the power 4/4! - ...
ln(1 + x) = x - x squared/2 + x cubed/3 - ..., valid for -1 less than x less than or equal to 1

Worked example. Find the Taylor polynomial of degree 2 for f(x) = e^x at a = 0.

  1. Step 1. Compute f(0) = e^0 = 1.
  2. Step 2. Compute f'(0) = e^0 = 1, because the derivative of e^x is e^x.
  3. Step 3. Compute f''(0) = 1, for the same reason.
  4. Step 4. Build the polynomial: 1 + 1 times x + (1 times x squared)/2! = 1 + x + x squared/2.

The next program adds terms of e^x one at a time, to see how the polynomial approaches the true value at x = 0.5.

import math

x = 0.5
total = 0.0
for n in range(6):
    total += x ** n / math.factorial(n)
    print("terms up to x^" + str(n) + ":", f"{total:.5f}")
print("exact e^0.5:", f"{math.exp(x):.5f}")
terms up to x^0: 1.00000
terms up to x^1: 1.50000
terms up to x^2: 1.62500
terms up to x^3: 1.64583
terms up to x^4: 1.64844
terms up to x^5: 1.64870
exact e^0.5: 1.64872

Look at the output. After only four terms the answer is already correct to about three decimal places. Each new term is smaller than the one before, because x to the power n divided by n! shrinks fast when x is small.

Maclaurin series of cos(x). From the formula above, the x squared coefficient of cos(x) is minus 1/2. This is a quick warm-up question type, and you can verify it by differentiating cos(x) twice at 0: cos(0) = 1, and the second derivative of cos(x) is minus cos(x), which equals minus 1 at 0. The x squared term is then (minus 1) times x squared divided by 2!, which is minus x squared over 2.

Quick check: what is the Taylor polynomial of degree 1 for f(x) = e^x at a = 0?

It is 1 + x. The constant is f(0) = 1, and the linear term is f'(0) times x = 1 times x.

Quick check: why does the Maclaurin series of sin(x) have no even powers?

Because sin(x) is an odd function, and each derivative at 0 is zero for even orders. The derivatives cycle through sin, cos, minus sin, minus cos, so the even-order derivatives at 0 are all zero.

Using Taylor series for approximation and limits

Approximation. If x is small, the first few Taylor terms are very close to the true value. Suppose you need e^0.1 but have no calculator. Use 1 + x with x = 0.1, giving 1.1. The true value is about 1.10517. The error is about 0.005, which is small compared with the answer.

Limits. Many limits that look impossible become easy once you replace each function by its Taylor polynomial near 0. The idea is simple. Write each function as its leading terms plus small corrections, cancel the powers that match, and read off what is left.

Example. Find lim (x approaches 0) of (e^x minus 1 minus x)/x squared.

  1. Step 1. Substitution gives 0/0, so a simple substitution is not enough.
  2. Step 2. Use the series e^x = 1 + x + x squared/2 + x cubed/6 + ... Subtract 1 and x: e^x minus 1 minus x = x squared/2 + x cubed/6 + ...
  3. Step 3. Divide by x squared: 1/2 + x/6 + ...
  4. Step 4. As x goes to 0, every term with x in it disappears. The limit is 1/2.
import math

for x in [0.1, 0.01, 0.001]:
    value = (math.exp(x) - 1 - x) / (x * x)
    print("x =", x, "value =", f"{value:.4f}")
print("answer from Taylor: 1/2 =", 1/2)
x = 0.1 value = 0.5171
x = 0.01 value = 0.5017
x = 0.001 value = 0.5002
answer from Taylor: 1/2 = 0.5

TrapOnly use as many terms as you need. If the leading term in the numerator is x squared, you need the expansion up to x squared, and the x cubed terms do not change the limit. But if the leading terms cancel, you must go further, as in the next example.

Example with cancellation. Find lim (x approaches 0) of (sin(x) minus x)/x cubed.

  1. Step 1. Use sin(x) = x minus x cubed/6 + x to the power 5/120 - ...
  2. Step 2. Subtract x: sin(x) minus x = minus x cubed/6 + x to the power 5/120 - ...
  3. Step 3. Divide by x cubed: minus 1/6 + x squared/120 - ...
  4. Step 4. Let x go to 0: the limit is minus 1/6.

GATE loves thisExpansions are often faster than L'Hopital's rule, which may need three or four rounds. Choose the method that gives the answer with the least work.

Quick check: what is lim (x approaches 0) of sin(x)/x using Taylor series?

sin(x)/x = (x minus x cubed/6 + ...)/x = 1 minus x squared/6 + ... As x goes to 0, the limit is 1.

Quick check: what does the quadratic Taylor polynomial add to the linear one for e^x near 0?

It adds the curvature term x squared divided by 2. This term matters when x is not tiny, and it makes the approximation more accurate.

The remainder term

An approximation is only useful if you know how wrong it can be. The difference between the true function and its Taylor polynomial of degree n is the remainder, R_n(x). A standard form for the remainder is the Lagrange form:

R_n(x) = f^(n+1)(c) times (x - a) to the power (n+1) divided by (n+1)!, for some c between a and x

Each symbol means:

  • f^(n+1)(c) is the next derivative, evaluated at an unknown point c between a and x.
  • (x - a) to the power (n+1) shrinks quickly when x is close to a.
  • (n+1)! makes the error smaller again as n grows.

In practice we use the bound |R_n(x)| at most M times |x - a| to the power (n+1) divided by (n+1)!, where M is the largest size of the next derivative on the interval. This gives a guaranteed error bound. The next program applies this to sin(0.1), using the terms x minus x cubed/6.

import math

x = 0.1
approx = x - x ** 3 / math.factorial(3)
actual = math.sin(x)
bound = x ** 5 / math.factorial(5)
print("approximation:", f"{approx:.7f}")
print("math.sin(0.1):", f"{actual:.7f}")
print("actual error:", f"{abs(actual - approx):.2e}")
print("remainder bound:", f"{bound:.2e}")
approximation: 0.0998333
math.sin(0.1): 0.0998334
actual error: 8.33e-08
remainder bound: 8.33e-08

Key ideaThe remainder bound guarantees the error. For sin(0.1) the true error is 8.33e-08, and the bound is 8.33e-08, so the approximation is safe.

Quick check: for e^x with the terms up to x squared/2, what is the remainder's power of x?

The remainder starts with x cubed, because the next term after x squared/2 is x cubed/6. So R_2(x) has size about x cubed, and it shrinks fast when x is small.

Quick check: does a bigger n always give a smaller remainder?

For a fixed x close to a, usually yes, because (x - a) to the power (n+1) over (n+1)! shrinks fast. The bound for M also matters, so always check the size of the next derivative.

Coefficient in a Maclaurin series

Find the coefficient of x squared in the Maclaurin series of cos(x). Give your answer as a decimal number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The number multiplying x squared.
  2. Which idea applies. The series cos(x) = 1 minus x squared/2! + x to the power 4/4! - ...
  3. Work it out. The x squared term is minus x squared divided by 2! = minus x squared divided by 2. The coefficient is minus 1/2 = minus 0.5.
  4. Check. The second derivative of cos(x) at 0 is minus cos(0) = minus 1. Divide by 2! = 2 to get minus 1/2.

Answer: -0.5

Spot the corner

Which of the following functions has a corner at x = 0? (A) x squared (B) |x| (C) cube root of x (D) sin(x).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The function with a sharp bend at 0.
  2. Which idea applies. A corner means the left and right slopes exist but differ.
  3. Work it out. x squared is smooth at 0, with slope 0. The cube root has a vertical tangent, not a corner. sin(x) is smooth with slope 1. For |x|, the left slope is minus 1 and the right slope is 1, so there is a corner.
  4. Check. Only |x| has the two different one-sided slopes.

Answer: (B)

Differentiability, select all that apply

Select all that apply: (A) the cube root of x has a vertical tangent at x = 0; (B) |x| has a corner at x = 0; (C) x to the power 2/3 is differentiable at x = 0; (D) the step function that is 0 for x less than 0 and 1 for x at least 0 is differentiable at x = 0.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true.
  2. Which idea applies. Recognise each picture: corner, cusp, vertical tangent, or break.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true: the slope of the cube root tends to plus infinity from both sides. (B) is true: the left slope is minus 1 and the right slope is 1. Exam-level 1: a smooth join between two pieces (NAT, 2 marks)

A cost curve is built from two pieces joined at x = 1. For x at most 1, the cost is f(x) = x2. For x greater than 1, the cost is f(x) = ax + b, where a and b are constants. The curve must be continuous at x = 1, so there is no sudden jump, and differentiable at x = 1, so there is no sharp corner. Find a2 + b2, correct to 2 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The value of a2 + b2, found from two conditions at x = 1.
  2. Which idea applies. Continuity at 1 means the two pieces have the same value at 1. Differentiability at 1 means the two pieces have the same slope at 1, so the left and right derivatives must match.
  3. Work it out. The left piece has value f(1) = 1 and derivative 2x, so its slope at 1 is 2. The right piece has value a + b at 1 and slope a. Continuity gives a + b = 1. Differentiability gives a = 2. So b = 1 - 2 = -1, and a2 + b2 = 4 + 1 = 5, which is 5.00.
  4. Check. With a = 2 and b = -1, the right piece has value 1 at x = 1, matching the left piece. The Python block verifies the arithmetic with exact fractions.
from fractions import Fraction
left_value, left_slope = Fraction(1), Fraction(2)
a = left_slope
b = left_value - a
print("a =", a, " b =", b)
print("right value at 1 =", a + b, " left value at 1 =", left_value)
print("a^2 + b^2 = %.2f" % float(a*a + b*b))
a = 2  b = -1
right value at 1 = 1  left value at 1 = 1
a^2 + b^2 = 5.00

Answer: 5.00

Exam-level 2: differentiability at a point (MSQ, 2 marks)

Four functions are studied at the point x = 0. In each case the derivative at 0 is found from the difference quotient (f(h) - f(0))/h as h approaches 0. Select all that apply. (A) f(x) = |x| is differentiable at 0. (B) f(x) = x1/3, the real cube root, is differentiable at 0. (C) f(x) = x2/3 is differentiable at 0. (D) f(x) = x2 sin(1/x) for x not equal to 0, with f(0) = 0, is differentiable at 0.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which of the four functions have a finite derivative at 0.
  2. Which idea applies. Compute the difference quotient from the right and from the left. The derivative exists only when both sides approach the same finite number.
  3. Work it out. (A) For h greater than 0 the quotient is |h|/h = 1, and for h less than 0 it is -1. The two sides differ, so there is a corner and (A) is false. (B) The quotient is h1/3/h = h-2/3, which grows without bound as h approaches 0. The tangent is vertical, so there is no finite derivative and (B) is false. (C) The quotient has size |h|-1/3 and opposite signs on the two sides, so it does not settle, and (C) is false. (D) The quotient is h2 sin(1/h)/h = h sin(1/h). Since the sine is at most 1 in size, the quotient is at most |h| in size, so it goes to 0. The derivative is 0, and (D) is true.
  4. Check. The Python block prints the difference quotients at h = 0.001 and h = -0.001. For (A) they are 1 and -1, for (B) and (C) they are large, and for (D) they are close to 0.
import math

def cbrt(t):
    return math.copysign(abs(t) ** (1 / 3), t)

def f_abs(t):
    return abs(t)

def f_cbrt(t):
    return cbrt(t)

def f_23(t):
    return abs(t) ** (2 / 3)

def f_d(t):

Exam-level 3: a third derivative from a product of series (MCQ, 2 marks)

A damped oscillator's displacement near its start is modelled by f(x) = ex sin x, where x is a small time in seconds. Its Maclaurin series, the Taylor series at x = 0, starts with terms in x, x2 and x3. What is the third derivative f'''(0)? (A) 1/3. (B) 2. (C) 6. (D) 1/6. Choose one option.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The third derivative of f at x = 0.
  2. Which idea applies. Write the Maclaurin series of ex and sin x, multiply them, and read off the coefficient c of x3. A coefficient c of x3 means f'''(0) = 3! times c.
  3. Work it out. ex = 1 + x + x2/2 + x3/6 + ... and sin x = x - x3/6 + .... The x3 terms of the product come from (1)(-x3/6), from (x)(0 times x2), from (x2/2)(x), and from (x3/6)(0). These add to -1/6 + 0 + 1/2 + 0 = 1/3. So c = 1/3, and f'''(0) = 3! times 1/3 = 6 times 1/3 = 2. Option (B) is correct. Option (A) is the coefficient itself, not the third derivative, which is a common trap.
  4. Check. The Python block multiplies the two truncated series with exact fractions. The x1 and x2 coefficients are 1, which agree with f'(0) = 1 and f''(0) = 2, and the third derivative comes out as 2.
from fractions import Fraction
from math import factorial
N = 4
exp_c = [Fraction(1, factorial(k)) for k in range(N)]
sin_c = [Fraction(0), Fraction(1), Fraction(0), Fraction(-1, 6)]
prod = [Fraction(0)] * N
for i in range(N):
    for j in range(N):
        if i + j < N:
            prod[i + j] += exp_c[i] * sin_c[j]
for k, c in enumerate(prod):
    print("coefficient of x^%d = %s" % (k, c))
print("f'''(0) = 3! times coefficient of x^3 = %s" % (factorial(3) * prod[3]))
coefficient of x^0 = 0
coefficient of x^1 = 1
coefficient of x^2 = 1
coefficient of x^3 = 1/3
f'''(0) = 3! times coefficient of x^3 = 2

Answer: (B) 2

Exam-level 4: a limit that needs a second-order expansion (NAT, 2 marks)

Two small-angle models are compared using the limit L = lim (cos x - e-x2/2)/x4 as x approaches 0, where x is an angle in radians. At x = 0 both the numerator and the denominator are zero, so direct substitution fails. Find L, correct to 4 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The value of L, to 4 decimal places.
  2. Which idea applies. Expand cos x and the exponential as Taylor series up to the x4 term. The leading term that survives in the numerator decides the limit.
  3. Work it out. cos x = 1 - x2/2 + x4/24 - .... Let u = -x2/2. Then eu = 1 + u + u2/2 + ..., and since u2 = x4/4, this gives e-x2/2 = 1 - x2/2 + x4/8 + .... Subtracting, the x2 terms cancel and the numerator is (1/24 - 1/8)x4 + ... = (1/24 - 3/24)x4 + ... = -x4/12 + .... Dividing by x4 gives -1/12 in the limit, so L = -1/12, about -0.0833.
  4. Check. The Python block evaluates the expression at x = 0.1 and at x = 0.01. The values approach -0.0833 as x gets smaller.
import math
def h(x):
    return (math.cos(x) - math.exp(-x*x/2)) / x**4
for x in [0.1, 0.01]:
    print("x =", x, "value =", round(h(x), 4))
print("-1/12 to 4 dp =", round(-1/12, 4))
x = 0.1 value = -0.0831
x = 0.01 value = -0.0833
-1/12 to 4 dp = -0.0833

Answer: -0.0833

Summary

Derivative
Plain meaning: the slope at a point. Precise definition: the limit of (f(a + h) minus f(a))/h as h goes to 0.
Differentiable at a
Plain meaning: one clear slope at a. Precise definition: the derivative f'(a) exists as a finite number.
Corner
Plain meaning: a sharp bend. Precise definition: left and right derivatives exist but differ.
Cusp
Plain meaning: a pencil tip. Precise definition: slopes tend to infinities of opposite signs.
Vertical tangent
Plain meaning: a vertical graph. Precise definition: slope tends to infinity of the same sign from both sides.
Second derivative
Plain meaning: how the slope changes. Precise definition: the derivative of f'(x), written f''(x).
Factorial
Plain meaning: a product of counting numbers. Precise definition: n! = 1 times 2 times ... times n, with 0! = 1.
Taylor series
Plain meaning: a polynomial copy of a function near a point. Precise definition: the sum of f^(n)(a)(x - a)^n/n!.
Maclaurin series
Plain meaning: a Taylor series at 0. Precise definition: the Taylor series with a = 0.
Remainder
Plain meaning: the leftover error. Precise definition: f(x) minus the Taylor polynomial of degree n.
Lagrange remainder
Plain meaning: an exact form of the error. Precise definition: f^(n+1)(c)(x - a)^(n+1)/(n+1)! for some c between a and x.
Error bound
Plain meaning: a guaranteed limit on the error. Precise definition: a value at least as large as the absolute remainder.

Remember these

  1. Differentiability is a limit of slopes, and it must agree from both sides.
  2. Corners, cusps and vertical tangents are three ways the derivative can fail.
  3. A function with a break is never differentiable at the break.
  4. Taylor series copy a function near a point, using its derivatives at that point.
  5. Memorise the Maclaurin series of e^x, sin(x) and cos(x).
  6. When a limit gives 0/0, expanding in series is often faster than L'Hopital's rule.
  7. The Lagrange remainder gives a guaranteed bound on the error of an approximation.

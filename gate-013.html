Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

An eigenvector is a special direction that a matrix only stretches, never turns. Its stretch factor is the eigenvalue. Eigenvalue questions are among the most frequent numerical questions in GATE DA and RA, so mastering the 2 by 2 and 3 by 3 methods pays off.

The idea in plain words

Take a rubber sheet with a grid drawn on it, and pull it. Most arrows drawn on the sheet change direction as the sheet stretches. But if you pull along one special line, arrows lying on that line stay on the same line. They just get longer or shorter. Those special arrows are the eigenvectors. The stretch factor on each is the eigenvalue.

A matrix is the rule that does the pulling. So for an eigenvector v and its eigenvalue λ, the matrix does the same thing to v as multiplying by a plain number:

Av = λv

Read it in words: "applying A to v gives the same result as scaling v by λ". The word "eigen" comes from German and means "own" or "characteristic". Each matrix has its own special directions.

Why does GATE care? Eigenvalues tell you about stability (do repeated applications blow up?), about how a matrix behaves when raised to a power, and about whether a matrix can be simplified. GATE gives many eigenvalue questions that need only a few lines of working, so the methods on this page are worth practising until they feel automatic.

Key terms

Eigenvector
A non-zero vector v whose direction is kept by the matrix. Precise: Av = λv with v ≠ 0.
Eigenvalue
The stretch factor λ on an eigenvector. Precise: a number λ such that Av = λv for some non-zero v.
Identity matrix
The matrix I with 1s on the diagonal and 0s elsewhere. It leaves every vector unchanged.
Characteristic equation
The equation det(A − λI) = 0. Its solutions are the eigenvalues.
Characteristic polynomial
The expression det(A − λI), a polynomial in λ of degree n for an n × n matrix.
Trace
The sum of the diagonal entries of a square matrix. Precise: trace(A) = a11 + a22 + ... + ann.
Eigenspace
All eigenvectors for one eigenvalue, together with the zero vector. It is the null space of A − λI.
Algebraic multiplicity
How many times λ appears as a root of the characteristic polynomial.
Geometric multiplicity
The number of independent eigenvectors for λ. Precise: the dimension of the eigenspace.
Diagonalizable
A matrix that can be written as A = PDP⁻¹ with D diagonal. Equivalently, it has a full set of independent eigenvectors.

Step 1: Eigenvectors as special directions

Let us test the idea with a concrete matrix. Let A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]]. Try two vectors.

  1. Take v = (1, 1). Compute Av: the first entry is 2·1 + 1·1 = 3, and the second entry is 1·1 + 2·1 = 3. So Av = (3, 3).
  2. Notice that (3, 3) = 3 · (1, 1). The direction did not change, and the vector was stretched by 3. So (1, 1) is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 3.
  3. Take w = (1, −1). Compute Aw: the first entry is 2 − 1 = 1, and the second is 1 − 2 = −1. So Aw = (1, −1) = 1 · w. An eigenvector with eigenvalue 1.
  4. Take u = (1, 0). Compute Au = (2, 1). This is not a multiple of (1, 0), so u is not an eigenvector.

Look at the picture. It shows the matrix bending the grid. Look for the two special directions that stay on their own line. Drag the picture (if your device allows) and notice that the arrows along those two lines only stretch.

Notice that the grid does not simply rotate. The eigenvector lines are the straight lines through the origin that the matrix maps onto themselves, which is exactly what the definition Av = λv says.

TrapThe zero vector is never an eigenvector, even though A·0 = λ·0 holds for every λ. An eigenvector must be non-zero.

Question: is (1, 2) an eigenvector of [[3, 0], [0, 5]]?

No. The product is (3, 10), which is not a multiple of (1, 2). Only the axes (1, 0) and (0, 1) are eigenvectors of this diagonal matrix.

Question: if v is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 3, what is A(2v)?

A(2v) = 2Av = 2 · 3v = 3(2v). So 2v is also an eigenvector with eigenvalue 3. Any non-zero multiple of an eigenvector is an eigenvector.

Step 2: Finding eigenvalues with the characteristic equation

Trying random vectors does not scale. We need a systematic method. Rewrite Av = λv as (A − λI)v = 0, where I is the identity matrix. This says that the matrix A − λI sends a non-zero vector v to zero. A matrix that does this must be singular, which means its determinant is zero. So:

det(A − λI) = 0 (the characteristic equation)

The expression det(A − λI) is the characteristic polynomial. Its roots are the eigenvalues. Here is the method:

  1. Subtract λ from each diagonal entry to form A − λI.
  2. Compute the determinant, which is a polynomial in λ.
  3. Set it to zero and solve for λ.

Worked example (2 × 2). Let A = [[4, 1], [2, 3]].

  1. A − λI = [[4 − λ, 1], [2, 3 − λ]].
  2. det = (4 − λ)(3 − λ) − 1·2 = (12 − 4λ − 3λ + λ²) − 2 = λ² − 7λ + 10.
  3. Set λ² − 7λ + 10 = 0. Factor: (λ − 5)(λ − 2) = 0.
  4. So λ = 5 or λ = 2.

There is a faster route for 2 × 2 matrices. The characteristic polynomial is always λ² − (trace)λ + det. For A above, the trace is 7 and the determinant is 4·3 − 1·2 = 10. That gives λ² − 7λ + 10 directly, with no expansion.

2 × 2: λ² − trace(A)·λ + det(A) = 0

Now find the eigenvectors. For λ = 5, solve (A − 5I)v = 0, where A − 5I = [[−1, 1], [2, −2]]. The first row says −v1 + v2 = 0, so v2 = v1. Take v = (1, 1). For λ = 2, A − 2I = [[2, 1], [2, 1]], and the first row says 2v1 + v2 = 0. Take v = (1, −2).

Check: A(1, −2) = (4 − 2, 2 − 6) = (2, −4) = 2(1, −2). Correct.

The next program computes the eigenvalues with the formula and checks the eigenvector equation.

import math

def eig2(a, b, c, d):
    tr = a + d
    det = a * d - b * c
    disc = tr * tr - 4 * det
    r = math.sqrt(disc)
    return (tr + r) / 2, (tr - r) / 2

l1, l2 = eig2(4, 1, 2, 3)
print("eigenvalues:", round(l1, 6), round(l2, 6))

A = [[4, 1], [2, 3]]
for lam, v in [(5, (1, 1)), (2, (1, -2))]:
    Av = (A[0][0] * v[0] + A[0][1] * v[1], A[1][0] * v[0] + A[1][1] * v[1])
    print("Av =", Av, "and lambda*v =", (lam * v[0], lam * v[1]))
eigenvalues: 5.0 2.0
Av = (5, 5) and lambda*v = (5, 5)
Av = (2, -4) and lambda*v = (2, -4)

Key ideaEigenvalues are roots of det(A − λI) = 0. For a 2 × 2 matrix, use the trace and determinant shortcut. For larger matrices, expand the determinant carefully.

Question: find the eigenvalues of [[1, 2], [2, 1]].

Trace 2, determinant 1 − 4 = −3. So λ² − 2λ − 3 = 0, which factors as (λ − 3)(λ + 1) = 0. The eigenvalues are 3 and −1.

Question: why is the characteristic equation det(A − λI) = 0 and not det(A) = λ?

Because we need (A − λI)v = 0 for some non-zero v. A matrix sends a non-zero vector to zero only if it is singular, so its determinant must vanish. The equation det(A) = λ would be a different and wrong statement.

Step 3: Properties that save work

Some facts let you check answers quickly. For any n × n matrix, with eigenvalues λ1, ..., λn counted with multiplicity:

λ1 + λ2 + ... + λn = trace(A) λ1 · λ2 · ... · λn = det(A)

Check with A = [[4, 1], [2, 3]]: the eigenvalues 5 and 2 add to 7 = trace, and multiply to 10 = det. Both checks pass.

Other useful facts:

  • Triangular matrices. For an upper or lower triangular matrix (zeros on one side of the diagonal), the eigenvalues are simply the diagonal entries. No calculation is needed.
  • Powers. If Av = λv, then Akv = λkv.
  • Inverse. If A is invertible and Av = λv, then A⁻¹v = (1/λ)v. So the eigenvalues of A⁻¹ are 1/λ.
  • Shift. The eigenvalues of A + cI are λ + c. The same eigenvectors work.
  • Real matrices. A real matrix with a complex eigenvalue a + bi also has the eigenvalue a − bi. Complex eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs.
  • Symmetric matrices. A symmetric real matrix has only real eigenvalues.

GATE loves thisMany GATE questions give the eigenvalues and ask for the determinant, the trace or the eigenvalues of a related matrix. Use the product and sum rules and the power and inverse rules, and you may not need to compute the matrix at all.

Worked example. A 3 × 3 matrix has eigenvalues 1, 2 and 4. Find det(A) and the eigenvalues of A⁻¹.

  1. det(A) = 1 · 2 · 4 = 8.
  2. The eigenvalues of A⁻¹ are 1/1, 1/2 and 1/4, which are 1, 1/2 and 1/4.
  3. Check: det(A⁻¹) = 1/8, which is 1/det(A). Consistent.
Question: what is the trace of a matrix whose eigenvalues are 2, −1 and 5?

2 + (−1) + 5 = 6. The trace is the sum of the eigenvalues, which is also the sum of the diagonal entries.

Question: the upper triangular matrix [[3, 7], [0, 5]] has which eigenvalues?

3 and 5. These are the diagonal entries, and the 7 does not affect the eigenvalues.

Step 4: Multiplicities and diagonalization

Eigenvalues can repeat. Suppose the characteristic polynomial is (λ − 2)²(λ − 5). Then the eigenvalue 2 has algebraic multiplicity 2, because it is a repeated root. The eigenvalue 5 has algebraic multiplicity 1.

The geometric multiplicity is different. It counts the independent eigenvectors for that eigenvalue, which is the dimension of the null space of A − λI. A basic fact is:

1 ≤ geometric multiplicity ≤ algebraic multiplicity

Here is why diagonalization matters. If a matrix has n independent eigenvectors, we can place them as the columns of a matrix P, and then:

A = P D P⁻¹, where D is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues

This is called diagonalization. It works exactly when the geometric multiplicity equals the algebraic multiplicity for every eigenvalue.

Worked example (diagonalizable). Let A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]]. From Step 1, the eigenvectors are (1, 1) with eigenvalue 3, and (1, −1) with eigenvalue 1. Put them as columns:

P = [ 1   1 ]     D = [ 3  0 ]     P⁻¹ = (1/2) [ 1   1 ]
    [ 1  -1 ]         [ 0  1 ]                   [ 1  -1 ]

The inverse of P follows the 2 × 2 rule: for [[a, b], [c, d]], the inverse is (1/det)[[d, −b], [−c, a]], where det P = −1 − 1 = −2. The code below checks that PDP⁻¹ = A.

Worked example (not diagonalizable). Let B = [[2, 1], [0, 2]]. Its characteristic polynomial is (λ − 2)², so the eigenvalue 2 has algebraic multiplicity 2. But A − 2I = [[0, 1], [0, 0]] has only one independent null vector, (1, 0). So the geometric multiplicity is 1, which is less than 2. B cannot be diagonalized.

Look at the picture for B. The only straight line that stays on itself is the horizontal axis. A second independent direction does not exist, which is why the matrix cannot be diagonalized. Try to find a second line.

from fractions import Fraction as F

def matmul(A, B):
    return [[sum(A[i][k] * B[k][j] for k in range(len(B))) for j in range(len(B[0]))] for i in range(len(A))]

P = [[1, 1], [1, -1]]
D = [[3, 0], [0, 1]]
Pinv = [[F(1, 2), F(1, 2)], [F(1, 2), F(-1, 2)]]
A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]]
PDPinv = matmul(matmul(P, D), Pinv)
print("P D P^-1 =", [[str(x) for x in row] for row in PDPinv])
print("equals A:", [[str(x) for x in row] for row in PDPinv] == [[str(x) for x in row] for row in A])
P D P^-1 = [['2', '1'], ['1', '2']]
equals A: True

TrapA matrix with distinct eigenvalues is always diagonalizable. A repeated eigenvalue does not automatically break diagonalization, but it can. Always compare the geometric and algebraic multiplicities.

Question: a 3 × 3 matrix has eigenvalue 4 with algebraic multiplicity 2 and geometric multiplicity 1. Is it diagonalizable?

No. Diagonalizing a 3 × 3 matrix needs 3 independent eigenvectors. The eigenvalue 4 needs 2 of them (its algebraic multiplicity) but gives only 1, so the total falls short.

Step 5: Powers of a matrix

Why do we care about diagonalization? Because it makes powers easy. Multiplying a matrix by itself many times is slow. But if A = PDP⁻¹, then:

Ak = (PDP⁻¹)(PDP⁻¹)...(PDP⁻¹) = P Dk P⁻¹

The middle P⁻¹P pairs cancel, which leaves only Dk. And Dk is easy, because you raise each diagonal entry to the power k. This is why eigenvalues describe long-term behaviour: a repeated process is controlled by λk.

Worked example. For A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]], the eigenvalues are 3 and 1. So Ak has eigenvalues 3k and 1k = 1. As k grows, the component along (1, 1) grows like 3k, while the component along (1, −1) stays put. So Ak grows along the direction (1, 1).

Key ideaFor a matrix with eigenvalues of absolute value less than 1, the powers Ak shrink to zero. If some eigenvalue has absolute value above 1, the powers grow. This is why eigenvalues decide stability in iterative systems.

Question: if A has eigenvalues 2 and 3, what are the eigenvalues of A2?

4 and 9. Each eigenvalue is squared, and the eigenvectors stay the same.

Question: does every 2 × 2 matrix with eigenvalues 1 and 1 equal the identity?

No. The matrix [[1, 1], [0, 1]] has both eigenvalues equal to 1, but it is not the identity. It is not diagonalizable, which is why repeated eigenvalues need the multiplicity check.

Solved GATE-style problems

Try each problem before reading the answer. Problems marked "verified PYQ" are real past GATE questions. The rest are GATE-style practice problems.

Is it an eigenvector?

Let A = [[3, 0], [0, 5]]. Is v = (1, 0) an eigenvector of A? If so, what is its eigenvalue?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Whether Av = λv for this v, and the value of λ.
  2. Which idea applies. Compute Av and check whether it is a multiple of v.
  3. Work it out. Av = (3·1 + 0·0, 0·1 + 5·0) = (3, 0) = 3 · (1, 0).
  4. Check. The result is a scalar multiple of v with λ = 3, so v is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 3.

Answer: yes, with eigenvalue 3.

Eigenvalues of a symmetric 2 × 2

Find both eigenvalues of A = [[1, 2], [2, 1]].

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The two eigenvalues.
  2. Which idea applies. The 2 × 2 shortcut λ² − trace·λ + det = 0.
  3. Work it out. trace = 2 and det = 1 − 4 = −3. So λ² − 2λ − 3 = 0, which factors as (λ − 3)(λ + 1) = 0.
  4. Check. The sum 3 + (−1) = 2 is the trace, and the product 3 · (−1) = −3 is the determinant.

Answer: 3 and −1.

Which are eigenvalues? (MSQ)

Let A = [[4, 1], [2, 3]]. Select all that are eigenvalues of A.

(A) 2   (B) 5   (C) 3   (D) −5

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which of the four numbers are roots of det(A − λI) = 0.
  2. Which idea applies. From the worked example, the characteristic polynomial is λ² − 7λ + 10.
  3. Work it out. Test each option: at λ = 2, 4 − 14 + 10 = 0 (yes). At λ = 5, 25 − 35 + 10 = 0 (yes). At λ = 3, 9 − 21 + 10 = −2 (no). At λ = −5, 25 + 35 + 10 = 70 (no).
  4. Check. The roots are exactly 2 and 5, which add to the trace 7 and multiply to the determinant 10.

Answer: (A) and (B).

Find a and b from the eigenvalues (NAT, verified PYQ)

GATE CSE 2015 Set 1 (verified PYQ). Consider the 2 × 2 matrix A = [[1, 4], [b, a]], where a and b are unknown. The eigenvalues of A are −1 and 7. What are a and b? Give a first, then b.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The two unknown entries.
  2. Which idea applies. The sum of eigenvalues is the trace, and the product is the determinant.
  3. Work it out. Trace: 1 + a = −1 + 7 = 6, so a = 5. Determinant: a − 4b = (−1)(7) = −7. So 5 − 4b = −7, which gives 4b = 12 and b = 3.
  4. Check. With a = 5 and b = 3, the matrix is [[1, 4], [3, 5]], with trace 6 and determinant 5 − 12 = −7. Both match.

Answer: a = 5 and b = 3.

Determinant from complex eigenvalues (NAT, verified PYQ)

GATE CSE 2016 Set 1 · Q05 (verified PYQ). Two eigenvalues of a 3 × 3 real matrix P are 2 + i and 3, where i is the imaginary unit. What is det(P)?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The determinant, from partial information about the eigenvalues.
  2. Which idea applies. P is real, so complex eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs. The third eigenvalue must be 2 − i. The determinant is the product of all eigenvalues.
  3. Work it out. det(P) = (2 + i)(2 − i)(3) = (4 − i²)(3) = (4 + 1)(3) = 15.
  4. Check. The product (2 + i)(2 − i) = 5 is always real, so the determinant of a real matrix is real. Consistent.

Answer: 15.

Determinant of a transposed inverse (NAT, verified PYQ)

GATE CSE 2016 Set 2 · Q06 (verified PYQ). The eigenvalues of a matrix A are 1, 2 and 4. What is det((A⁻¹)ᵀ)? Give the answer as a decimal.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The determinant of the transpose of the inverse.
  2. Which idea applies. The determinant is the product of the eigenvalues. Transposing does not change the determinant.
  3. Work it out. det(A) = 1 · 2 · 4 = 8. Transposing does not change the determinant, so det((A⁻¹)ᵀ) = det(A⁻¹). And det(A⁻¹) = 1/det(A) = 1/8 = 0.125.
  4. Check. Using the eigenvalue rule directly: the eigenvalues of A⁻¹ are 1, 1/2 and 1/4, whose product is 1/8. Same answer.

Answer: 0.125.

Largest eigenvalue of a rank-one matrix (NAT, verified PYQ)

GATE CSE 2018 · Q17 (verified PYQ). Consider the matrix A = uvᵀ, where u = [1, 2]ᵀ and v = [1, 1]ᵀ. What is the largest eigenvalue of A?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The largest eigenvalue of A.
  2. Which idea applies. Compute A directly, then use trace and determinant.
  3. Work it out. uvᵀ = [[1·1, 1·1], [2·1, 2·1]] = [[1, 1], [2, 2]]. The trace is 3 and the determinant is 2 − 2 = 0. So λ² − 3λ = 0, which gives λ = 0 or λ = 3.
  4. Check. The eigenvector for 3 is u itself: A(1, 2) = (1 + 2, 2 + 4) = (3, 6) = 3(1, 2). A fast alternative for a rank-one matrix uvᵀ is that the non-zero eigenvalue is vᵀu = 1 + 2 = 3.

Answer: 3.

Repeated eigenvalues and diagonalizability (MSQ, verified PYQ)

GATE CSE 2018 · Q26 (verified PYQ). A matrix P has the property that its only eigenvectors are the multiples of [1, 4]ᵀ. Which of the following statements are necessarily true?

(I) P does not have an inverse.   (II) P has a repeated eigenvalue.   (III) P cannot be diagonalized.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which of the three statements must hold for every such P.
  2. Which idea applies. P is 2 × 2 but has only one independent eigenvector. Diagonalizing a 2 × 2 matrix needs two independent eigenvectors.
  3. Work it out. (III) is true, because there are not enough eigenvectors. (II) is true: two distinct eigenvalues would each give an eigenvector, so the eigenvalue must be repeated. (I) is not necessary: invertibility depends only on whether 0 is an eigenvalue. The example P = [[5, −1], [16, −3]] has trace 2 and determinant 1. Its characteristic polynomial is λ² − 2λ + 1 = (λ − 1)², so λ = 1 is repeated. Solving (P − I)v = 0 gives the multiples of [1, 4]ᵀ. Yet det(P) = 1 ≠ 0, so P is invertible.
  4. Check. The example shows that a matrix with this eigenvector structure can be invertible, so (I) cannot be concluded.

Answer: (II) and (III) only.

Largest eigenvalue of J minus I (NAT, verified PYQ)

GATE CSE 2021 Set 1 · Q52 (verified PYQ). Let A be the 4 × 4 matrix with 0 on the diagonal and 1 everywhere else. What is the largest eigenvalue of A?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The largest eigenvalue of A.
  2. Which idea applies. Write A = J − I, where J is the all-ones matrix. Find the eigenvalues of J, then shift them.
  3. Work it out. J has rank 1 and trace 4, so its eigenvalues are 4, 0, 0, 0. Subtracting I shifts every eigenvalue by −1, so the eigenvalues of A are 3, −1, −1, −1. The largest is 3.
  4. Check. The trace of A is 0, and 3 + (−1) + (−1) + (−1) = 0. Consistent.

Answer: 3.

Counting independent eigenvectors (NAT)

Let A = [[2, 1], [0, 2]]. How many linearly independent eigenvectors does A have? Give the answer as an integer.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The number of independent eigenvectors, which is the geometric multiplicity summed over eigenvalues.
  2. Which idea applies. Find the eigenvalue, then find the null space of A − λI.
  3. Work it out. The characteristic polynomial is (λ − 2)², so λ = 2 is the only eigenvalue. A − 2I = [[0, 1], [0, 0]]. Its null space contains the vectors (x, 0), since the only equation is v2 = 0. So the eigenspace is spanned by (1, 0), a single independent eigenvector.
  4. Check. One eigenvector is less than the size 2, so A is not diagonalizable. This agrees with the example in Step 4.

Answer: 1.

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Eigenvalues of a polynomial in A (NAT, 2 marks)

A 3 × 3 matrix A has eigenvalues 1, 2 and −1. Find det(A² + 2A + I).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The determinant of a polynomial in A.
  2. Which idea applies. If λ is an eigenvalue of A, then p(λ) is an eigenvalue of p(A). The determinant is the product of eigenvalues.
  3. Work it out. p(λ) = (λ + 1)². p(1) = 4, p(2) = 9, p(−1) = 0. det = 4 · 9 · 0 = 0.
  4. Check. A has eigenvalue −1, so A + I is singular, and so is its square.

Answer: 0.

Exam-level 2: Eigenvalues of a rank-one matrix (NAT, 2 marks)

Let u = (1, 2, 3) and A = uuᵀ + 2I. Find the largest eigenvalue of A.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The top eigenvalue of a rank-one update of a multiple of I.
  2. Which idea applies. uuᵀ has eigenvalue ‖u‖² (eigenvector u) and 0 with multiplicity 2. Adding 2I shifts all eigenvalues by 2.
  3. Work it out. ‖u‖² = 14. Eigenvalues of A: 16, 2, 2. Largest = 16.
  4. Check. tr(A) = 14 + 6 = 20 = 16 + 2 + 2. The code runs power iteration.
u = [1, 2, 3]
A = [[u[i] * u[j] + 2 * (i == j) for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]
x = [1.0, 0.0, 0.0]
for _ in range(50):
    y = [sum(A[i][j] * x[j] for j in range(3)) for i in range(3)]
    lam = max(abs(v) for v in y)
    x = [v / lam for v in y]
print("largest eigenvalue = %.4f" % lam)

largest eigenvalue = 16.0000

Answer: 16.

Exam-level 3: Eigen facts (MSQ, 2 marks)

Let A be a real n × n matrix. Which are TRUE? (A) A and Aᵀ have the same eigenvalues. (B) Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent. (C) If A is symmetric, all eigenvalues are real. (D) If A is invertible, A and A−1 have the same eigenvalues.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which standard eigenvalue facts hold.
  2. Which idea applies. det(Aᵀ − λI) = det(A − λI). Ax = λx gives A−1x = (1/λ)x.
  3. Work it out. (A) Same characteristic polynomial. True. (B) A classic theorem. True. (C) Spectral theorem. True. (D) A−1 has eigenvalues 1/λ, which differ in general. False.
  4. Check. A = diag(2, 3) has inverse with eigenvalues 1/2 and 1/3.

Answer: (A), (B) and (C).

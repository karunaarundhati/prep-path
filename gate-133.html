Resistive, capacitive, inductive sensors

A sensor turns a physical quantity such as strain, distance or position into an electrical signal that a robot can read. Three of the most common sensor principles are based on resistance, capacitance and inductance. This topic explains how each one works, how to calculate its output, and how to choose the right sensor for a job.

The idea in plain words

A robot cannot feel a bend in its arm or the distance to a wall directly. It needs a sensor, a device that notices a physical change and reports it as an electrical signal. Think of a bathroom scale. Pressing on it squashes a spring, and a pointer moves. The pointer is a mechanical signal. A digital scale turns the same press into an electrical signal that a chip can read. The sensor is the part that makes that change.

A transducer is the more general word. It converts energy from one form into another, for example from mechanical movement into a voltage. Many people use "sensor" and "transducer" to mean the same thing. In GATE questions the two words usually refer to the same kind of device, so you do not need to worry about the difference.

Most sensors in a robot depend on one of three simple electrical properties: resistance, capacitance or inductance. Each property changes when a physical quantity changes. This topic shows how each one works, and how to calculate the output. These are the kind of numerical questions you will see in GATE Robotics and Automation (RA).

Key terms

Sensor
A device that detects a physical quantity and gives an electrical signal related to it.
Transducer
A device that converts energy from one form to another. A sensor is one kind of transducer.
Measurand
The physical quantity being measured, such as strain, displacement or speed.
Resistance (R)
How hard it is for current to flow, in ohms. Precise: R = ρL/A for a wire of length L and area A.
Strain
The fractional change in length of a material when it is stretched or squeezed. It has no unit. Written ε.
Strain gauge
A thin resistive foil glued to a surface. When the surface strains, the foil's resistance changes.
Gauge factor (GF)
The ratio of fractional change in resistance to strain: GF = (ΔR/R)/ε. It has no unit.
Potentiometer
A resistor with a sliding contact, called the wiper. Moving the wiper changes the resistance between the wiper and one end.
Capacitance (C)
The ability of two conductors separated by an insulator to store charge, in farads (F).
Permittivity (ε)
How easily an insulator (dielectric) lets an electric field pass through it. Free space has ε0 = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F/m.
Inductance (L)
The ability of a coil to store energy in a magnetic field, in henries (H).
Sensitivity
How much the output changes for a given change in input. Precise: sensitivity = change in output / change in input.
Linearity
How closely the output follows a straight line when the input changes.

Step 1: Resistive sensors

A resistive sensor changes its resistance when a physical quantity changes. Two examples cover most of what GATE asks: the strain gauge, which senses small changes in length, and the potentiometer, which senses position.

The strain gauge

Stretch a wire and it becomes longer and thinner. Its resistance goes up. Squeeze it and the resistance goes down. A strain gauge uses a thin metal foil pattern glued to a part, so that the foil stretches exactly when the part stretches. The foil's fractional change in resistance is proportional to the strain:

GF = (ΔR / R) / ε, so ΔR = R × GF × ε

Symbols: R is the unstrained resistance, ΔR is the change in resistance, ε is the strain (a number, with no unit), and GF is the gauge factor. A common value of GF for metal foil gauges is about 2. The formula works both ways: from strain you get ΔR, and from ΔR you get strain.

Worked example: a gauge on a bracket

A gauge has R = 120 Ω and GF = 2.0. The bracket strains by 500 microstrain, which is ε = 500 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.0005. Step by step:

  1. Write the formula: ΔR = R × GF × ε.
  2. Substitute: ΔR = 120 × 2.0 × 0.0005.
  3. Multiply: 120 × 2.0 = 240, and 240 × 0.0005 = 0.12.
  4. So ΔR = 0.12 Ω, and the fractional change is 0.12 / 120 = 0.001.
R = 120.0          # gauge resistance in ohms
GF = 2.0           # gauge factor (no unit)
strain = 500e-6    # 500 microstrain, written as a plain number
dR = R * GF * strain
print("delta R =", round(dR, 3), "ohm")
print("delta R / R =", round(dR / R, 4))
delta R = 0.12 ohm
delta R / R = 0.001

The change of 0.12 Ω is very small. A strain gauge is always read with a Wheatstone bridge, a circuit of four resistors that is balanced when no strain is present. For a quarter bridge with one active gauge, and small changes, the output voltage is approximately Vout = Vs × (ΔR/R) / 4, where Vs is the supply voltage. This is a standard approximation for small changes.

Vs = 5.0           # bridge supply voltage in volts
ratio = 0.001      # delta R / R of the active arm
Vout = Vs * ratio / 4
print("Vout =", round(Vout * 1000, 3), "mV")
Vout = 1.25 mV

The potentiometer

A potentiometer is a resistor with a sliding contact. If the total track has resistance Rtotal and the wiper is at a fraction x/L of the track, the resistance from one end to the wiper is Rtotal × x/L. Used as a voltage divider with supply Vs, the unloaded output is Vout = Vs × x/L. The output is a straight line in position, which makes the potentiometer a simple position sensor. Its weakness is friction and wear at the wiper.

TrapThe strain gauge formula uses the fractional change ΔR/R, not the absolute change ΔR. A question that gives ΔR and asks for strain must divide by R first.

A gauge with GF = 2 has ΔR/R = 0.004. What is the strain?

ε = (ΔR/R) / GF = 0.004 / 2 = 0.002, which is 2000 microstrain.

A potentiometer has Vs = 5 V and the wiper is at 30% of the track. What is Vout?

Vout = 5 × 0.3 = 1.5 V.

Step 2: Capacitive sensors

A capacitor is two conducting plates separated by an insulator. Its capacitance depends on the plate area, the separation and the insulator. A capacitive sensor uses one of these three quantities as the input, so that the measured quantity changes the capacitance. For a parallel-plate capacitor with air between the plates:

C = ε0 × εr × A / d

Symbols: C is the capacitance in farads, ε0 is the permittivity of free space, 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F/m, εr is the relative permittivity of the insulator (1 for air), A is the area of one plate in square metres, and d is the separation in metres. The three sensor modes are:

  • Distance mode: the gap d changes. C is inversely proportional to d, so the response is not a straight line.
  • Area mode: the overlap A changes. C is proportional to A, so the response is a straight line. This is the mode to choose when linearity matters.
  • Dielectric mode: the material between the plates changes, for example a liquid level or moisture. εr changes, so C changes.

Worked example: a 1 mm gap

A capacitor has plates of area 100 cm², which is A = 0.01 m², with air between them. The gap is 1 mm, so d = 0.001 m. The gap then increases to 1.1 mm.

  1. Before. C1 = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.01 / 0.001 = 8.85 × 10⁻¹¹ F, which is 88.5 pF.
  2. After. C2 = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.01 / 0.0011 = 80.45 pF.
  3. Change. (80.45 − 88.5) / 88.5 = −9.09%. A 10% increase in gap gives about a 9% fall in capacitance, because C is proportional to 1/d.
import math
eps0 = 8.85e-12    # permittivity of free space, F/m
er = 1.0           # air
A = 0.01           # plate area, 100 cm^2 in m^2
d1 = 1.0e-3        # gap 1 mm
d2 = 1.1e-3        # gap 1.1 mm
C1 = eps0 * er * A / d1
C2 = eps0 * er * A / d2
print("C1 =", round(C1 * 1e12, 1), "pF")
print("C2 =", round(C2 * 1e12, 2), "pF")
print("percent change =", round((C2 - C1) / C1 * 100, 2), "%")
C1 = 88.5 pF
C2 = 80.45 pF
percent change = -9.09 %

Key ideaIn distance mode the capacitance falls as the gap grows. In area mode the capacitance rises as the overlap grows. Always check which quantity is changing before you decide the direction of the change.

TrapDo not treat a percent change in gap as the same percent change in capacitance. Because C is proportional to 1/d, a 10% increase in d gives about a 9.1% decrease in C, not 10%.

A capacitive sensor in distance mode has its gap halved. What happens to C?

C doubles, because C is proportional to 1/d and 1/(d/2) = 2/d.

Which capacitive mode gives a straight-line output?

Area mode, because C is directly proportional to the overlap area A.

Step 3: Inductive sensors

An inductor is a coil of wire. When current flows, it creates a magnetic field, and the coil stores energy in that field. The ability to store this energy is called inductance. For a coil with N turns on a core of area A and length l, with permeability μ:

L = N² × μ × A / l

Symbols: L is the inductance in henries, N is the number of turns, μ is the permeability of the core (for air, μ0 = 4π × 10⁻⁷ H/m), A is the core area in square metres, and l is the length of the magnetic path in metres. Notice that N appears squared. Doubling the turns quadruples the inductance.

An inductive sensor changes L by moving part of the magnetic path. A common design has a movable iron core. When the core moves in or out of the coil, the inductance changes. The measured displacement is therefore converted into a change in inductance. The LVDT, which uses the same principle to give a voltage, is covered in the next topic.

Worked example: inductance of a coil

A coil has N = 500 turns, core area A = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ m² and path length l = 0.1 m, with air as the core. Find L.

  1. Write the formula: L = N² μ0 A / l.
  2. N² = 500² = 250 000.
  3. μ0 = 4π × 10⁻⁷ = 1.2566 × 10⁻⁶ H/m.
  4. Multiply: 250 000 × 1.2566 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.31416. Then × A = 0.31416 × 10⁻⁴ = 3.1416 × 10⁻⁵. Divide by l: 3.1416 × 10⁻⁵ / 0.1 = 3.1416 × 10⁻⁴ H.
  5. So L = 314 µH.
import math
mu0 = 4 * math.pi * 1e-7   # permeability of free space, H/m
N = 500                    # number of turns
A = 1.0e-4                 # core area, m^2
l = 0.1                    # magnetic path length, m
L = N**2 * mu0 * A / l
print("L =", round(L * 1e6, 1), "microhenry")
L = 314.2 microhenry

TrapUse μ0 for air and the stated core permeability for iron. Forgetting the square on N is a common error in GATE questions, and it changes the answer by a large factor.

GATE loves thisExpect questions asking how L changes when the number of turns or the air gap changes. For a simple air gap, L falls as the gap grows, and the N² rule applies to the turns.

A coil has N = 200 turns. The turns are changed to 400, with everything else the same. By what factor does L change?

By 4, because L is proportional to N². (400/200)² = 4.

Why is the inductive sensor output usually a change in inductance and not a voltage?

The sensor is a passive element. Its input is a displacement that changes its inductance, and a separate circuit turns that change into a measurable signal.

Step 4: Choosing a sensor

Every sensor has a sensitivity, a linear range and a typical use. Comparing these three is the usual exam question.

SensorPrincipleTypical measurandLinearityTypical use
Strain gaugeResistance changes with lengthStrain, force, pressureGood over small strainsLoad cells, robot arm stress
PotentiometerResistance set by wiper positionLinear or angular positionGood, but wiper wearsSimple joint position
Capacitive, area modeC proportional to overlapSmall displacement, levelLinearTouch and level sensing
Capacitive, distance modeC proportional to 1/gapGap, proximityNon-linearProximity sensing
InductiveL changes with core positionDisplacementLinear over a limited rangePosition in robots and tools

Sensitivity is the ratio of output change to input change. For a capacitive distance sensor, the sensitivity is largest at small gaps, because C changes fastest when d is small. A sensor with high sensitivity can detect small changes, but its range is often short. A sensor with a long range and good linearity is often less sensitive. This trade-off is the heart of most design questions.

Key ideaSensitivity, range and linearity are different things. A sensor can be very sensitive and still not linear. Always check the linear range in the question before you assume a straight-line output.

Which capacitive mode is best if the output must be a straight line over a wide range?

Area mode, because C is directly proportional to the overlap area.

Why might a potentiometer be a poor choice in a dusty environment?

Dust can get into the sliding contact, which adds noise and wears the track faster.

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original GATE-style questions for practice. They are not real past paper questions. Try each one before reading the answer.

Change in resistance of a strain gauge

A strain gauge has R = 120 Ω and gauge factor GF = 2. It is strained by 500 microstrain. Find the change in resistance ΔR in ohms.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. ΔR in ohms.
  2. Which idea applies. ΔR = R × GF × ε.
  3. Work it out. ε = 500 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.0005. ΔR = 120 × 2 × 0.0005 = 0.12 Ω.
  4. Check. ΔR/R = 0.12/120 = 0.001, which equals GF × ε = 2 × 0.0005.
R = 120.0
GF = 2.0
strain = 500e-6
dR = R * GF * strain
print("delta R =", round(dR, 3), "ohm")
delta R = 0.12 ohm

Answer: ΔR = 0.12 Ω.

Which sensor statements are true?

Select all that apply.
(A) A strain gauge output is a change in resistance.
(B) In a parallel-plate capacitive sensor, C increases when the plate separation increases.
(C) In a parallel-plate capacitive sensor, C increases when the overlap area increases.
(D) An inductance increases when the number of turns increases.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements match the sensor formulas.
  2. Which idea applies. C = ε0 εr A / d, L = N² μ A / l, and the strain gauge rule.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) False, C falls when d grows. (C) True, C is proportional to A. (D) True, L is proportional to N².
  4. Check. Look at the formula: d is in the denominator, so bigger d means smaller C.

Answer: (A), (C) and (D).

Capacitance after the gap changes

A parallel-plate capacitive sensor has plate area 0.01 m², air between the plates, and a gap of 1 mm. The gap increases to 1.1 mm. Find the percent change in capacitance, to two decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Percent change in C.
  2. Which idea applies. C = ε0 A / d, so C is inversely proportional to d.
  3. Work it out. C1 = 88.5 pF and C2 = 80.45 pF. Change = (80.45 − 88.5)/88.5 = −9.09%.
  4. Check. A 10% increase in gap should give a fall of a little less than 10%, so −9.09% is reasonable.
eps0 = 8.85e-12
er = 1.0
A = 0.01
C1 = eps0 * er * A / 1.0e-3
C2 = eps0 * er * A / 1.1e-3
print("percent change =", round((C2 - C1) / C1 * 100, 2), "%")
percent change = -9.09 %

Answer: −9.09%, a fall of about 9.09%.

Output of a quarter bridge

A quarter Wheatstone bridge has supply Vs = 5 V and one active strain gauge. The gauge changes by ΔR/R = 0.001. Using the small-change approximation Vout = Vs (ΔR/R)/4, find Vout in millivolts.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Vout in mV.
  2. Which idea applies. The quarter bridge approximation.
  3. Work it out. Vout = 5 × 0.001 / 4 = 0.00125 V = 1.25 mV.
  4. Check. The output is about one thousandth of the supply divided by four, which is the right size for a small strain.
Vs = 5.0
ratio = 0.001
Vout = Vs * ratio / 4
print("Vout =", round(Vout * 1000, 3), "mV")
Vout = 1.25 mV

Answer: 1.25 mV.

Inductance of a coil

A coil has N = 500 turns on a core of area 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ m² and magnetic path length 0.1 m, with air as the core. Find L in microhenries, to one decimal place.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. L in µH.
  2. Which idea applies. L = N² μ0 A / l.
  3. Work it out. N² = 250 000. μ0 = 1.2566 × 10⁻⁶. So L = 250 000 × 1.2566 × 10⁻⁶ × 10⁻⁴ / 0.1 = 3.142 × 10⁻⁴ H = 314.2 µH.
  4. Check. A coil with 500 turns is a few hundred microhenries, which is a sensible size.
import math
mu0 = 4 * math.pi * 1e-7
N = 500
A = 1.0e-4
l = 0.1
L = N**2 * mu0 * A / l
print("L =", round(L * 1e6, 1), "microhenry")
L = 314.2 microhenry

Answer: L = 314.2 µH.

Capacitance after the gap shrinks

A parallel-plate capacitive sensor has area 0.01 m², air between the plates, and a gap of 1 mm, so its capacitance is 88.5 pF. A force closes the gap to 0.8 mm. Find the new capacitance in picofarads, to one decimal place.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. C in pF after the gap closes.
  2. Which idea applies. C is proportional to 1/d, with A and ε0 fixed.
  3. Work it out. Cnew = 88.5 × (1.0 / 0.8) = 110.6 pF.
  4. Check. The gap fell, so C must rise. 110.6 is bigger than 88.5, which is correct.
eps0 = 8.85e-12
A = 0.01
C = eps0 * A / 1.0e-3
C_new = eps0 * A / 0.8e-3
print("C =", round(C * 1e12, 1), "pF")
print("C_new =", round(C_new * 1e12, 1), "pF")
C = 88.5 pF
C_new = 110.6 pF

Answer: Cnew = 110.6 pF.

Back-calculating the gauge factor

A strain gauge with R = 120 Ω shows a change of 0.24 Ω when the strain is 500 microstrain. Find the gauge factor GF.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. GF, a number with no unit.
  2. Which idea applies. GF = (ΔR/R) / ε, rearranged from the gauge formula.
  3. Work it out. ΔR/R = 0.24 / 120 = 0.002. ε = 0.0005. GF = 0.002 / 0.0005 = 4.0.
  4. Check. Multiply back: R × GF × ε = 120 × 4 × 0.0005 = 0.24 Ω, which matches the given change.
R = 120.0
dR = 0.24
strain = 500e-6
GF = (dR / R) / strain
print("GF =", round(GF, 2))
GF = 4.0

Answer: GF = 4.0.

Statements about sensor design

Select all that apply.
(A) The inductance of a coil is proportional to N².
(B) A capacitive distance sensor has a constant sensitivity over its whole range.
(C) The gauge factor is a dimensionless number.
(D) A resistive sensor always gives a perfectly linear output.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which design statements are true.
  2. Which idea applies. Sensor formulas and the idea of linearity.
  3. Work it out. (A) True, from L = N² μ A / l. (B) False, C = k/d changes faster at small gaps, so the sensitivity is not constant. (C) True, GF is a ratio of two fractional changes. (D) False, many resistive sensors are linear only over a limited range.
  4. Check. Any claim that says "always" or "constant" should be checked against the formula.

Answer: (A) and (C).

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: RTD resistance (NAT, 2 marks)

A Pt100 RTD has R0 = 100 Ω at 0 °C and α = 0.00385 /°C. Find its resistance at 120 °C, correct to 1 decimal place.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. R(T) with the linear model.
  2. Which idea applies. R = R0(1 + αT).
  3. Work it out. 100(1 + 0.462) = 146.2 Ω.
  4. Check. Each degree adds 0.385 Ω.

Answer: 146.2 Ω.

Exam-level 2: Parallel plate capacitive sensor (NAT, 2 marks)

A parallel plate sensor has capacitance 20 pF at a gap of 1 mm. The gap shrinks to 0.8 mm. Find the new capacitance.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The new capacitance.
  2. Which idea applies. C = εA/d, so C is inversely proportional to the gap.
  3. Work it out. 20 · 1/0.8 = 25 pF.
  4. Check. A smaller gap gives a larger capacitance, as expected.

Answer: 25 pF.

Exam-level 3: Quarter bridge output (NAT, 2 marks)

A Wheatstone bridge with four 120 Ω arms and 10 V excitation has one arm replaced by a sensor whose resistance rises by 1.2 Ω. Find the output voltage in mV, correct to 2 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The bridge imbalance voltage.
  2. Which idea applies. Vo = V[Rs/(Rs + R) − 1/2].
  3. Work it out. 121.2/241.2 = 0.502488. Minus 0.5 gives 0.002488. Times 10 V: 24.88 mV.
  4. Check. The linear estimate VΔR/(4R) = 25 mV is close.
V, R, dR = 10, 120, 1.2
print("%.2f mV" % (1000 * V * ((R + dR) / (2 * R + dR) - 0.5)))

24.88 mV

Answer: about 24.88 mV.

Remember these

  1. Strain gauge: ΔR = R × GF × ε. Always use the fractional change ΔR/R.
  2. Capacitance falls as the gap grows. Use C proportional to 1/d.
  3. For a straight-line capacitive output, use area mode.
  4. Inductance goes as the square of the turns, N².
  5. Sensitivity, linearity and range are different properties. Check all three.
  6. Quarter bridge output is about Vs (ΔR/R) / 4 for small changes.

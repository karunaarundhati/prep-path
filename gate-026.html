Double and triple integrals

A double integral adds up a quantity over a flat region, and a triple integral does the same over a solid. They give areas, volumes and totals, and changing the order or the coordinates often makes a hard problem easy, which is why they appear often in GATE RA and DA numericals.

The idea in plain words

Think about a single integral first. An ordinary integral adds up many thin slices. If you want the area under a curve, you cut the area into thin vertical strips, find the height of each strip, multiply by its tiny width, and add all the pieces.

A double integral does the same thing in two directions. Instead of strips, you cut a flat region into tiny squares. Each square has a small area. On each square you measure something, such as a height, a density or a temperature, multiply it by the small area, and add all the results. The total is a double integral.

A triple integral goes one step further. It cuts a solid into tiny blocks, measures a quantity in each block, and adds. When the quantity is just 1, the total is the volume of the solid.

Why does this matter for GATE? Many questions ask for an area, a volume, a centre of mass or a probability over a region. These are all double or triple integrals. Coordinates such as polar or spherical coordinates make many regions simple, and the Jacobian is the factor that keeps the area or volume correct when you switch coordinates.

Key ideaA double integral of 1 over a region is its area. A triple integral of 1 over a solid is its volume. Everything else is the same idea with a different quantity inside.

Key terms

Double integral
The sum of f(x,y) over a flat region R, written ∬R f(x,y) dA. Plain meaning: add up f times tiny areas.
Iterated integral
A double integral computed as two single integrals, one after the other. For example ∫ from a to b of (∫ from c to d of f dy) dx.
Rectangular region
A region of the form a ≤ x ≤ b and c ≤ y ≤ d. Its limits are all constants.
Type I and type II regions
Type I: a ≤ x ≤ b with the y-limits given by functions of x. Type II: c ≤ y ≤ d with the x-limits given by functions of y.
Order of integration
Whether you integrate with respect to y first (inside) or x first. Swapping it is allowed and sometimes makes the integral easy.
Triple integral
The sum of f(x,y,z) over a solid region, written ∭ f dV.
Jacobian
A factor, written |∂(x,y)/∂(u,v)|, that stretches or shrinks a tiny area when you change coordinates. It is the number that multiplies dx dy when you switch to new variables.
Polar coordinates
Points described by a distance r from the origin and an angle θ. The map is x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ, with Jacobian r.
Cylindrical coordinates
Polar coordinates in the xy-plane plus a height z. The Jacobian is r.
Spherical coordinates
Points described by a distance ρ from the origin, an angle θ around the z-axis, and an angle φ down from the z-axis. The Jacobian is ρ2 sin φ.

Step 1: Double integrals over a rectangle

Compute one direction at a time

For a rectangle, the limits never depend on the other variable, so you can handle the integral in two stages. First integrate with respect to one variable while the other is treated as a constant. Then integrate the result with respect to the second variable.

∫ from 0 to 2 of ( ∫ from 0 to 1 of x y dx ) dy

Here x and y are the variables, and the numbers 0, 1 and 2 are the limits. The inner integral runs over x, so y is a constant during it.

Worked example: every step

  1. Inner integral. ∫ from 0 to 1 of x y dx. The factor y is a constant, so it comes out: y × ∫ from 0 to 1 of x dx.
  2. Evaluate. ∫ from 0 to 1 of x dx = [x2/2] from 0 to 1 = 1/2 - 0 = 1/2. So the inner integral is y × 1/2 = y/2.
  3. Outer integral. ∫ from 0 to 2 of (y/2) dy = (1/2) × ∫ from 0 to 2 of y dy.
  4. Evaluate. ∫ from 0 to 2 of y dy = [y2/2] from 0 to 2 = 4/2 - 0 = 2. Then (1/2) × 2 = 1.

So the double integral of x y over the rectangle 0 ≤ x ≤ 1, 0 ≤ y ≤ 2 is 1.

TipWhen the function splits into a product of an x-part and a y-part, as x y does, the double integral is simply the product of two single integrals. Here it is (1/2) × 2 = 1.

TrapKeep the inner limits with the inner variable. In ∫ from 0 to 2 of (∫ from 0 to 1 of x y dx) dy, the numbers 0 and 1 belong to x, and 0 and 2 belong to y. Mixing them up gives a wrong answer with no warning.

Question: What is ∫ from 0 to 3 of ∫ from 0 to 2 of y dy dx?

The inner integral in y gives [y2/2] from 0 to 2 = 2. The outer integral in x is ∫ from 0 to 3 of 2 dx = 6.

Question: Why can the inner integral be done first?

Over a rectangle the limits of each variable are constants, so the variables do not depend on each other. Doing one integral first does not change the other limits.

Step 2: Regions that are not rectangles

Slice the region

Many regions are bounded by curves. The trick is to describe the region with one variable running over constant limits and the other running between two curves. Think of the region as a stack of vertical strips. Each strip starts on a lower curve and ends on an upper curve.

Consider the region between y = x2 (the lower curve) and y = x (the upper curve), for x from 0 to 1. For each x, y goes from x2 up to x. Then:

Area = ∫ from 0 to 1 of ( ∫ from x2 to x of 1 dy ) dx

Worked example: area between two curves

  1. Inner integral. ∫ from x2 to x of 1 dy = [y] from x2 to x = x - x2. This is the height of the strip.
  2. Outer integral. ∫ from 0 to 1 of (x - x2) dx.
  3. Evaluate. [x2/2 - x3/3] from 0 to 1 = (1/2 - 1/3) - 0 = 1/6.

The area is 1/6. The Python check later in this step confirms the number.

Changing the order of integration

The same region can be sliced horizontally instead. Look at the triangle with corners (0,0), (1,0) and (1,1). It is the set of points with 0 ≤ y ≤ x ≤ 1. Written with x outside:

∫ from 0 to 1 of ( ∫ from 0 to x of f dy ) dx = ∫ from 0 to 1 of ( ∫ from y to 1 of f dx ) dy

The right-hand side slices the same triangle with y outside. For each y, x runs from y up to 1. Both sides describe the same region, so they give the same answer. Sometimes one order is much easier.

Worked example: switching the order

Evaluate ∫ from 0 to 1 of ( ∫ from x to 1 of ey2 dy ) dx. The inner integral has no elementary antiderivative, so try the other order.

  1. Read the region. The limits say 0 ≤ x ≤ y ≤ 1. For each y, x runs from 0 to y.
  2. Rewrite. ∫ from 0 to 1 of ( ∫ from 0 to y of ey2 dx ) dy.
  3. Inner integral. The integrand does not depend on x, so it is ey2 × (y - 0) = y ey2.
  4. Outer integral. Let u = y2, so du = 2y dy. Then ∫ from 0 to 1 of y ey2 dy = (1/2) ∫ from 0 to 1 of eu du = (1/2)(e - 1).

The answer is (e - 1)/2, about 0.859.

GATE loves thisWhen an inner integral looks impossible, sketch the region and try the other order. The region does not change, only the way you slice it.

TrapWhen you swap the order, the limits of the new inner integral must be functions of the new outer variable. Read the old limits off a sketch, not by copying them. For 0 ≤ y ≤ x ≤ 1, the new inner limits are y to 1, not 0 to x.

Question: What is the area of the region 0 ≤ y ≤ x ≤ 1 (the triangle)?

Area = ∫ from 0 to 1 of ( ∫ from 0 to x of 1 dy ) dx = ∫ from 0 to 1 of x dx = 1/2. This is half the unit square, as expected.

Question: Why do both orders give the same answer?

Both orders add up the same function over the same set of points. Only the slicing changes, so the total is the same.

Step 3: Triple integrals and volume

From area to volume

A triple integral adds a third direction. The integrand is f(x,y,z), and the region is a solid. Set the integrand to 1 to get the volume. The usual order is innermost z, then y, then x, though any order that matches the region works.

Volume = ∭ 1 dV = ∫ ( ∫ ( ∫ 1 dz ) dy ) dx

Worked example: the tetrahedron

Find the volume of the solid in the first octant (x, y, z ≥ 0) under the plane x + y + z = 1. Its base is the triangle x + y ≤ 1 in the xy-plane.

  1. Limits for z. z starts at 0 and goes up to the plane, so z goes from 0 to 1 - x - y.
  2. Limits for y. Over the base triangle, y goes from 0 to 1 - x.
  3. Limits for x. x goes from 0 to 1.
  4. Innermost integral. ∫ from 0 to 1-x-y of 1 dz = 1 - x - y.
  5. Middle integral. Let a = 1 - x. Then ∫ from 0 to a of (a - y) dy = [a y - y2/2] from 0 to a = a2 - a2/2 = a2/2 = (1 - x)2/2.
  6. Outer integral. ∫ from 0 to 1 of (1 - x)2/2 dx. Let u = 1 - x, so dx = -du, and the limits become 1 to 0. This is ∫ from 0 to 1 of u2/2 du = [u3/6] from 0 to 1 = 1/6.

The volume is 1/6. This matches the formula a3/6 for a corner tetrahedron with legs of length a = 1.

TrapThe base of a solid is a region in the xy-plane. Its limits come from the sketch of that base, not from the solid's height. Many wrong answers come from using a square base when the base is a triangle.

Question: What is the volume of the unit cube 0 ≤ x, y, z ≤ 1?

The triple integral of 1 is ∫ from 0 to 1 of ∫ from 0 to 1 of ∫ from 0 to 1 of 1 dz dy dx = 1 × 1 × 1 = 1.

Question: The corner tetrahedron has legs of length 2. What is its volume?

Use a3/6 with a = 2. Volume = 8/6 = 4/3.

Step 4: Change of variables and the Jacobian

Why change coordinates?

A disk is awkward in x and y, because its boundary is x2 + y2 = 4 and the limits are messy. In polar coordinates the boundary is simply r = 2, and the limits are easy. The problem is that a tiny square in x and y is not a tiny square in r and θ. The Jacobian fixes this by saying how much area one tiny square of r and θ really covers.

The Jacobian rule

∬R f(x,y) dx dy = ∬S f(x(u,v), y(u,v)) · |∂(x,y)/∂(u,v)| du dv

The Jacobian determinant is

∂(x,y)/∂(u,v) = det [ ∂x/∂u ∂x/∂v ; ∂y/∂u ∂y/∂v ]

For polar coordinates, x = r cos θ and y = r sin θ. The partial derivatives are ∂x/∂r = cos θ, ∂x/∂θ = -r sin θ, ∂y/∂r = sin θ and ∂y/∂θ = r cos θ. The determinant is cos θ × r cos θ - (-r sin θ) × sin θ = r cos2 θ + r sin2 θ = r. So dx dy becomes r dr dθ.

Worked example: area of a disk

  1. Region. The disk x2 + y2 ≤ 4 is r ≤ 2 in polar coordinates, with 0 ≤ θ ≤ 2π.
  2. Integrand. For area, f = 1, and the Jacobian is r, so the integrand is r.
  3. Inner integral in r. ∫ from 0 to 2 of r dr = [r2/2] from 0 to 2 = 2.
  4. Outer integral in θ. ∫ from 0 to 2π of 2 dθ = 4π.

The area of the disk of radius 2 is 4π, about 12.566.

Cylindrical and spherical coordinates

For solids, two more systems are common. In cylindrical coordinates, x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ and z stays as z. The Jacobian is r. In spherical coordinates, x = ρ sin φ cos θ, y = ρ sin φ sin θ and z = ρ cos φ. The Jacobian is ρ2 sin φ. Use cylindrical coordinates when the solid is round around the z-axis, and spherical coordinates when it is round around the origin.

Worked example: volume of a ball

  1. Region. The unit ball is ρ ≤ 1, with 0 ≤ θ ≤ 2π and 0 ≤ φ ≤ π.
  2. Integrand. For volume, f = 1, so the integrand is ρ2 sin φ.
  3. Radial part. ∫ from 0 to 1 of ρ2 dρ = 1/3.
  4. Angle φ. ∫ from 0 to π of sin φ dφ = [-cos φ] from 0 to π = 1 + 1 = 2.
  5. Angle θ. ∫ from 0 to 2π of dθ = 2π.
  6. Multiply. (1/3) × 2 × 2π = 4π/3, about 4.189.

Key ideaThe Jacobian is the local stretching factor. Every time you change coordinates, multiply by it. Forgetting it is the most common error in change-of-variables questions.

# Midpoint rule for the double integral of x*y over 0 <= x <= 1, 0 <= y <= 2
n = 400
dx = 1 / n
dy = 2 / n
total = 0.0
for i in range(n):
    x = (i + 0.5) * dx
    for j in range(n):
        y = (j + 0.5) * dy
        total += x * y * dx * dy
print(round(total, 3))

Question: The Jacobian for a change x = 2u, y = 3v is what?

The partial derivatives are ∂x/∂u = 2, ∂x/∂v = 0, ∂y/∂u = 0 and ∂y/∂v = 3. The determinant is 2 × 3 - 0 × 0 = 6. So dx dy = 6 du dv.

Question: Why is the polar Jacobian r and not 1?

Far from the origin, a tiny step in θ moves you a long way, so a tiny region of r and θ covers a larger area. The factor r grows with distance, which is exactly what the Jacobian measures.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original GATE-style questions written for practice. They are not past exam questions.

A simple rectangle

Evaluate ∫ from 0 to 2 of ( ∫ from 0 to 3 of y dx ) dy.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A double integral over the rectangle 0 ≤ x ≤ 3, 0 ≤ y ≤ 2.
  2. Which idea applies. Integrate the inner variable first. The limits are constants.
  3. Work it out. The inner integral in x of y is y × 3 = 3y, because y is constant. The outer integral is ∫ from 0 to 2 of 3y dy = 3 × [y2/2] from 0 to 2 = 3 × 2 = 6.
  4. Check. The average height is y averaged over 0 to 2, which is 1. Times the area 3 × 2 = 6 gives 6 again.

Answer: 6.

Triangle with several claims

Let R be the region 0 ≤ y ≤ x ≤ 1. Which of the following statements are true? Select all that apply.

(A) The area of R is 1/2. (B) The area of R is 1. (C) ∫ from 0 to 1 of ( ∫ from y to 1 of 1 dx ) dy = 1/2. (D) ∫ from 0 to 1 of ( ∫ from 0 to x of x dy ) dx = 1/4.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Test each statement separately.
  2. Which idea applies. A double integral of 1 is an area. Reversing the order gives the same region.
  3. Work it out. The triangle is half the unit square, so its area is 1/2. (A) is true and (B) is false. In (C) the inner integral is 1 - y, and ∫ from 0 to 1 of (1 - y) dy = 1/2, so (C) is true. In (D) the inner integral is x × x = x2, and ∫ from 0 to 1 of x2 dx = 1/3, not 1/4, so (D) is false.
  4. Check. Both (A) and (C) describe the same triangle, so they must agree, and they do.

Answer: (A) and (C).

Area between a line and a steeper line

Find the area of the region between the lines y = x and y = 2x for 0 ≤ x ≤ 1. Give the answer as a decimal (NAT style).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The area of a region bounded by two lines.
  2. Which idea applies. Slice vertically: for each x, y runs from the lower line to the upper line.
  3. Work it out. The height of each strip is 2x - x = x. The area is ∫ from 0 to 1 of x dx = [x2/2] from 0 to 1 = 1/2. The answer is 0.5.
  4. Check. The region is a triangle with corners (0,0), (1,1) and (1,2). Its base is the vertical segment at x = 1 of length 1, and its height is 1, so its area is 1 × 1/2 = 1/2.

Answer: 0.5.

Swap the order to make it easy

Evaluate I = ∫ from 0 to 1 of ( ∫ from 0 to y of 6x dx ) dy by first changing the order of integration. Give the value of I (NAT style).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The value of I, possibly after reversing the order.
  2. Which idea applies. The region is 0 ≤ x ≤ y ≤ 1. Reverse it.
  3. Work it out. For fixed x, y runs from x to 1. So I = ∫ from 0 to 1 of ( ∫ from x to 1 of 6x dy ) dx. The inner integral is 6x(1 - x). Then ∫ from 0 to 1 of (6x - 6x2) dx = [3x2 - 2x3] from 0 to 1 = 3 - 2 = 1.
  4. Check. Directly: the inner integral gives 3y2, and ∫ from 0 to 1 of 3y2 dy = 1. Both orders agree.

Answer: 1.

Volume of a corner solid

Find the volume of the solid with x, y, z ≥ 0 and x + y + z ≤ 2. Give the answer to three decimal places (NAT style).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A volume, to three decimals.
  2. Which idea applies. A corner tetrahedron with legs of length a has volume a3/6.
  3. Work it out. Here a = 2, so the volume is 23/6 = 8/6 = 4/3 = 1.3333..., which rounds to 1.333.
  4. Check. Scaling the earlier example (legs of 1, volume 1/6) by 2 in each direction multiplies the volume by 23 = 8. So 8 × 1/6 = 4/3 again.

Answer: 1.333.

A polar integral

Evaluate ∬ (x2 + y2) dA over the unit disk x2 + y2 ≤ 1. Give the exact value.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The integral of the squared distance over a disk.
  2. Which idea applies. Polar coordinates, where x2 + y2 = r2 and dA = r dr dθ.
  3. Work it out. The integrand becomes r2, and with the Jacobian the integrand is r2 × r = r3. The limits are 0 ≤ r ≤ 1 and 0 ≤ θ ≤ 2π. So ∫ from 0 to 2π of ( ∫ from 0 to 1 of r3 dr ) dθ = ∫ from 0 to 2π of (1/4) dθ = (1/4)(2π) = π/2.
  4. Check. Numerically π/2 is about 1.571. A rough check: the disk has area π, and the average of x2 + y2 over the disk is 1/2 (its average of r2 is 1/2), so π × 1/2 = π/2.

Answer: π/2.

Area of an ellipse by change of variables

Find the area of the ellipse x2/4 + y2/9 ≤ 1 by substituting x = 2u and y = 3v. Give the exact value.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The area of an ellipse with semi-axes 2 and 3.
  2. Which idea applies. The Jacobian of the substitution.
  3. Work it out. The Jacobian is |2 × 3 - 0 × 0| = 6. The ellipse becomes u2 + v2 ≤ 1, the unit disk. So the area is ∬ 6 du dv = 6 × (area of the unit disk) = 6π.
  4. Check. An ellipse with semi-axes a and b has area πab. Here πab = π × 2 × 3 = 6π.

Answer: 6π, about 18.85.

Volume of a half ball

Find the volume of the upper half of the ball of radius 3, that is, the points with x2 + y2 + z2 ≤ 9 and z ≥ 0. Give the exact value.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The volume of half of a ball.
  2. Which idea applies. Spherical coordinates, with the Jacobian ρ2 sin φ.
  3. Work it out. The upper half has 0 ≤ φ ≤ π/2 and 0 ≤ ρ ≤ 3, with θ from 0 to 2π. The radial part is ∫ from 0 to 3 of ρ2 dρ = 27/3 = 9. The angle part in φ is ∫ from 0 to π/2 of sin φ dφ = [-cos φ] from 0 to π/2 = 0 - (-1) = 1. The angle part in θ is 2π. Multiply: 9 × 1 × 2π = 18π.
  4. Check. The full ball has volume (4/3)π × 33 = 36π. Half of that is 18π.

Answer: 18π, about 56.55.

Exam-level 1: mass of a triangular plate (NAT, 2 marks)

A thin triangular metal plate occupies the region 0 ≤ y ≤ x ≤ 2 in the xy-plane, where lengths are in metres. Its density at a point (x,y) is rho(x,y) = xy kilograms per square metre. The mass of the plate is the double integral of rho over the region. Set up the iterated integral with y running from 0 to x and x running from 0 to 2, evaluate it, and give the mass in kilograms.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The total mass, which is the double integral of the density over the triangle.
  2. Which idea applies. Write the region as an iterated integral, do the inner integral first, then the outer one.
  3. Work it out. Mass = integral from x = 0 to 2 of (integral from y = 0 to x of xy dy) dx. The inner integral is x times (x2/2), which is x3/2. The outer integral is [x4/8] from 0 to 2 = 16/8 = 2.
  4. Check. The Python check below adds up the density over many small squares. The result should be close to 2.
N = 400
h = 2 / N
total = 0.0
for i in range(N):
    x = (i + 0.5) * h
    for j in range(N):
        y = (j + 0.5) * h
        if y <= x:
            total += x * y * h * h
print("mass about", round(total, 1))
mass about 2.0

Answer: 2 kilograms.

Exam-level 2: swapping the order of integration (MSQ, 2 marks)

For a continuous function f(x,y), the double integral over a region is written as the integral from y = 0 to 1 of (the integral from x = y to 1 of f(x,y) dx) dy. Which of the following statements are true? Select all that apply.

(A) The region is the triangle with corners (0, 0), (1, 0) and (1, 1). (B) The area of the region is 1/2. (C) The same integral can be written as the integral from x = 0 to 1 of (the integral from y = 0 to x of f(x,y) dy) dx. (D) The area of the region is 1.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which of the four statements are true for this region and its two orders of integration.
  2. Which idea applies. Describe the region in words, sketch its corners, then read the same region in the other order.
  3. Work it out. For each y between 0 and 1, x runs from y to 1, and y is at least 0. So the boundaries are y = 0, x = 1 and x = y. The corners are (0, 0), (1, 0) and (1, 1), so (A) is true. Reading the region with x fixed first: for each x between 0 and 1, y runs from 0 to x. That is statement (C), so (C) is true. The area is the triangle's area, half of the unit square's area of 1, so (B) is true and (D) is false.
  4. Check. The Python check below counts small squares inside the region and estimates its area.
N = 500
h = 1 / N
area = 0.0
for i in range(N):
    x = (i + 0.5) * h
    for j in range(N):
        y = (j + 0.5) * h
        if y <= x:
            area += h * h
print("area about", round(area, 2))
area about 0.5

Answer: (A), (B) and (C). (D) is wrong because 1 is the area of the whole unit square, not of this triangle.

Exam-level 3: moment of inertia in polar coordinates (NAT, 2 marks)

A flat disc of radius 2 metres has uniform density 1 kilogram per square metre. It spins about an axis through its centre, perpendicular to the disc. Its moment of inertia is I = the double integral of (x2 + y2) over the disc. Switch to polar coordinates, where x2 + y2 = r2 and dA = r dr dθ, with r from 0 to 2 and θ from 0 to 2π. Evaluate I and give the answer to two decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The moment of inertia I as a number.
  2. Which idea applies. Polar coordinates, with the extra factor r from the Jacobian.
  3. Work it out. The integrand becomes r2 times the Jacobian r, which is r3. Then I = (integral from θ = 0 to 2π of dθ) times (integral from r = 0 to 2 of r3 dr) = 2π times [r4/4] from 0 to 2 = 2π times 4 = 8π. Since π is about 3.14159, 8π is about 25.13.
  4. Check. The Python check adds up r3 dr dθ over a fine polar grid. The sum should be close to 8π.
import math

R = 2.0
Nr = 400
Nt = 400
dr = R / Nr
dt = 2 * math.pi / Nt
I = 0.0
for i in range(Nr):
    r = (i + 0.5) * dr
    for j in range(Nt):
        I += r * r * r * dr * dt
print("I about", round(I, 2))
I about 25.13

Answer: 8π, about 25.13 kg m2.

Exam-level 4: volume under a bowl over a square (NAT, 2 marks)

The height of a hill above a square piece of land is modelled by z = x2 + y2, where x and y run from 0 to 1 kilometre and z is the height in hundreds of metres. The volume of material between the land and the surface is the double integral of z over the square. Use the fact that the two terms give equal contributions by symmetry, evaluate the volume, and give the answer to three decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The volume under z = x2 + y2 over the unit square.
  2. Which idea applies. Split the double integral into two single integrals, and use symmetry to see they are equal.
  3. Work it out. The integral of x2 over the unit square is (integral from 0 to 1 of x2 dx) times (integral from 0 to 1 of 1 dy) = 1/3 times 1 = 1/3. The y2 term gives the same value by symmetry, 1/3. The total is 1/3 + 1/3 = 2/3, which is about 0.667.
  4. Check. The Python check below uses a fine grid of small squares to add up the volume.
N = 500
h = 1 / N
total = 0.0
for i in range(N):
    x = (i + 0.5) * h
    for j in range(N):
        y = (j + 0.5) * h
        total += (x * x + y * y) * h * h
print("volume about", round(total, 3))
volume about 0.667

Answer: 2/3, about 0.667 (hundreds of metres times square kilometres).

Summary

Double integral
A sum of f over a flat region, written with two integral signs. Plain meaning: add f times tiny areas.
Iterated integral
A double integral done as two single integrals, one inside the other.
Area as an integral
The double integral of 1 over the region R gives its area.
Type I region
A region with x between constants and y between two functions of x.
Order of integration
The choice of which variable is integrated first. Swapping it is allowed for the same region.
Triple integral
A sum of f over a solid. With f = 1 it gives the volume.
Jacobian
The factor |∂(x,y)/∂(u,v)| that corrects the area when coordinates change.
Polar coordinates
Distance r and angle θ. Area element r dr dθ.
Cylindrical coordinates
Polar in the xy-plane plus z. Volume element r dr dθ dz.
Spherical coordinates
Distance ρ, angles θ and φ. Volume element ρ2 sin φ dρ dφ dθ.
Corner tetrahedron
A solid with legs a has volume a3/6.
Unit disk area
π, which is the double integral of 1 over r ≤ 1.

Remember these

  • Set the limits from a sketch of the region before you integrate.
  • For a region bounded by curves, the inner limits are the two curves.
  • If the inner integral is hard, try the other order, and redraw the region.
  • A double integral of 1 is an area. A triple integral of 1 is a volume.
  • Polar Jacobian is r. Spherical Jacobian is ρ2 sin φ.
  • Never forget the Jacobian when you change variables.

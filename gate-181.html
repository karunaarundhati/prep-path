Effect of damping & vibration isolation

Damping is the energy loss that makes vibrations die away, and vibration isolation is the design of springs and dampers so that a machine shakes its support as little as possible. This page explains the damping ratio, the three types of damped motion, the logarithmic decrement and transmissibility, with GATE-style numbers.

The idea in plain words

A child's swing slows down and stops. A ruler on a table stops ringing after a moment. Something is taking energy out of the motion. That something is damping. In machines it comes from a shock absorber, rubber mounts, friction in joints, or oil squeezed through a small gap.

Damping has two effects that engineers care about. First, it makes free vibrations die away. Second, it limits the size of the vibration when a force keeps pushing at resonance. Both effects matter in design, but they pull in opposite directions when we want to isolate a machine from its floor. This page shows how to balance the two.

Think of a car on its suspension. Soft springs keep the ride smooth, but a bump makes the car bounce for a long time unless the shock absorbers are good. In GATE, this topic appears in the Robotics and Automation (RA) paper, for example in questions on robot mounts and sensitive equipment.

Key terms

Each term has a plain meaning first, then a precise definition.

Damping
Any effect that removes energy from a vibrating system. Plain meaning: something that slows the motion.
Damping coefficient (c)
The constant in the damping force c ẋ, in N s/m. The force opposes the velocity.
Critical damping coefficient (cc)
The smallest damping that stops oscillation: cc = 2mωn.
Damping ratio (ζ)
The ratio of the actual damping to critical damping: ζ = c/cc = c/(2mωn).
Underdamped system
A system with ζ less than 1. It oscillates while the amplitude dies away.
Critically damped system
A system with ζ equal to 1. It returns to rest fastest without oscillating.
Overdamped system
A system with ζ greater than 1. It returns slowly without oscillating.
Damped natural frequency (ωd)
The frequency of oscillation of an underdamped system, ωd = ωn√(1 - ζ²).
Logarithmic decrement (δ)
The natural log of the ratio of two successive peak amplitudes in free damped motion.
Transmissibility (TR)
The ratio of transmitted force (or motion) to applied force (or base motion). A value below 1 means isolation.
Isolation
Designing springs and dampers so that the force or motion reaching the support is much smaller than the disturbance.
Base excitation
A vibration that comes from a moving support rather than from a force on the mass.

TrapThe damped natural frequency ωd is lower than ωn. Do not replace ωn with ωd in the frequency ratio r unless the question says so.

Damping and the damping ratio

The model

A dashpot is a piston moving in oil. It pushes back with a force proportional to the speed of the piston. For a mass on a spring with a dashpot, the free motion obeys

m x'' + c x' + k x = 0

Here c x' is the damping force. The prime (') means the first derivative with respect to time, so x' is the velocity. The damping force always opposes the velocity, so it removes energy each cycle.

   |----/\/\/\/----+----[ mass m ]----> x
   wall  spring k    |
                     |
                  [ dashpot c ]   pushes back against velocity

Critical damping and the damping ratio

There is a special amount of damping that just stops the oscillation. It is the critical damping:

cc = 2 m ωn

The damping ratio compares the actual damping with this value:

ζ = c / cc = c / (2 m ωn)

Read each symbol: c is the actual damping, m is the mass, and ωn is the undamped natural frequency. The ratio ζ has no units, and it describes the system in one number.

Worked example

A mass of 10 kg is on a spring with k = 10000 N/m, and has a dashpot with c = 63.25 N s/m. Find ζ.

Step 1. Natural frequency: ωn = √(10000/10) = 31.62 rad/s.

Step 2. Critical damping: cc = 2 × 10 × 31.62 = 632.5 N s/m.

Step 3. Damping ratio: ζ = 63.25/632.5 = 0.1.

Step 4. Check: ζ is much less than 1, so the system is underdamped and will oscillate. This is the common case for machines.

Self-check: if the damping is doubled and the mass and spring are unchanged, what is ζ?

It doubles to 0.2. Still less than 1, so the system is still underdamped.

Self-check: does the damping ratio depend on the starting push?

No. ζ depends only on the physical properties m, k and c. The starting push changes the amplitude of the motion, not the damping ratio.

Underdamped, critically damped and overdamped motion

Three behaviours

The value of ζ decides what the free motion looks like. There are three cases.

CaseDamping ratioBehaviour after a push from restExample in daily life
Underdampedζ less than 1Oscillates with decreasing amplitudeA car body after a bump, with a few bounces
Critically dampedζ = 1Returns to rest fastest, with no oscillationA good door closer that shuts smoothly
Overdampedζ greater than 1Returns to rest slowly, with no oscillationA door closer with thick oil that drags

Key ideaCritical damping is the best choice when you want the system to settle quickly without overshoot. Underdamping is the normal state of most machines.

Why the critical case is special

When ζ is less than 1, the solution has a sine-like part that keeps the motion going, but it is multiplied by a fading exponential. When ζ = 1, that oscillating part disappears and only a fading term remains. When ζ is greater than 1, there are two fading exponentials and no oscillation at all. The exponential fading is the key idea, and we study it in the next section.

Self-check: a damper is set so that ζ = 1.5. Does the mass oscillate after a push?

No. ζ = 1.5 is overdamped, so the mass returns to rest slowly without crossing its rest position.

Self-check: which case gives the smallest settling time?

The critically damped case, ζ = 1. Less damping oscillates for longer, and more damping slows the return.

Free damped vibration and the logarithmic decrement

The decaying oscillation

For an underdamped system (ζ less than 1), the free motion is

x(t) = X e-ζωnt cos(ωdt - φ), with ωd = ωn√(1 - ζ²)

Here X is the starting amplitude, e is the base of natural logarithms (about 2.718), and φ is a phase angle. The factor e-ζωnt is the decay envelope. It shrinks the amplitude over time. The cosine part repeats at the damped frequency ωd, which is a little lower than ωn.

TipDamping lowers the frequency a little. For ζ = 0.1, ωd is only about 0.5 per cent lower than ωn. For small damping, the two are almost the same.

The logarithmic decrement

Look at two successive peaks of an underdamped oscillation, one period apart. Their ratio is a fixed number, because the envelope decays in the same way every cycle. The natural log of this ratio is the logarithmic decrement:

δ = ln(x1/x2) = 2πζ / √(1 - ζ²)

For small damping, δ is close to 2πζ. We can turn this around to find ζ from a measured decay:

ζ = δ / √(4π² + δ²)

If we measure the amplitudes after n cycles, the formula becomes δ = (1/n) ln(x0/xn). This is the form most often used with measurements.

Worked example

The free vibration of a machine has a peak of 20 mm, and after two full cycles the peak is 5 mm. Find δ and ζ.

Step 1. The number of cycles is n = 2. Use δ = (1/n) ln(x0/xn) = (1/2) ln(20/5) = (1/2) ln 4.

Step 2. ln 4 = 1.3863, so δ = 0.6931.

Step 3. Find ζ: ζ = 0.6931/√(4π² + 0.6931²) = 0.6931/√(39.478 + 0.4805) = 0.6931/6.3213 = 0.1097.

Step 4. Check: the amplitude fell by a factor of 4 in two cycles, and the damping ratio is about 0.11. Small, which is typical of a steel structure.

import math

x0, xn, n = 20.0, 5.0, 2
delta = math.log(x0 / xn) / n
zeta = delta / math.sqrt(4 * math.pi ** 2 + delta ** 2)
print(f"delta = {delta:.4f}")
print(f"zeta = {zeta:.4f}")
delta = 0.6931
zeta = 0.1097

TrapUse the natural log, written ln, not log to base 10. Also remember to divide by the number of cycles when the decay is measured over several cycles.

Self-check: if the damping is zero, what is the logarithmic decrement?

Zero, because ln(1) = 0. Without damping the peaks stay the same, so the ratio is 1.

Self-check: the peaks fall by a factor of 2 each cycle. What is δ?

δ = ln 2 = 0.6931, the same as the worked example, because the decrement depends only on the ratio of successive peaks.

Effect of damping on the resonance peak

The forced response with damping

When a harmonic force drives a damped system, the steady-state amplitude is

X = (F0/k) / √((1 - r²)² + (2ζr)²)

Here F0/k is the static deflection and r = ω/ωn is the frequency ratio. The damping ratio enters through the term (2ζr)², which stops the amplitude becoming infinite at r = 1.

Peak amplitude

At r = 1 the amplitude is (F0/k)/(2ζ). That is the resonance value. The true peak of the curve is slightly shifted to a lower frequency, at

rpeak = √(1 - 2ζ²), with magnification Mpeak = 1/(2ζ√(1 - ζ²))

For small ζ the peak sits very close to r = 1, so the approximate value 1/(2ζ) is often used in GATE questions. The formula with the square root is the exact one.

Key ideaLess damping gives a higher and sharper peak. More damping lowers the peak and makes the curve flatter.

Worked example

A system with ζ = 0.1 has a static deflection F0/k = 2 mm. Find the peak amplitude.

Step 1. The magnification at the peak is 1/(2ζ√(1 - ζ²)) = 1/(0.2 × √0.99) = 1/(0.2 × 0.99499) = 5.025.

Step 2. Peak amplitude = 2 mm × 5.025 = 10.05 mm.

Step 3. Check: the approximate value 1/(2ζ) = 5, so the exact and approximate answers agree closely.

import math

z = 0.1
r_peak = math.sqrt(1 - 2 * z * z)
M_peak = 1 / (2 * z * math.sqrt(1 - z * z))
print(f"r at peak = {r_peak:.4f}")
print(f"peak magnification = {M_peak:.4f}")
print(f"peak amplitude = {2 * M_peak:.2f} mm")
r at peak = 0.9899
peak magnification = 5.0252
peak amplitude = 10.05 mm
Self-check: the damping ratio is doubled from 0.1 to 0.2. What happens to the approximate peak magnification?

It halves, from 5 to 2.5, because the approximate peak is 1/(2ζ).

Self-check: why is damping needed when a machine runs near its natural frequency?

Near resonance the amplitude is largest, and without damping it would grow dangerously. Damping keeps the amplitude within a safe limit.

Vibration isolation and transmissibility

The setup

A machine sits on a spring and damper, and the spring-damper unit sits on a floor. The machine has a harmonic force F0, and we want to know how much of this force reaches the floor. The picture is

        machine (mass m)
      ______________
     |              |
     |   force F0   |  cos wt
     |______________|
        |        |
     [ spring k ] [ damper c ]     (in parallel)
        |        |
     ___|________|___
           floor              transmitted force F_T reaches the floor

The transmissibility is the ratio of the force transmitted to the floor to the force applied by the machine:

TR = FT/F0 = √(1 + (2ζr)²) / √((1 - r²)² + (2ζr)²)

If TR is less than 1, the floor sees less force than the machine makes, and the mounting is isolating the vibration.

The key result: the crossover at r = √2

Look at the formula for TR. Compare the squared top and bottom. The top is 1 + (2ζr)². The bottom is (1 - r²)² + (2ζr)². The damping term appears in both, so it cancels in the comparison:

TR less than 1 when (1 - r²)² greater than 1, which means r² greater than 2

So isolation starts when r is greater than √2, whatever the damping ratio is. For r less than √2 the mounting makes the force worse, and all damping curves cross at r = √2 with TR = 1.

Key ideaFor isolation the forcing frequency must be greater than √2 times the natural frequency. A soft mount with a low natural frequency is the usual way to achieve this.

Worked example

A machine runs at 30 Hz and the mount has a natural frequency of 5 Hz. The damping ratio is negligible. The machine's force amplitude is 500 N. Find the transmissibility and the transmitted force.

Step 1. Frequency ratio: r = 30/5 = 6, so r² = 36.

Step 2. Undamped transmissibility: TR = 1/|1 - r²| = 1/35 = 0.02857.

Step 3. Transmitted force: FT = 500 × 0.02857 = 14.3 N.

Step 4. Check: r is greater than √2, so isolation is expected, and TR is below 1. 14.3 N is much less than 500 N.

import math

def TR(r, z):
    num = math.sqrt(1 + (2 * z * r) ** 2)
    den = math.sqrt((1 - r * r) ** 2 + (2 * z * r) ** 2)
    return num / den

print(f"TR undamped r=6: {TR(6, 0.0):.5f}")
print(f"TR zeta=0.1 r=6: {TR(6, 0.1):.4f}")
print(f"TR zeta=0.3 r=6: {TR(6, 0.3):.4f}")
print(f"transmitted force = {500 * TR(6, 0.0):.1f} N")
TR undamped r=6: 0.02857
TR zeta=0.1 r=6: 0.0446
TR zeta=0.3 r=6: 0.1062
transmitted force = 14.3 N

TrapA common mistake is to think more damping always means better protection. At r = 6 the damping of 0.1 already raises TR from 0.0286 to 0.0446. Damping helps near resonance but hurts isolation far above it.

Self-check: at r = 1.2 (below √2) would a stiffer mount improve isolation?

It depends. Below √2 the force is already amplified. A stiffer mount raises the natural frequency and moves the system towards r = 1, which is worse. So a softer mount (lower natural frequency) gives isolation.

Self-check: what is TR exactly at r = √2?

TR = 1, for any damping ratio. This is the crossover point.

Choosing damping for isolation

The trade-off

There are two goals that often conflict. For a machine that runs at a speed close to its natural frequency, we want high damping to keep the peak amplitude small. For a machine that runs well above its natural frequency, we want low damping to keep the transmissibility small. Designers use a compromise, and they choose the natural frequency well below the operating speed so that the machine does not run near resonance in normal use.

Worked example: the effect of damping on the transmitted force

Using the same machine (r = 6), compare the transmitted force for ζ = 0.3 and ζ = 0 (undamped).

Step 1. Undamped TR = 0.02857. Transmitted force = 14.3 N.

Step 2. With ζ = 0.3, TR = 0.1062. Transmitted force = 500 × 0.1062 = 53.1 N.

Step 3. The damping increases the transmitted force by a factor 0.1062/0.02857 = 3.7. This is the cost of heavy damping at high frequency.

GATE loves thisExpect a question that asks for the damping ratio that gives a required transmissibility. Set TR equal to the target and solve for ζ. Work with the squared form to avoid square roots.

Self-check: why is a rubber mount often a good choice for isolation?

Rubber gives a low natural frequency (soft spring) and a small amount of damping. The low natural frequency puts the machine well above resonance, which is where isolation works.

Self-check: what is the main reason to add a damper to a machine that runs at high speed?

To limit the peak during start-up and shut-down, when the machine passes through resonance on the way to its running speed.

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original GATE-style problems for practice, not real past paper questions. Work each one on paper before reading the answer.

Damping ratio of a small system

A mass of 5 kg is on a spring of stiffness 2000 N/m, with a damper of c = 20 N s/m. Find the damping ratio and say whether the system is underdamped.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. ζ and the type of motion.
  2. Which idea applies. ωn = √(k/m), then ζ = c/(2mωn).
  3. Work it out. ωn = √400 = 20 rad/s. 2mωn = 2 × 5 × 20 = 200. ζ = 20/200 = 0.1.
  4. Check. 0.1 is less than 1, so the system is underdamped, which matches the worked example style.

Answer: ζ = 0.1, underdamped.

Classify the damping

A mass of 1 kg is on a spring of stiffness 100 N/m with a damper of c = 20 N s/m. Is the system underdamped, critically damped or overdamped?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The type of damping.
  2. Which idea applies. Compare c with cc = 2mωn.
  3. Work it out. ωn = √100 = 10 rad/s. cc = 2 × 1 × 10 = 20 N s/m. Since c = cc, ζ = 1.
  4. Check. Exactly one: ζ = 1 means critical damping.

Answer: critically damped, ζ = 1.

Which statements are true?

Consider these statements about damped single degree of freedom systems.

(A) With ζ greater than 1, the mass returns to rest without oscillating.
(B) The damped natural frequency is higher than the undamped natural frequency.
(C) For an undamped system the logarithmic decrement is zero.
(D) The transmissibility is below 1 only when the frequency ratio r is greater than √2, for any damping ratio.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true (multiple selection).
  2. Which idea applies. Overdamped motion does not oscillate. ωd = ωn√(1 - ζ²) is lower. The decrement is ln(1) = 0 with no damping. The isolation condition is r² greater than 2.
  3. Work it out. (A) true. (B) false, because ωd is lower. (C) true. (D) true, since TR less than 1 is equivalent to (1 - r²)² greater than 1, which means r² greater than 2.
  4. Check. At r = 6 and ζ = 0.1 the transmissibility is 0.0446, below 1, consistent with (D).

Answer: (A), (C) and (D).

Damping from a measured decay

The free vibration of a structure shows a peak of 20 mm, and after two full cycles the peak is 5 mm. Find the damping ratio to four decimal places (numerical answer).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. ζ to four decimal places.
  2. Which idea applies. δ = (1/n) ln(x0/xn), then ζ = δ/√(4π² + δ²).
  3. Work it out. δ = (1/2) ln 4 = 0.6931. ζ = 0.6931/√(39.478 + 0.4805) = 0.1097.
  4. Check. A damping ratio of about 0.11 is small, so the approximation ζ ≈ δ/(2π) = 0.1103 is close.
import math

delta = math.log(20 / 5) / 2
zeta = delta / math.sqrt(4 * math.pi ** 2 + delta ** 2)
print(f"zeta = {zeta:.4f}")
zeta = 0.1097

Answer: 0.1097.

Transmitted force on a mount

A machine produces a harmonic force of amplitude 500 N at 30 Hz. It is mounted on an undamped isolator with a natural frequency of 5 Hz. Find the transmitted force amplitude in newtons, to one decimal place (numerical answer).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. FT in N.
  2. Which idea applies. r = 30/5 = 6, TR = 1/(r² - 1), FT = TR F0.
  3. Work it out. TR = 1/35 = 0.02857. FT = 500/35 = 14.3 N.
  4. Check. Much less than 500 N, as isolation predicts.

Answer: 14.3 N.

Cost of damping at high frequency

The machine in the previous problem (r = 6, force 500 N) is given a damping ratio of 0.3 instead of zero. By what factor does the transmitted force increase compared with the undamped mount? Give the factor to one decimal place (numerical answer).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The ratio of damped to undamped transmitted force.
  2. Which idea applies. The transmissibility formula with ζ = 0.3.
  3. Work it out. Damped TR = √(1 + 3.6²)/√(35² + 3.6²) = √13.96/√1237.96 = 3.7363/35.1846 = 0.1062. Undamped TR = 0.02857. Factor = 0.1062/0.02857 = 3.7.
  4. Check. A factor above 1 means damping is worse for isolation at this frequency, which is the trap described on this page.
import math

def TR(r, z):
    return math.sqrt(1 + (2 * z * r) ** 2) / math.sqrt((1 - r * r) ** 2 + (2 * z * r) ** 2)

factor = TR(6, 0.3) / TR(6, 0.0)
print(f"factor = {factor:.1f}")
factor = 3.7

Answer: 3.7.

Peak of a damped response

A forced system has damping ratio ζ = 0.1 and static deflection F0/k = 2 mm. Find the frequency ratio r at which the amplitude peaks, and the peak amplitude in millimetres (numerical answer, to two decimal places for the amplitude).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. r at the peak and the peak amplitude.
  2. Which idea applies. rpeak = √(1 - 2ζ²) and Mpeak = 1/(2ζ√(1 - ζ²)).
  3. Work it out. rpeak = √0.98 = 0.9899. Mpeak = 5.0252. Amplitude = 2 × 5.0252 = 10.05 mm.
  4. Check. The peak is close to r = 1 and the amplitude is close to 1/(2ζ) × 2 mm = 10 mm, which agrees.

Answer: r = 0.9899 and peak amplitude 10.05 mm.

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Damping ratio (NAT, 2 marks)

m = 5 kg, k = 2000 N/m, c = 40 N·s/m. Find the damping ratio.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. ζ = c/(2√(km)).
  2. Which idea applies. Critical damping cc = 2√(km).
  3. Work it out. cc = 2 · 100 = 200. ζ = 0.2.
  4. Check. Underdamped, since ζ < 1.

Answer: 0.2.

Exam-level 2: Logarithmic decrement (NAT, 2 marks)

For ζ = 0.2, find the logarithmic decrement, correct to 4 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. δ = 2πζ/√(1 − ζ²).
  2. Which idea applies. Ratio of successive peaks is eδ.
  3. Work it out. 1.2566/0.9798 = 1.2825.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
import math
z = 0.2
print("%.4f" % (2 * math.pi * z / math.sqrt(1 - z * z)))

1.2825

Answer: about 1.2825.

Exam-level 3: Transmissibility (NAT, 2 marks)

An undamped isolator has frequency ratio r = 3. Find the transmissibility.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. T = 1/|1 − r²|.
  2. Which idea applies. Isolation needs r > √2.
  3. Work it out. 1/8 = 0.125.
  4. Check. 87.5% of the force is isolated.

Answer: 0.125.

Remember these

  • ζ = c/(2mωn) is the key number for every damped problem.
  • Use the natural log for the decrement, and divide by the number of cycles.
  • Damping lowers the resonance peak, roughly to 1/(2ζ) of the static deflection.
  • Isolation needs a frequency ratio greater than √2.
  • More damping can make isolation worse at high frequency.
  • Soft mounts with a low natural frequency are the usual route to isolation.

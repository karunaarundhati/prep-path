Bending and shear stresses

When a beam carries a load, it bends, and the top and bottom fibres of the beam feel stress. The flexure formula gives the bending stress, and the shear formula gives the sliding stress inside the beam. Both are core GATE Robotics and Automation mechanics, and this page builds them from a simple picture.

The idea in plain words

Welcome. Put a book across two chairs and sit on the middle of it. The book bends. Look closely and you will see that the top of the book is squeezed and the bottom is stretched. Somewhere in the middle, the paper is neither squeezed nor stretched. This picture is the heart of beam bending, and it is why this page exists. A beam is a long bar that carries loads across its length, such as a shelf, a floor joist or the arm of a robot.

When a beam bends, two things happen inside it. The first is bending stress. It is a normal stress that is tension on one side and compression on the other. The second is shear stress. It is a sliding stress between layers of the beam, which tries to slide one slice past the next. Bending stress is usually the bigger worry for a designer, because it causes the beam to break at the top or bottom surface. Shear stress is smaller in a long, slender beam, but it matters for short, deep beams and for glued joints.

Why does this matter for GATE? The flexure formula and the shear formula appear in almost every GATE mechanics question about beams. A question might give a beam section and a bending moment and ask for the maximum stress, or give a shear force and ask for the shear stress at a height. In GATE Robotics and Automation (RA) papers, the numbers are usually small and the steps are clear once you know the two formulas and the meaning of each symbol.

We will go slowly. First we see where the stress lives in a bent beam, then we learn the flexure formula, then the shear formula, and then we check where the maximum values act. Every formula is followed by a worked example with every step.

Key terms

Read this list now and return to it when a word feels new.

Beam
A long structural member that carries loads across its length. Plain meaning: a bar used as a shelf or a floor support.
Bending moment (M)
The turning effect of the loads on one section of a beam. Plain meaning: how strongly the beam is trying to bend at that point. Unit: N mm or kN m.
Shear force (V)
The sideways force on one section of a beam, which is the net vertical force on one side. Unit: N or kN.
Neutral axis
The line through the cross-section where the bending stress is zero. Plain meaning: the line that is neither stretched nor squeezed.
Bending stress (sigma)
The normal stress caused by bending. Tension on one side, compression on the other.
Flexure formula
The formula sigma = M y/I for bending stress. Plain meaning: the stress grows with the bending moment and with the distance from the neutral axis.
y (distance from the neutral axis)
The distance from the neutral axis to the point where the stress is wanted. Plain meaning: how far from the middle of the beam.
Second moment of area (I)
A property of the cross-section that measures how well the shape resists bending. Plain meaning: how stiff the shape is for bending. Unit: mm4.
Section modulus (Z)
I divided by the largest distance from the neutral axis. Plain meaning: a ready-made number for the strength of a shape in bending. Unit: mm3.
First moment of area (Q)
The area of the part of the cross-section beyond a chosen level, multiplied by the distance from that part's centroid to the neutral axis. Unit: mm3.
Shear stress (tau)
The sliding stress inside the beam. Plain meaning: layers trying to slip past each other.
Shear formula
The formula tau = VQ/(Ib) for shear stress at a level in a beam.
Width (b)
The width of the beam section at the level where the shear stress is wanted.
Simply supported beam
A beam resting on two supports at its ends, free to turn at both.
Cantilever beam
A beam fixed at one end and free at the other.
Uniformly distributed load (UDL)
A load spread evenly along the length, measured in kN/m or N/m.

Why a beam bends and where the stress is

The stretch and the squeeze

Take a rubber ruler, hold it at its two ends, and push the middle down. The top edge gets longer and the bottom edge gets shorter, or the other way round. A short piece of the ruler along the top is stretched, and a piece along the bottom is squeezed. The middle line of the ruler does not change length at all. This middle line is the neutral axis.

Now imagine the cross-section of a beam, a rectangle drawn as a flat shape. The neutral axis runs along the middle, horizontally, in the direction of the beam's length. Points above the neutral axis are in tension or compression, depending on the way the beam bends, and points below it are the opposite. The further a point is from the neutral axis, the bigger its stress. This simple fact is the key to the whole subject.

Cross-section of a beam (side view of a rectangle):

      top fibre:   tension or compression, largest
   +-----------------------------------+
   |                                   |
   |                                   |  <- distance y from the
   |                                   |     neutral axis
   |...................................|  <- neutral axis: stress zero
   |                                   |
   |                                   |
   +-----------------------------------+
      bottom fibre: opposite sign, largest

   Stress grows linearly from zero at the neutral axis
   to its largest value at the top and bottom.

The picture shows the most important result of this topic. The stress is zero on the neutral axis and grows in a straight line to the top and bottom surfaces. The top and bottom fibres (the thin layers at the surfaces) carry the largest bending stress. This is why a beam is deep and not thin when it must resist bending: depth puts the material far from the neutral axis, where it is most useful.

Key ideaBending stress grows with the distance from the neutral axis. It is zero on the neutral axis and largest at the outer surfaces.

What is the bending stress on the neutral axis?

Zero. The neutral axis is the line where the stress changes sign and is zero.

Why is a deep beam stronger in bending than a thin one of the same material?

Depth moves more material far from the neutral axis. Far material carries large stress, so it resists bending more effectively.

The flexure formula: sigma = My/I

Writing the formula

We now turn the picture into a formula. The bending stress at a distance y from the neutral axis is:

sigma = M y / I

Here sigma is the bending stress in N/mm2 (MPa), M is the bending moment in N mm, y is the distance from the neutral axis in mm, and I is the second moment of area of the cross-section in mm4. The formula is called the flexure formula. Every symbol has a plain meaning. M is how strongly the beam is trying to bend at that point. y is how far from the middle you are looking. I is how well the shape of the section resists bending.

Notice that the stress is proportional to y, which matches the straight-line picture from the first section. Notice also that the stress is proportional to M. Double the bending moment and the stress doubles. The formula says the same thing in one line.

The second moment of area for a rectangle

For a rectangle of width b and depth h, with the neutral axis through the middle, the second moment of area is:

I = b h3 / 12

Look at the power 3 on the depth. Doubling the depth multiplies I by 8. This is why depth is so powerful. A beam that is twice as deep is eight times as stiff in bending, if the width is the same.

Worked example

A rectangular beam has width b = 50 mm and depth h = 100 mm. The bending moment at a section is M = 2 kN m = 2 x 106 N mm. Find the bending stress at the top fibre.

  • Step 1: find I. I = b h3/12 = 50 x 1003/12 = 50 x 1000000/12 = 4166666.7 mm4.
  • Step 2: find y at the top fibre. The neutral axis is at the middle, so y = h/2 = 50 mm.
  • Step 3: apply the formula. sigma = M y / I = (2 x 106 x 50)/4166666.7 = 100 x 106/4166666.7 = 24 N/mm2.
  • Step 4: state the answer. The bending stress at the top is 24 MPa in tension or compression, depending on the direction of the bending.

The Python check below repeats these steps and also computes the section modulus, which is the subject of the next section.

b = 50.0
h = 100.0
I = b * h ** 3 / 12
y = h / 2
M = 2e6
print("I =", round(I, 1), "mm^4")
print("sigma =", round(M * y / I, 4), "MPa")
Z = I / y
print("Z =", round(Z, 1), "mm^3")
print("M/Z =", round(M / Z, 4), "MPa")
I = 4166666.7 mm^4
sigma = 24.0 MPa
Z = 83333.3 mm^3
M/Z = 24.0 MPa
A rectangle is 40 mm wide and 60 mm deep. What is I about the middle?

I = b h3/12 = 40 x 603/12 = 40 x 216000/12 = 720000 mm4.

If the depth of a rectangle doubles and the width stays the same, by what factor does I change?

By a factor of 8, because I depends on h cubed.

Section modulus for quick answers

A shortcut for the outer fibre

The largest bending stress occurs at the outer fibre, where y is largest. Engineers therefore combine I and the largest y into one number called the section modulus:

Z = I / ymax
sigmamax = M / Z

The section modulus Z has units of mm3. It is a property of the shape alone. Once Z is known, the largest bending stress is just the moment divided by Z. This saves steps in an exam.

For a rectangle, ymax = h/2, so Z = (b h3/12)/(h/2) = b h2/6. For a solid circle of diameter d, Z = pi d3/32. Learn the rectangle value, because it is the one used most often.

TipIf a question gives a rectangular section and asks for the largest bending stress, compute Z = bh2/6 first, then sigma = M/Z. This is fewer steps and fewer chances to make an arithmetic slip.

What is the section modulus of a rectangle 20 mm wide and 30 mm deep?

Z = b h2/6 = 20 x 900/6 = 3000 mm3.

A section has Z = 60000 mm3 and the moment is 1.2 x 106 N mm. What is the largest bending stress?

sigma = M/Z = 1200000/60000 = 20 N/mm2, which is 20 MPa.

Shear stress in a beam: tau = VQ/(Ib)

Why there is shear in a bending beam

Think of a stack of wooden planks held together only by friction. If you bend the stack, the top plank wants to slide along the one below it. Inside a solid beam, the same sliding happens between thin layers. The sliding is resisted by the material, so there is a shear stress. The shear force V at a section is the total of this sliding, across the whole section.

The shear stress is found from a formula that looks longer than it is:

tau = V Q / (I b)

Here tau is the shear stress at the level where we look, V is the shear force at the section in N, Q is the first moment of area, I is the second moment of area in mm4, and b is the width of the section at that level in mm. The unit of tau is N/mm2, which is MPa.

Understanding Q

Q is the one new quantity. Choose a level in the section at height y from the neutral axis. Look at the part of the section beyond that level, between y and the outer surface. Let its area be A'. Let its centroid (its balance point) be at distance y' from the neutral axis. Then:

Q = A' x y'

Q is the area of the part beyond the level times the distance of its centre from the neutral axis. It measures how much material is trying to slide. Q is largest at the neutral axis and zero at the top surface, because there is no material beyond the top surface.

Worked example with the shear formula

Take a rectangle of width b = 50 mm and depth h = 100 mm, so I = 4166666.7 mm4, as before. The shear force at the section is V = 10 kN = 10000 N. Find the shear stress at the neutral axis.

  • Step 1: choose the level. The neutral axis is at y = 0.
  • Step 2: find A'. The part beyond the neutral axis is the top half, of area 50 x 50 = 2500 mm2.
  • Step 3: find y'. The centroid of the top half is 25 mm above the neutral axis, because it is a half-depth block of depth 50 mm.
  • Step 4: compute Q = A' y' = 2500 x 25 = 62500 mm3.
  • Step 5: use the width at this level. b = 50 mm.
  • Step 6: apply the formula. tau = VQ/(Ib) = 10000 x 62500/(4166666.7 x 50) = 625000000/208333333 = 3.0 N/mm2.

So the shear stress at the neutral axis is 3 MPa. The Python check below uses the same formula at three levels.

b = 50.0
h = 100.0
V = 10000.0
I = b * h ** 3 / 12
def shear_stress(y):
    Q = b * (h / 2 - y) * ((h / 2 + y) / 2)
    return V * Q / (I * b)
print("tau at neutral axis:", round(shear_stress(0), 4), "MPa")
print("tau at y = 25 mm:", round(shear_stress(25), 4), "MPa")
print("tau at top surface:", round(shear_stress(50), 4), "MPa")
tau at neutral axis: 3.0 MPa
tau at y = 25 mm: 2.25 MPa
tau at top surface: 0.0 MPa

TrapUse the width b at the level where you want the stress, not the width at the top. In a T-section or an I-section the width changes with height, and the wrong width gives the wrong answer.

Key ideaShear stress is zero at the top and bottom surfaces and largest at the neutral axis for a simple rectangle. The surface is free to move, so there is no sliding force there.

Why is Q zero at the top surface?

Because there is no area beyond the top surface. The area A' is zero, so Q is zero.

What is Q at the neutral axis of a rectangle of width b and depth h?

The half-section has area bh/2 and its centroid is h/4 from the neutral axis. So Q = (bh/2)(h/4) = bh2/8.

Shear distribution in rectangular and circular sections

The parabola for a rectangle

When you use the shear formula at many levels, a pattern appears. For a rectangle, the shear stress varies as a parabola: zero at the top and bottom surfaces, rising to a maximum at the neutral axis. The formula for a rectangle is:

tau(y) = (1.5 V / (b h)) x (1 - 4y2/h2)

At y = 0 the bracket is 1, so tau = 1.5 V/(bh). Since the area is A = bh, the maximum shear stress is:

tau max = 1.5 V / A (rectangle)

The maximum is 1.5 times the average shear stress V/A. This "1.5" is one of the most useful facts for GATE. Check it with the example: V = 10000 N and A = 5000 mm2, so tau max = 1.5 x 10000/5000 = 3 MPa, which matches the worked example.

GATE loves thisA question may give a rectangle and a shear force and ask for the maximum shear stress. Use tau max = 1.5 V/A. Do not use V/A, which is only the average.

The circle

For a solid circular section of diameter d, the maximum shear stress at the neutral axis is found in the same way, and the factor is different:

tau max = 4 V / (3 A) (solid circle)

Here A = pi d2/4 is the area of the circle. The factor 4/3 is about 1.33, which is smaller than the 1.5 for a rectangle. This is why a circle is slightly more efficient in shear than a rectangle of the same area. The Python check below uses the circle formula for a diameter of 80 mm.

import math
V = 8000.0
d = 80.0
A = math.pi * d ** 2 / 4
tau_max = 4 * V / (3 * A)
print("area =", round(A, 2), "mm^2")
print("tau max =", round(tau_max, 2), "MPa")
area = 5026.55 mm^2
tau max = 2.12 MPa
For a rectangle of area 4000 mm2 with V = 8 kN, what is tau max?

tau max = 1.5 V/A = 1.5 x 8000/4000 = 3 MPa.

Is the shear stress zero at the top of a rectangular beam?

Yes. The formula gives zero at y = h/2, because the bracket (1 - 4y2/h2) is zero there.

Where the maximum stresses act

Along the beam

So far we looked at one section. A beam has a different bending moment and shear force at every section, so the stresses change along the length too. The two places to check are the location of the largest bending moment and the location of the largest shear force.

Take a simply supported beam of span L = 4 m, carrying a uniformly distributed load of w = 2 kN/m. Its reactions at the two ends are wL/2 = 4 kN each. The shear force is largest at the supports, where it is 4 kN. The bending moment is largest at the middle of the span, where it equals wL2/8 = 2 x 16/8 = 4 kN m.

   w = 2 kN/m (spread along the whole span)
   |||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||
   ^                               ^
   A                               B
   |<-----------  L = 4 m  ------>|

   Reaction at A = wL/2 = 4 kN
   Reaction at B = wL/2 = 4 kN

   Max shear force: 4 kN, at the supports
   Max bending moment: wL^2/8 = 4 kN m, at the midspan

Across the section

At the section of maximum bending moment, the bending stress is largest at the top and bottom fibres. At the section of maximum shear force, the shear stress is largest at the neutral axis. For a designer, the critical point is the section where both the moment and the shear are large, together with the outer fibre where the bending stress is largest.

TrapThe largest bending stress and the largest shear stress are not always in the same place. Bending stress is largest at the outer fibre. Shear stress is largest at the neutral axis. Do not mix the two locations.

For a simply supported beam with a central point load, where is the bending moment largest?

At the point of load, which is the middle of the span for a central load.

Where is the shear force largest on a simply supported beam with a UDL?

At the supports, where the reaction equals wL/2.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original, written in the style of GATE. They are not real past questions. Try each one before you read the solution.

Section modulus of a rectangle

A rectangular section is 40 mm wide and 60 mm deep. Find its section modulus about the horizontal neutral axis, in mm3. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Z in mm3.
  2. Which idea applies. Z = b h2/6.
  3. Work it out. Z = 40 x 602/6 = 40 x 3600/6 = 144000/6 = 24000 mm3.
  4. Check. Z = I/ymax = 720000/30 = 24000. Both routes agree.

Answer: 24000 mm3.

Bending stress from Z

A beam section has section modulus Z = 60000 mm3 and carries a bending moment of 1.2 x 106 N mm. Find the largest bending stress in MPa. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The largest bending stress.
  2. Which idea applies. sigmamax = M/Z.
  3. Work it out. sigma = 1200000/60000 = 20 N/mm2, which is 20 MPa.
  4. Check. 20 x 60000 = 1200000, which returns the moment. The check works.

Answer: 20 MPa.

Maximum shear in a rectangle (NAT)

A rectangular beam of width 50 mm and depth 100 mm carries a shear force of 10 kN. Find the maximum shear stress in MPa. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. tau max in MPa.
  2. Which idea applies. For a rectangle, tau max = 1.5 V/A.
  3. Work it out. A = 50 x 100 = 5000 mm2. tau max = 1.5 x 10000/5000 = 3 MPa.
  4. Check. The average shear V/A = 2 MPa, and 1.5 times 2 = 3. The maximum is above the average, as it should be.

Answer: 3 MPa.

Which statements are correct? (MSQ)

For a beam in simple bending, which of the following statements are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) The bending stress is zero on the neutral axis
(B) The bending stress is largest at the top and bottom fibres
(C) For a rectangle, the shear stress is largest at the top surface
(D) For a rectangle, the maximum shear stress is 1.5 times the average shear stress

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Every correct statement.
  2. Which idea applies. The flexure formula, Z, and the shear distribution for a rectangle.
  3. Work it out. (A) is correct, since sigma = My/I is zero when y = 0. (B) is correct, since y is largest at the outer fibres. (C) is wrong, because the shear is zero at the top surface and largest at the neutral axis. (D) is correct, since tau max = 1.5 V/A and V/A is the average.
  4. Check. Three correct statements and one wrong one, and the wrong one is the parabola confusion from the chapter.

Answer: (A), (B) and (D).

Shear in a round shaft (NAT)

A solid circular section of diameter 80 mm carries a shear force of 8 kN. Find the maximum shear stress in MPa, to two decimal places. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. tau max for a solid circle, to two decimals.
  2. Which idea applies. tau max = 4V/(3A), with A = pi d2/4.
  3. Work it out. A = pi x 802/4 = 5026.55 mm2. tau max = 4 x 8000/(3 x 5026.55) = 32000/15079.6 = 2.12 MPa. The Python check below confirms the result.
  4. Check. The circle value 4/3 of the average (8000/5026.55 = 1.59 MPa) is 2.12, which is 4/3 times 1.59. The check works.
import math
V = 8000.0
d = 80.0
A = math.pi * d ** 2 / 4
print("average shear:", round(V / A, 2), "MPa")
print("tau max:", round(4 * V / (3 * A), 2), "MPa")
average shear: 1.59 MPa
tau max: 2.12 MPa

Answer: 2.12 MPa.

Maximum bending stress on a loaded span (NAT)

A simply supported beam of span 4 m carries a uniformly distributed load of 5 kN/m over its full length. Its section is rectangular, 100 mm wide and 200 mm deep. Find the largest bending stress in MPa. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The largest bending stress anywhere in the beam.
  2. Which idea applies. First find Mmax = wL2/8 at midspan. Then sigma = M y/I at the outer fibre, with I = bh3/12.
  3. Work it out. Mmax = 5 x 42/8 = 10 kN m = 107 N mm. I = 100 x 2003/12 = 66666666.7 mm4. y = 100 mm. sigma = 107 x 100/66666666.7 = 15 MPa. The Python check below repeats the steps.
  4. Check. Using Z = bh2/6 = 100 x 40000/6 = 666666.7 mm3 gives 107/666666.7 = 15 MPa, the same answer.
w = 5.0
L = 4.0
M_max = w * L ** 2 / 8 * 1e6
b = 100.0
h = 200.0
I = b * h ** 3 / 12
sigma = M_max * (h / 2) / I
print("M max =", M_max, "N mm")
print("sigma max =", round(sigma, 2), "MPa")
M max = 10000000.0 N mm
sigma max = 15.0 MPa

Answer: 15 MPa.

Shear stress at a height in a rectangle (NAT)

A rectangular section is 50 mm wide and 100 mm deep and carries a shear force of 10 kN. Find the shear stress, in MPa, at a level 25 mm above the neutral axis. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. tau at y = 25 mm.
  2. Which idea applies. tau(y) = (1.5 V/(bh))(1 - 4y2/h2), or the shear formula with Q.
  3. Work it out. 1.5 V/(bh) = 1.5 x 10000/5000 = 3 MPa. The bracket is 1 - 4 x 252/1002 = 1 - 0.25 = 0.75. So tau = 3 x 0.75 = 2.25 MPa.
  4. Check. The chapter's Python check gave 2.25 MPa at y = 25 mm, so the two methods agree.

Answer: 2.25 MPa.

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Bending stress (NAT, 2 marks)

A rectangular beam 100 mm wide and 200 mm deep carries a bending moment of 20 kN·m. Find the maximum bending stress in MPa.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. σ = M/Z.
  2. Which idea applies. Z = bh²/6.
  3. Work it out. Z = 100 · 200²/6 = 666667 mm³. σ = 20e6/666667 = 30 MPa.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
print("%.1f MPa" % (20e6 / (100 * 200 ** 2 / 6)))

30.0 MPa

Answer: 30 MPa.

Exam-level 2: Shear stress in a rectangle (NAT, 2 marks)

The same section carries a shear force of 40 kN. Find the maximum shear stress in MPa.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. τmax = 1.5V/A.
  2. Which idea applies. For a rectangle, the peak shear at the neutral axis is 1.5 times the average.
  3. Work it out. 1.5 · 40000/20000 = 3 MPa.
  4. Check. Average shear is 2 MPa.

Answer: 3 MPa.

Exam-level 3: Circular section (NAT, 2 marks)

A solid circular beam of diameter 60 mm carries 1.5 kN·m. Find the maximum bending stress, correct to 2 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. σ = 32M/(πd³).
  2. Which idea applies. Z = πd³/32.
  3. Work it out. 32 · 1.5e6/(π · 216000) = 70.74 MPa.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
import math
print("%.2f MPa" % (32 * 1.5e6 / (math.pi * 60 ** 3)))

70.74 MPa

Answer: about 70.74 MPa.

Remember these

  1. Bending stress is sigma = My/I. It is zero at the neutral axis and largest at the outer fibres.
  2. Section modulus Z = I/ymax. For a rectangle, Z = bh2/6.
  3. Shear stress is tau = VQ/(Ib). Q is zero at the top surface.
  4. For a rectangle, tau max = 1.5 V/A. For a solid circle, tau max = 4V/(3A).
  5. For a simply supported beam with a UDL, M max = wL2/8 at midspan.
  6. Do not confuse the place of the largest bending stress with the place of the largest shear stress.

Central Limit Theorem

The Central Limit Theorem says that averages of many independent observations behave in a predictable, bell-shaped way, whatever the original data look like. It is the reason z-tests and confidence intervals work, so GATE DA treats it as a core idea.

The idea in plain words

Suppose you want to know the average height of students in a large college. You cannot measure everyone, so you measure a random group of 30 students and compute their average. If you took a different random group, you would get a slightly different average. The average itself is random. It changes from sample to sample.

The Central Limit Theorem, usually shortened to CLT, tells you what the pattern of those sample averages looks like. Take many samples, compute the average of each, and plot them. The plot becomes a bell shape, and this happens even when the individual heights are not bell-shaped. The bell is centred on the true population mean, and it becomes narrower as the sample gets bigger.

This matters because it lets you say how far a sample average is likely to be from the truth. That is the basis of confidence intervals and hypothesis tests, which appear throughout GATE DA.

Key terms

Each entry gives the plain meaning first and the precise definition second.

Population
The whole group you care about. Precise definition: the full set of values, with mean mu and variance sigma squared.
Sample
A part of the population that you actually observe. Precise definition: n observations, usually drawn at random.
Sample mean
The average of the sample. Written x-bar. Precise definition: x-bar = (x1 + x2 + ... + xn) / n.
Random variable
A quantity whose value depends on chance.
i.i.d.
Independent and identically distributed. Each observation is drawn separately from the same distribution.
Sampling distribution
The distribution of a statistic, such as x-bar, over all possible samples of size n.
Standard error (SE)
The standard deviation of a sampling distribution. For the mean, SE = sigma / square root of n.
Normal distribution
The bell-shaped distribution, fully described by its mean and standard deviation.
z-score
How many standard errors a value is from the mean. Precise definition: z = (x-bar - mu) / (sigma / square root of n).
Finite variance
The population variance is a finite number, not infinite.

Sampling distributions: the distribution of a sample mean

A die you can roll by hand

Roll a fair six-sided die. The population is the six faces 1 to 6, each with probability 1/6. The mean is mu = 3.5, and the variance is sigma squared = 35/12, which is about 2.92.

Now roll the die twice and take the average of the two results. There are 36 equally likely pairs. The average can be 1, 1.5, 2, and so on up to 6. The average is not always 3.5. It moves around, but it is pulled toward 3.5 because extreme results cancel out. Only one of the 36 pairs, (1, 1), gives an average of exactly 1, so extreme averages are rare.

The list of all these averages, with their probabilities, is the sampling distribution of the sample mean for n = 2. Its shape is already more bell-like than a single die, which is flat.

The mean of the sample mean is still mu

A key fact: if you average the sample means over all samples, you get the population mean back. We say the sample mean is unbiased. For the die, the average of the two-dice means is 3.5, the same as one die. Sampling does not shift the centre.

Key ideaThe sample mean has the same centre as the population, but a smaller spread. The sampling distribution describes how the sample mean varies from sample to sample.

TrapA sampling distribution is about the statistic, such as the mean, not about the individual observations. Do not mix up the spread of single values with the spread of the sample mean.

Self-check: is the mean of the sample mean equal to the population mean?

Yes. The sample mean is unbiased, so its expected value equals mu, whatever n is.

Self-check: does a larger sample change the centre of the sampling distribution?

No. The centre stays at mu. Only the spread changes, and it gets smaller.

The Central Limit Theorem statement and conditions

The statement

Let X1, X2, ..., Xn be independent observations from the same distribution, with mean mu and finite variance sigma squared. Then, as n grows, the distribution of the sample mean x-bar approaches a normal distribution:

x-bar is approximately Normal(mu, sigma squared / n) for large n

In words: the sample mean is approximately normal, with the same mean as the population and variance equal to the population variance divided by n. The population itself need not be normal.

Why the shape changes

Think of adding many small random effects. A height is shaped by many genes and nutrition factors. A sum or average of many independent pieces tends to lose the peculiar features of each piece, such as skew, and settle into the bell. The more pieces you combine, the closer the result is to normal.

The conditions

  • Independence. The observations should not influence each other. Drawing 30 students at random from a large college satisfies this. Sampling 30 neighbours of one student usually does not.
  • Identical distribution. Every observation should come from the same distribution. Together these two conditions are called i.i.d.
  • Finite variance. The population variance must be a finite number. Some heavy-tailed distributions fail this, and then the CLT can fail too.
  • Large enough n. The approximation is good when n is large. A common rule of thumb is that n of 30 or more is often enough, but very skewed populations may need more.

TrapThe CLT is about the distribution of the sample mean, not the population. Never say the population becomes normal as n grows. The population stays the same shape.

GATE loves thisA conceptual question may ask which condition is needed. The usual answers are independence, identical distribution, and finite variance. Remember that the CLT does not require the population to be normal.

Self-check: a population is strongly skewed. Can the sample mean of 100 observations still be approximately normal?

Yes. The CLT says the sample mean is approximately normal for large n, even when the population is skewed, provided the observations are i.i.d. with finite variance.

Self-check: which condition fails if the 30 observations are taken from the same family but each has a different mean?

Identical distribution fails. The observations come from different distributions, so the simple CLT statement does not apply directly.

Standard error: how the spread of the mean shrinks with n

Spread of the sample mean

The variance of the sample mean is sigma squared divided by n. Its standard deviation is called the standard error:

Var(x-bar) = sigma squared / n, so SE = sigma / square root of n

Symbols: sigma is the population standard deviation, and n is the sample size. The division by n means that averaging reduces random noise.

The die again

For one die, sigma squared = 35/12, so the variance of a single observation is 35/12. For the mean of two dice, the variance is (35/12) / 2 = 35/24. The Python program in this page checks this by counting all 36 pairs, and the result is the same. The standard error for n = 2 is sigma / square root of 2 = 1.7078 / 1.4142 = 1.2076.

Why the square root matters

The standard error falls as the square root of n, not as n. To halve the standard error, you must quadruple the sample size. Doubling n cuts the spread by only a factor of about 1.41. This is a common GATE trap, so keep the square root in mind.

Key ideaStandard error is the standard deviation of the sample mean. It is always smaller than the population standard deviation when n is larger than 1.

TrapDo not divide the population SD by n. The correct divisor is the square root of n.

Self-check: sigma = 12 and n = 36. What is the standard error?

SE = 12 / square root of 36 = 12 / 6 = 2.

Self-check: to reduce the standard error from 2 to 1, how much must n grow?

The standard error must be halved, so n must be multiplied by 4. Doubling n would only reduce SE to about 1.41.

Using the CLT in GATE-style numerical questions

The recipe

Most numerical questions follow the same four steps:

  1. Identify mu, sigma and n from the question.
  2. Compute the standard error SE = sigma / square root of n.
  3. Convert the value of interest to a z-score: z = (x-bar - mu) / SE.
  4. Use the standard normal table, or the normal CDF, to find the probability.

The standard normal probabilities you need most are these: P(Z less than 1) is about 0.8413, so P(Z greater than 1) is about 0.1587. P(Z less than 2) is about 0.9772.

A worked example

A population has mean mu = 50 and standard deviation sigma = 10. A random sample of n = 25 is taken. Find the probability that the sample mean is greater than 52.

Step 1: mu = 50, sigma = 10, n = 25.

Step 2: SE = 10 / square root of 25 = 10 / 5 = 2.

Step 3: z = (52 - 50) / 2 = 1.

Step 4: P(x-bar greater than 52) = P(Z greater than 1) = 1 - 0.8413 = 0.1587.

Notice that we never needed the shape of the population. The CLT justifies using the normal table here, because n = 25 is reasonably large.

TipAlways write SE before z. Students who skip the square root of n get a z-score that is too small, and the probability comes out wrong.

Sample size effect

Using the same population, if n were 100 instead, SE = 10 / 10 = 1. The same gap of 2 would then give z = 2, and P(x-bar greater than 52) = P(Z greater than 2) = 0.0228. A larger sample makes the sample mean more precise, so extreme values become less likely.

Self-check: mu = 80, sigma = 20, n = 16. What is the probability that the sample mean is below 76?

SE = 20 / 4 = 5. z = (76 - 80) / 5 = -0.8. P(Z less than -0.8) = P(Z greater than 0.8) = 1 - 0.7881 = 0.2119.

Self-check: why does the CLT matter for a z-test?

A z-test assumes the sample mean is approximately normal with known sigma. The CLT provides this approximation for large n, even when the population is not normal.

A Python check

This program enumerates all outcomes for the die example, checks that the variance of the sample mean equals sigma squared over n, and computes the normal tail probability for the worked example.

from fractions import Fraction as F
from itertools import product
import math

faces = range(1, 7)
mu = F(sum(faces), 6)
var = sum((F(f) - mu) ** 2 for f in faces) / 6
print("population mean =", mu, "| population variance =", var)

for n in (1, 2):
    outcomes = list(product(faces, repeat=n))
    means = [F(sum(o), n) for o in outcomes]
    m = sum(means) / len(means)
    v = sum((x - m) ** 2 for x in means) / len(means)
    print(f"n={n}: mean of sample mean = {m}, variance = {v}, equals var/n? {v == var / n}")

se = math.sqrt(float(var)) / math.sqrt(2)
print("standard error for n=2 =", round(se, 4))
p = F(sum(1 for o in product(faces, repeat=2) if F(sum(o), 2) <= 2), 36)
print("P(mean of 2 dice <= 2) =", p)

def phi(z):
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2)))

se_example = 10 / math.sqrt(25)
z = (52 - 50) / se_example
print("P(sample mean > 52) =", round(1 - phi(z), 4))

population mean = 7/2 | population variance = 35/12
n=1: mean of sample mean = 7/2, variance = 35/12, equals var/n? True
n=2: mean of sample mean = 7/2, variance = 35/24, equals var/n? True
standard error for n=2 = 1.2076
P(mean of 2 dice <= 2) = 1/6
P(sample mean > 52) = 0.1587

The last line confirms the worked example. The first lines confirm that the variance of the sample mean is sigma squared over n.

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original, GATE-style exercises written for practice. They are not past exam questions. Try each one on paper before reading the answer.

Standard error

The population standard deviation is 15. A sample of size 25 is drawn at random. Find the standard error of the sample mean.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The SD of the sample mean.
  2. Which idea applies. SE = sigma / square root of n.
  3. Work it out. SE = 15 / square root of 25 = 15 / 5 = 3.
  4. Check. The SE is smaller than 15, as it must be for n greater than 1.

Answer: 3

Which statement is correct?

Which of the following is correct about the sample mean of i.i.d. observations with finite variance?

  • (A) It is always exactly normal.
  • (B) Its mean equals the population mean.
  • (C) Its variance equals the population variance.
  • (D) Its variance grows with n.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The correct property of x-bar.
  2. Which idea applies. The CLT gives approximate normality, E[x-bar] = mu, and Var = sigma squared over n.
  3. Work it out. (A) is only approximately normal. (B) is correct. (C) is wrong, since the variance is divided by n. (D) is wrong, since the variance shrinks as n grows.
  4. Check. The die example in this page confirms (B) and the division by n.

Answer: (B)

Probability for a sample mean

A population has mean 100 and standard deviation 16. A sample of size 64 is drawn. Find P(sample mean less than 102). Give the answer to 4 decimal places, using the standard normal table.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A lower-tail probability for x-bar.
  2. Which idea applies. SE, z-score, then the normal CDF.
  3. Work it out. SE = 16 / 8 = 2. z = (102 - 100) / 2 = 1. P(Z less than 1) = 0.8413.
  4. Check. A z of 1 is one standard error above the mean, so the probability should be a bit more than one half, as 0.8413 is.

Answer: 0.8413

Which statements about the CLT are true?

Select all that apply.

  • (A) The CLT requires the population to be normal.
  • (B) The sample mean is approximately normal for large n even when the population is skewed.
  • (C) Doubling n halves the standard error.
  • (D) The mean of the sample mean equals the population mean.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Judge each statement.
  2. Which idea applies. The CLT conditions, the square root of n, and unbiasedness.
  3. Work it out. (A) False: normality of the population is not needed. (B) True. (C) False: doubling n reduces SE by a factor of about 1.41. (D) True.
  4. Check. Compare (C) with the self-check in the standard error section, which asks how much n must grow.

Answer: (B) and (D). Select all that apply.

Sample size needed for a target standard error

The population standard deviation is 24. How large must n be so that the standard error is 3?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The sample size n.
  2. Which idea applies. SE = sigma / square root of n, rearranged.
  3. Work it out. 3 = 24 / square root of n, so square root of n = 8, so n = 64.
  4. Check. SE = 24 / 8 = 3, which matches the target.

Answer: 64

Two-sided probability for a sample mean

A population has mean 40 and standard deviation 8. A sample of size 16 is drawn. Find P(36 less than sample mean less than 44). Give the answer to 4 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A two-sided probability for x-bar.
  2. Which idea applies. SE, then z-scores at both ends, then the symmetry of the normal curve.
  3. Work it out. SE = 8 / 4 = 2. The lower z is (36 - 40) / 2 = -2, and the upper z is (44 - 40) / 2 = 2. P(-2 less than Z less than 2) = 2 times 0.9772 - 1 = 0.9544.
  4. Check. The interval is two standard errors either side of the mean, and the familiar value near 0.95 is correct.

Answer: 0.9544

Effect of sample size on a probability

A population has mean 50 and standard deviation 10. Sample means from size 25 and from size 100 are compared. Which statements are true?

  • (A) P(sample mean greater than 52) is larger for n = 25 than for n = 100.
  • (B) The standard error for n = 100 is 1.
  • (C) The centre of both sampling distributions is 50.
  • (D) For n = 100, P(sample mean greater than 52) is about 0.0228.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Compare the two sampling distributions.
  2. Which idea applies. SE = sigma / square root of n, z-scores, and unbiasedness.
  3. Work it out. For n = 25, SE = 2 and z = 1, so the probability is 0.1587. For n = 100, SE = 1 and z = 2, so the probability is 0.0228. (A) True, since 0.1587 is larger than 0.0228. (B) True. (C) True. (D) True.
  4. Check. A larger sample is more precise, so a value two units above the mean is far less likely to appear in the sample mean.

Answer: (A), (B), (C) and (D). Select all that apply.

Exam-level 1: Sample mean of skewed bulbs (NAT, 2 marks)

A lamp factory tests bulbs. The lifetime of one bulb has mean 1000 hours and standard deviation 200 hours. The lifetimes are strongly skewed, so they are not normal. A quality check takes a random sample of n = 49 independent bulbs and records the sample mean lifetime. Using the Central Limit Theorem and the normal approximation, find the probability that the sample mean is greater than 1020 hours. Give a decimal to 3 places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A tail probability for the sample mean, not for one bulb.
  2. Which idea applies. The Central Limit Theorem: the sample mean is roughly normal with mean 1000 and standard error sigma / square root of n.
  3. Work it out. The standard error is 200 / 7 = 28.57. The z-score is (1020 - 1000) / 28.57 = 0.70. Then P(Z greater than 0.70) = 1 - 0.7580 = 0.242.
  4. Check. The sample mean is far less spread than one bulb (28.57 against 200), so a gap of 20 hours is a modest z-score, and a tail probability near 0.24 is plausible.

Answer: 0.242

import math
def Phi(z):
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2)))
mu, sigma, n = 1000, 200, 49
se = sigma / math.sqrt(n)
z = (1020 - mu) / se
print(f"standard error = {round(se, 4)}")
print(f"z = {round(z, 4)}")
print(f"P(sample mean > 1020) = {round(1 - Phi(z), 3)}")

z = 0.7
P(sample mean > 1020) = 0.242

Exam-level 2: Waiting times of a uniform queue (NAT, 2 marks)

The waiting time for one customer at a counter is uniformly distributed between 0 and 10 minutes. For a uniform distribution from a to b, the variance is (b - a) squared divided by 12, so one waiting time has mean 5 minutes and variance 100 / 12. A random sample of 36 independent customers is taken. Using the normal approximation from the Central Limit Theorem, find the probability that the sample mean lies between 4.5 and 5.5 minutes. Give a decimal to 3 places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The probability that the sample mean falls in a narrow band around the population mean.
  2. Which idea applies. The Central Limit Theorem, with standard error sqrt(variance / n), then a two-sided z-probability.
  3. Work it out. The standard error is square root of (100/12 divided by 36) = 0.4811. The z-score of the band edges is 0.5 / 0.4811 = 1.039. The probability is P(-1.039 less than Z less than 1.039) = 2 times Phi(1.039) - 1 = 0.701.
  4. Check. A band of width 1 covers about 2 standard errors either side of the mean, and 70 percent is a sensible share for that.

Answer: 0.701

import math
def Phi(z):
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2)))
a, b, n = 0, 10, 36
mu = (a + b) / 2
var = (b - a) ** 2 / 12
se = math.sqrt(var / n)
z = 0.5 / se
print(f"mean of one wait = {mu}")
print(f"standard error = {round(se, 4)}")
print(f"P(sample mean between 4.5 and 5.5) = {round(2 * Phi(z) - 1, 3)}")
mean of one wait = 5.0
standard error = 0.4811
P(sample mean between 4.5 and 5.5) = 0.701

Exam-level 3: Which CLT statements hold (MSQ, 2 marks)

Let sigma be the population standard deviation of one observation and let X-bar be the mean of a random sample of size n from that population. Assume the population variance is finite. Select all that apply.

  • (A) For large n, the distribution of X-bar is approximately normal, even when the population is not normal.
  • (B) The standard error of X-bar is sigma divided by n.
  • (C) If n grows from 4 to 100 and sigma stays the same, the standard error falls by a factor of 5.
  • (D) The Central Limit Theorem requires the population itself to be normal.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements about the sampling distribution of the mean are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. The Central Limit Theorem and the standard error formula sigma / square root of n.
  3. Work it out. (A) True: this is the statement of the CLT. (B) False: the standard error is sigma divided by the square root of n, not by n. (C) True: the square root of (100 / 4) = 5, so the standard error goes from sigma / 2 to sigma / 10, a factor of 5. (D) False: the population need not be normal, which is the whole point of the theorem.
  4. Check. The Python block shows the correct standard errors and the wrong formula sigma / n for n = 4 giving 3, which is half the correct 6.

Answer: (A) and (C). Select all that apply.

import math
sigma = 12
for n in (4, 100):
    print(f"n = {n}, SE = {sigma / math.sqrt(n)}")
print(f"wrong formula sigma/n with n = 4 gives {sigma / 4}")
print(f"ratio of correct SEs = {round((sigma / math.sqrt(4)) / (sigma / math.sqrt(100)), 1)}")
n = 4, SE = 6.0
n = 100, SE = 1.2
wrong formula sigma/n with n = 4 gives 3.0
ratio of correct SEs = 5.0

Exam-level 4: Sample size for a margin of error (NAT, 2 marks)

A researcher wants to estimate the mean reaction time of drivers, in milliseconds. Earlier studies give a standard deviation of 15 milliseconds. She wants a 95 percent confidence interval whose margin of error is at most 3 milliseconds, using the normal critical value 1.96. Find the smallest whole number n of drivers that achieves this. Remember that the margin of error uses the standard error, which shrinks with the square root of n.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The smallest sample size n for a given margin of error.
  2. Which idea applies. Margin of error = 1.96 times sigma / square root of n. Solve for n, then round up.
  3. Work it out. We need 1.96 times 15 / square root of n at most 3, so square root of n at least 1.96 times 15 / 3 = 9.8. Then n is at least 9.8 squared = 96.04, so the smallest whole number is n = 97.
  4. Check. With n = 97 the margin is 29.4 / square root of 97 = 2.985, which is at most 3. With n = 96 it is 3.001, which is too big, so 97 is the right answer.

Answer: n = 97

import math
z, sigma, E = 1.96, 15, 3
n_exact = (z * sigma / E) ** 2
n = math.ceil(n_exact)
print(f"exact n = {round(n_exact, 2)}")
print(f"smallest whole n = {n}")
print(f"margin with n = {n} is {round(z * sigma / math.sqrt(n), 3)}")
print(f"margin with n = {n - 1} is {round(z * sigma / math.sqrt(n - 1), 3)}")

smallest whole n = 97
margin with n = 97 is 2.985

Summary

Population
The full group, with mean mu and variance sigma squared.
Sample mean
Average of n observations, written x-bar.
Sampling distribution
Distribution of a statistic over all samples of size n.
i.i.d.
Independent observations from the same distribution.
Central Limit Theorem
For large n, x-bar is approximately normal, whatever the population shape.
Unbiased
E[x-bar] = mu, so the sample mean is centred on the truth.
Standard error
SE = sigma / square root of n, the SD of the sample mean.
z-score for a mean
z = (x-bar - mu) / SE.
Finite variance
A condition the CLT needs.
Standard normal
The normal with mean 0 and SD 1, used for z-tables.
Square root law
Halving SE requires four times the sample size.
Independence
A condition for the CLT. Observations must not influence each other.

Remember these

  1. The CLT is about the sample mean, not the population.
  2. The sample mean is centred on mu.
  3. Var(x-bar) = sigma squared / n, so SE = sigma / square root of n.
  4. Always compute SE before the z-score.
  5. Doubling n does not halve the SE.
  6. The population does not need to be normal.

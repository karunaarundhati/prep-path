Shear force and bending moment diagrams

A beam under loads has two internal forces at every section: the shear force, which tries to slide one side past the other, and the bending moment, which tries to bend the beam. Drawing these as shear force diagrams (SFD) and bending moment diagrams (BMD) is one of the most common GATE Robotics and Automation mechanics skills, and this page builds it step by step from zero.

The idea in plain words

Welcome. Imagine a plank of wood resting on two bricks, with a bag of cement placed in the middle. The plank sags. Now imagine cutting the plank at some point and asking: what is holding the two cut pieces together? The answer is two things. The cut face must carry a sideways force, which stops one piece sliding down past the other. The cut face must also carry a turning effect, which stops the piece bending open. These two internal effects are the shear force and the bending moment.

Here is a simple way to remember them. The shear force is the sideways push across a cut, like the force between two layers of a book that slide. The bending moment is the turning effect across a cut, like the force that makes a diving board bend. Every beam problem asks you to find these two internal effects at every point along the beam, and to draw them. The drawings are called the shear force diagram (SFD) and the bending moment diagram (BMD). Once you have them, you know where the beam is most stressed, and you can design it.

Why does this matter for GATE? Almost every GATE mechanics question about beams begins with an SFD and BMD. The question may ask for the maximum bending moment, the shear force at a point, or the place where the moment is largest. The skill is systematic. Find the reactions, cut the beam, and write the shear and moment as functions of distance. The method is the same every time, so it is worth learning carefully.

We will go slowly. Every step is shown with small numbers, and each rule is explained with a picture or an everyday example first.

Key terms

Read this list now and return to it when a word feels new.

Beam
A long member that carries loads across its length. Plain meaning: a plank or bar used as a shelf or a bridge deck.
Support
A point that holds the beam and pushes back on it. Plain meaning: a wall, a column or a bracket.
Reaction
The upward or sideways force a support gives to the beam. Plain meaning: the push back from the support. Unit: kN.
Simply supported beam
A beam resting on a pin at one end and a roller at the other, free to rotate at both. Plain meaning: a beam sitting on two supports.
Cantilever beam
A beam fixed firmly at one end and free at the other. Plain meaning: a diving board.
Point load
A load acting at one point. Plain meaning: a single weight placed on the beam. Unit: kN.
Uniformly distributed load (UDL)
A load spread evenly along a length. Plain meaning: the same weight per metre all along the beam. Unit: kN/m.
Uniformly varying load (UVL)
A load that grows steadily from zero to a maximum along a length, so the load per metre changes in a straight line. Plain meaning: a load that ramps up like a triangle.
Shear force (V)
The net vertical force on one side of a section. Plain meaning: the sideways push across a cut. Unit: kN.
Bending moment (M)
The turning effect of the forces on one side of a section about that section. Plain meaning: how strongly the beam is bending at that point. Unit: kN m.
Section
An imaginary cut through the beam at one point, used to find V and M there.
Equilibrium
The state where the forces and moments on a body balance, so it does not move. Plain meaning: nothing is pushing the body in one direction more than another.
Sagging moment
A positive bending moment that makes the beam bend into a smile shape, with the bottom in tension. Plain meaning: the beam droops.
Hogging moment
A negative bending moment that makes the beam bend into a frown shape, with the top in tension. Plain meaning: the beam arches up.
Shear force diagram (SFD)
A graph of V against distance along the beam.
Bending moment diagram (BMD)
A graph of M against distance along the beam.

Supports, reactions and equilibrium

Why the reactions come first

Before we can cut a beam, we need to know the forces from the supports. These are the reactions. A beam at rest is in equilibrium, which means two things: the total upward force equals the total downward force, and the total turning effect about any point is zero. These two facts give us two equations, and for a simple beam they are enough to find the reactions.

Take the simple case of a beam of length L with a point load P at distance a from the left end A, and a support at the right end B. Let the reactions be RA at A and RB at B. Taking moments about B (the turning effect about B), the only force with a turning effect is RA and the load P. The turning effect of RA about B is RA times L. The turning effect of P about B is P times (L minus a). For equilibrium these must balance:

RA x L = P x (L - a)

Solve for RA. Then RB comes from the vertical balance, RA + RB = P. A simpler way is to take moments about A to find RB directly, which gives RB x L = P x a, so RB = P a/L.

        P (point load)
        |
        v
   A ___________a_______________ B
   ^ R_A       |<---- L ---->|     ^ R_B
  pin                                roller

   Moments about B:   R_A x L = P x (L - a)
   Moments about A:   R_B x L = P x a
   Vertical balance:  R_A + R_B = P

Key ideaTaking moments about a support removes that support's reaction from the equation. This is why moments about a support give the other reaction at once.

A 6 m beam has a 12 kN point load at 2 m from the left end. What is RB?

RB = P a/L = 12 x 2/6 = 4 kN.

What is the vertical balance for the same beam?

RA + RB = P, so RA = 12 - 4 = 8 kN.

Shear force and bending moment at a section

Cutting the beam

Choose a section at distance x from the left end. Imagine cutting the beam there and throwing away one side. The side that remains must stay in equilibrium, so the cut face must supply the forces that the thrown-away side used to supply. Those forces are the shear force V and the bending moment M.

  • Shear force at the section is the total vertical force on one side of the cut. In the simplest case, it is the sum of all the vertical forces to the left of the cut.
  • Bending moment at the section is the total turning effect of those same forces about the cut. It is the sum of each force times its distance from the cut.

Using the left side of the cut is usually simplest, because it has fewer forces. Using the right side gives the same answer if you keep the sign rule consistent.

Worked example at one section

Use the same beam: length 6 m, point load 12 kN at 2 m from A, and reactions RA = 8 kN and RB = 4 kN. Find V and M at the section x = 1 m, using the left side.

  • Step 1: the only force on the left of the cut is RA = 8 kN upward.
  • Step 2: shear force V = 8 kN upward on the left side.
  • Step 3: moment about the cut is RA times the distance from the cut to A, which is 8 x 1 = 8 kN m.
  • Step 4: this moment bends the left piece in the way that makes the beam sag, so it is a sagging moment, positive.

So at x = 1 m, V = 8 kN and M = 8 kN m. Both are read directly from the reactions, which is typical for sections close to a support.

The sign convention

Signs must be consistent, or the answers will not match. This page uses the most common textbook convention:

  1. The shear force is positive when the left part of the beam has a net upward force on it (the forces to the left point up).
  2. The bending moment is positive when it makes the beam sag, which means the bottom fibre is in tension and the beam bends into a smile.

TrapSome textbooks reverse one or both signs. A correct answer in one convention can look wrong in another. Always state the convention you use in your working, and keep it fixed through the whole problem.

In the convention above, is a sagging moment positive or negative?

Positive. A sagging moment makes the bottom fibre stretch and the beam bend into a smile.

At x = 1 m in the example, which force gives the moment?

The reaction RA = 8 kN, acting upward at the support 1 m to the left of the cut. Its moment is 8 x 1 = 8 kN m.

The link between load, shear and moment

Shear is the slope of the moment

Here is the most useful idea on this page. Moving from one section to the next, the change in shear force comes from the loads, and the change in bending moment comes from the shear. In calculus form:

dV/dx = -w
dM/dx = V

Here dV/dx is the slope of the shear force graph, w is the load per unit length at that point (positive when it acts downward), and dM/dx is the slope of the bending moment graph. You do not need calculus to use these. You only need to know the meaning: the slope of the moment graph is the shear, and the slope of the shear graph is minus the load.

Picture a car on a hill. The height of the hill is like the moment. The steepness of the hill is like the shear. If the hill is steep and going up, the moment is growing quickly. Where the hill flattens out, the shear is zero, and the moment reaches a peak or a valley.

What the rules tell us

  • Where there is no load, the shear is constant, so the SFD is a flat line. The moment changes in a straight line, so the BMD is a sloping line.
  • Where there is a UDL of w per metre, the shear changes in a straight line with slope -w, so the SFD is a sloping line. The moment is a curve (a parabola).
  • At a point load P, the shear jumps by P in the direction of the load. The BMD has a sharp corner (a change of slope) there, but no jump.
  • Where the shear is zero, the moment has a peak or a valley. This is how we find the maximum moment.

Key ideaA point load makes a jump in the SFD and a corner in the BMD. A moment (a couple) applied at a point makes a jump in the BMD. Keep these two rules apart.

If the shear force is zero over a length, what is the bending moment doing there?

It is constant, because its slope dM/dx = V = 0.

What happens to the shear graph where the load is a UDL of 4 kN/m?

It slopes downward with slope -4 kN per metre, so the shear falls by 4 kN for each metre along the UDL.

Drawing SFD and BMD for a simply supported beam

The method in five steps

  1. Find the reactions using moments and the vertical balance.
  2. Mark the points where the loads act, the supports sit and the load changes. Each marked point divides the beam into sections.
  3. Draw the SFD. Start from the left end with the reaction, then step at each point load, and make the line flat where there is no load.
  4. Draw the BMD. The moment starts at zero at a free end. Its slope at each point is the shear from the SFD. Compute the value at each marked point by adding areas, or by using the section method.
  5. Check. The moment must return to zero at the far end of a simply supported beam, and the shear must return to zero after the last reaction.

Worked example: point load on a simply supported beam

A beam of length 6 m is simply supported at its ends. A point load of 12 kN acts at 2 m from the left end A. We already found RA = 8 kN and RB = 4 kN.

The SFD. Just to the right of A, the shear is RA = 8 kN, upward. The shear stays at 8 kN until the load. At the load, the shear jumps down by 12 kN, so just to the right of the load the shear is 8 - 12 = -4 kN. The shear stays at -4 kN until B. At B, the reaction RB = 4 kN jumps the shear back up to 0. The SFD is therefore a flat line at +8 from A to the load, then a flat line at -4 from the load to B, with the final jump back to zero.

SFD (kN)

   +8 |===========|
      |           |
    0 +-----------+-------------------+
      |                              |
   -4 |                              |==========|
      A        2 m (load)              6 m = B

The sketch above is only a rough picture of the step pattern. The numbers are what matter: +8 kN from A to the load, and -4 kN from the load to B.

The BMD. At A the moment is zero, because it is a simple support with no moment. Moving right, the moment grows with slope +8 (the shear). At the load, x = 2 m, the moment is 8 x 2 = 16 kN m. Beyond the load, the shear is -4, so the moment decreases with slope -4. Over the next 4 m it falls by 4 x 4 = 16 kN m, returning to 16 - 16 = 0 at B. This matches the requirement that the moment is zero at a simple support.

L = 6.0
P = 12.0
a = 2.0
RB = P * a / L
RA = P - RB
print("RA =", RA, "kN")
print("RB =", RB, "kN")
def moment(x):
    m = RA * x
    if x > a:
        m -= P * (x - a)
    return m
for x in [0.0, 2.0, 4.0, 6.0]:
    print("M at x =", x, "is", moment(x), "kN m")
print("shear just left of load:", RA, "kN")
print("shear just right of load:", RA - P, "kN")

RB = 4.0 kN
M at x = 0.0 is 0.0 kN m
M at x = 2.0 is 16.0 kN m
M at x = 4.0 is 8.0 kN m
M at x = 6.0 is 0.0 kN m
shear just left of load: 8.0 kN
shear just right of load: -4.0 kN

GATE loves thisThe maximum bending moment for a simply supported beam with one point load occurs under the load, where the shear changes sign. Here that gives Mmax = 16 kN m. The simple formula for a central load, PL/4, is a special case.

Why is the moment zero at the supports of a simply supported beam?

A pin or roller support cannot resist a turning effect, so no moment acts there and the bending moment must be zero.

In the example, what is the moment at x = 4 m, and how did we get it?

M = RA x 4 - 12 x (4 - 2) = 32 - 24 = 8 kN m. The section lies beyond the load, so the load's moment is subtracted.

Cantilever beams and distributed loads

The cantilever

A cantilever is fixed at one end, so the support gives both a vertical reaction and a turning effect, called the fixing moment. There is no other support. This is why cantilevers are used for balconies, diving boards and the arms of many robots. For a cantilever, it is often easiest to work from the free end, where the moment and the shear are both zero. Moving toward the fixed end, the forces add up.

Take a cantilever of length L = 3 m with a UDL of w = 2 kN/m along its full length. Measure x from the free end. At a distance x from the free end, the load on the free side is w x, and it acts at its centre, which is x/2 from the section. So:

V(x) = w x (magnitude, measured from the free end)
M(x) = -w x2/2 (hogging, negative in our convention)

At the fixed end, x = L, so the shear is w L = 6 kN and the moment is -w L2/2 = -2 x 9/2 = -9 kN m. The shear reaction at the wall is 6 kN and the fixing moment is 9 kN m. The beam is hogging, which means the top is in tension and the wall must hold it.

w = 2.0
L = 3.0
def moment_from_free_end(x):
    return -w * x ** 2 / 2
print("moment at fixed end:", moment_from_free_end(L), "kN m")
print("reaction at fixed end:", w * L, "kN")
print("moment at midspan:", moment_from_free_end(L / 2), "kN m")
moment at fixed end: -9.0 kN m
reaction at fixed end: 6.0 kN
moment at midspan: -2.25 kN m

Uniformly varying loads

A uniformly varying load (UVL) is a triangle of load. Its total is the area of the triangle, which is half the base times the peak height. It acts at the centroid of the triangle. For a triangle that is zero at one end and peaks at the other, the centroid is two-thirds of the way along from the zero end. Replace the triangle with one point load equal to its area, placed at that centroid, and then the usual methods apply.

TipFor any distributed load, the total force is the area under the load graph, and it acts at the centroid of that area. Find this total first, then treat it as a point load.

What is the fixed end moment for a cantilever of length 4 m with a point load of 3 kN at the free end?

The moment at the wall is P x L = 3 x 4 = 12 kN m, hogging. Its size is 12 kN m.

A triangular load rises from 0 to 3 kN/m over 6 m. What is its total?

The triangle's area is half of 6 x 3, which is 9 kN.

Finding the maximum bending moment

Where the shear is zero

The largest bending moment occurs where the slope of the moment is zero, which means the shear is zero. On the SFD, look for the point where the graph crosses zero. For a point load on a simply supported beam, the shear changes sign under the load, and that is where the moment is largest. For a UDL on a simply supported beam, the shear is zero at the midspan, and the maximum moment is wL2/8.

For a beam with a load that changes along its length, the shear may cross zero at a point that is not a marked point. You then find where the shear is zero by solving V(x) = 0, and substitute that x into M(x). The next example shows this.

Worked example: triangular load on a simply supported beam

A beam of length 6 m is simply supported at its ends. Its load rises steadily from 0 at the left end to 3 kN/m at the right end. The total load is W = 3 x 6/2 = 9 kN, and its centroid is at 4 m from the left end. Moments about B give RA x 6 = 9 x 2, so RA = 3 kN.

At distance x from A, the load per metre is w(x) = 3x/6 = x/2. The shear is V(x) = 3 - x2/4, so it is zero at x2 = 12, which gives x = 3.46 m. The moment there is M = 3x - x3/12. Substituting x = 3.46 gives M = 10.39 - 3.46 = 6.93 kN m. The Python check below scans the beam to confirm this.

L = 6.0
w0 = 3.0
W = w0 * L / 2
RA = W / 3
n = 60000
best_x, best_M = 0.0, -1.0
for i in range(n + 1):
    x = L * i / n
    M = RA * x - w0 * x ** 3 / (6 * L)
    if M > best_M:
        best_M, best_x = M, x
print("total load:", W, "kN")
print("RA:", RA, "kN")
print("position of max moment:", round(best_x, 2), "m")
print("max moment:", round(best_M, 2), "kN m")
total load: 9.0 kN
RA: 3.0 kN
position of max moment: 3.46 m
max moment: 6.93 kN m

TrapThe maximum moment is not always at the midspan or under a point load. Whenever the load varies along the beam, find the point where the shear is zero, and only then compute the moment.

For a simply supported beam with a UDL of w over span L, where is the maximum moment and what is its value?

At the midspan, where the shear is zero. The value is wL2/8.

Why is the moment at the free end of a cantilever zero?

Because there is nothing on the free side of the section at the free end, so the moment about the section is zero.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original, written in the style of GATE. They are not real past questions. Try each one before you read the solution.

Reactions for a central load

A simply supported beam of span 4 m carries a point load of 10 kN at its midspan. Find the reaction at the left support in kN. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. RA in kN.
  2. Which idea applies. A central load is shared equally between the two supports.
  3. Work it out. Moments about B: RA x 4 = 10 x 2, so RA = 5 kN.
  4. Check. RB is also 5 kN, and 5 + 5 = 10 kN, which balances the load.

Answer: 5 kN.

Fixing moment of a cantilever

A cantilever of length 2 m carries a point load of 5 kN at its free end. Find the size (magnitude) of the bending moment at the fixed end, in kN m. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The size of the fixing moment in kN m.
  2. Which idea applies. The moment at the wall is the load times the length, P x L.
  3. Work it out. 5 x 2 = 10 kN m.
  4. Check. The shear at the wall is 5 kN, and the moment grows linearly from zero at the free end, so the average slope over 2 m is 5 kN, giving 10 kN m. The check works.

Answer: 10 kN m.

Maximum moment of a UDL (NAT)

A simply supported beam of span 4 m carries a uniformly distributed load of 2 kN/m over its full length. Find the maximum bending moment in kN m. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Mmax in kN m.
  2. Which idea applies. The shear is zero at midspan, and Mmax = wL2/8.
  3. Work it out. Mmax = 2 x 42/8 = 2 x 16/8 = 4 kN m.
  4. Check. The total load is 8 kN, and RA = 4 kN. The moment at midspan is RA x 2 minus the load on the left half times its centroid distance: 4 x 2 - (4 kN x 1 m) = 4 kN m. The two routes agree.

Answer: 4 kN m.

Which statements are correct? (MSQ)

For a beam in equilibrium, with the sign convention used on this page, which of the following statements are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) The bending moment is largest where the shear force is zero
(B) A point load makes a jump in the bending moment diagram
(C) A uniformly distributed load makes the shear force diagram a sloping straight line
(D) The change in bending moment between two sections equals the area under the shear force diagram between them

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Every correct statement.
  2. Which idea applies. dM/dx = V and dV/dx = -w, and what a point load does to each diagram.
  3. Work it out. (A) is correct, since the slope of M is V, which is zero at a peak. (B) is wrong: a point load makes a jump in the SFD and a corner in the BMD. (C) is correct, since the SFD slope is -w. (D) is correct, since the integral of V is the change in M.
  4. Check. Three correct statements. The wrong one swaps the effect of a point load on the two diagrams.

Answer: (A), (C) and (D).

Reaction at the support (NAT)

A simply supported beam of span 8 m carries a point load of 20 kN at a distance of 2 m from the left support. Find the reaction at the right support in kN. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. RB in kN.
  2. Which idea applies. Moments about the left support A give RB x L = P x a.
  3. Work it out. RB x 8 = 20 x 2 = 40, so RB = 5 kN.
  4. Check. RA = 20 - 5 = 15 kN. The load is nearer the left support, so the left reaction should be the larger one, and it is.

Answer: 5 kN.

Maximum moment for a triangular load (NAT)

A simply supported beam of span 6 m carries a load that rises uniformly from 0 at the left support to 3 kN/m at the right support. Find the maximum bending moment in kN m, to two decimal places. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Mmax in kN m, to two decimals.
  2. Which idea applies. Find RA from the total load and its centroid. Then find where V = 0 and substitute into M(x).
  3. Work it out. The total load is 9 kN, acting at 4 m from A. RA = 3 kN. The shear V(x) = 3 - x2/4 is zero at x = sqrt(12) = 3.46 m. The moment is M = 3x - x3/12, which gives 6.93 kN m. The Python check in the chapter scans the beam and gives the same answer.
  4. Check. The exact value is 4 times the square root of 3, which is 6.928, and this rounds to 6.93.

Answer: 6.93 kN m.

Which statements are correct for a central point load? (MSQ)

A simply supported beam of span L carries a point load P at midspan. Which of the following statements are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) The shear force jumps by P at midspan
(B) The maximum bending moment is PL/4
(C) The shear force is zero at midspan
(D) The bending moment at each support is equal to PL/2

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Every correct statement.
  2. Which idea applies. Each reaction is P/2. The shear is P/2 on the left and -P/2 on the right of the load. The moment at the load is (P/2)(L/2) = PL/4.
  3. Work it out. (A) is correct: the shear changes from P/2 to -P/2, a jump of P. (B) is correct: the moment at midspan is PL/4. (C) is wrong: the shear is P/2 just left of midspan and -P/2 just right of it, so it is not zero there. (D) is wrong: the moment at a simple support is always zero.
  4. Check. The jump P is the sum of the two shears (P/2 + P/2), which matches statement (A).

Answer: (A) and (B).

Exam-level 1: Maximum moment, point load (NAT, 2 marks)

A simply supported beam of span 8 m carries 20 kN at 3 m from the left. Find the maximum bending moment.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Mmax = Pab/L.
  2. Which idea applies. The maximum is under the load.
  3. Work it out. 20 · 3 · 5/8 = 37.5 kN·m.
  4. Check. RA = 12.5 kN and 12.5 · 3 = 37.5.

Answer: 37.5 kN·m.

Exam-level 2: UDL and point of zero shear (NAT, 2 marks)

A simply supported beam of span 6 m carries a UDL of 10 kN/m over the left 4 m only. Find the location of maximum moment from the left support and the moment there.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. x where V = 0 and M there.
  2. Which idea applies. RA from moments. Then V(x) = RA − 10x.
  3. Work it out. RB · 6 = 40 · 2, RB = 13.33, RA = 26.67. V = 0 at x = 2.667 m. M = 26.67 · 2.667 − 5 · 2.667² = 35.56 kN·m.
  4. Check. The code scans the beam.
RA = 40 - 40 * 2 / 6

  2. Keep one sign convention through the whole problem and state it.
  3. The slope of the moment diagram is the shear force.
  4. The slope of the shear diagram is minus the load.
  5. The maximum moment is where the shear is zero.
  6. A point load causes a jump in the SFD, not in the BMD.

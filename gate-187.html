Fatigue strength and the S-N diagram

Many machine parts fail not from one big load but from many small loads repeated over and over. This is fatigue. The S-N diagram is the picture engineers use to predict how many cycles a part survives, and it is a favourite topic for GATE Robotics and Automation numericals.

The idea in plain words

Welcome. Here is a puzzle that surprised engineers for a long time. Take a steel paper clip and bend it gently back and forth. It does not break at once. After a while, though, it snaps. The force was small, and each bend was small. But the bending was repeated many times, and the damage added up. This slow, repeated damage is called fatigue.

Think of a bridge with trucks crossing it every day, or a shaft in a motor that turns thousands of times a minute. Each load is far below the strength the material shows in a single pull test. Yet after millions of cycles, a tiny crack can start at a rough spot, grow a little with each cycle, and finally break the part. Fatigue is responsible for a very large share of real machine failures, which is why it matters so much.

The tool engineers use to predict fatigue is the S-N diagram. It is a graph of stress against life. "S" is the stress amplitude, and "N" is the number of cycles to failure. Once you can read this graph, you can answer questions such as "how many cycles will this shaft last?" or "what stress is safe for ten million cycles?"

Why does this matter for GATE? Machine design questions in the Robotics and Automation paper often give an S-N curve or two points on it, then ask for the life at a given stress, or the endurance limit after some correction factors. The arithmetic is simple, but the ideas must be clear. This page builds them one at a time, starting from zero.

Key terms

Read this list now, and come back to it when a word feels unfamiliar.

Cyclic loading
A load that rises and falls repeatedly over time. Precisely: a stress that varies periodically, such as a rotating shaft under a steady bending load.
Fatigue
Damage and eventual failure under repeated loads, even when each load is below the strength of the material. Precisely: progressive cracking under cyclic stress.
Stress amplitude (S or sigmaa)
Half of the difference between the maximum and minimum stress in a cycle. Precisely: the alternating part of the stress, in MPa.
Cycle
One full rise and fall of the stress, from start to back to start. Precisely: one complete period of the loading.
Number of cycles to failure (N)
How many cycles a part survives at a given stress amplitude before it breaks. Precisely: the life of the part, a number, often written N.
S-N diagram (Wohler curve)
A graph of stress amplitude against number of cycles to failure, usually on log-log axes. Precisely: a plot that shows fatigue strength for each life.
Endurance limit (Se)
The stress amplitude below which a steel can, in principle, survive without fatigue failure. Precisely: the stress level on the S-N curve that the material reaches for very large N, usually taken at about 106 cycles.
Fatigue strength
The stress amplitude a material can take for a stated number of cycles. Precisely: the value of S on the S-N curve at a given N.
Modifying factor (Marin factor)
A number, less than or equal to 1 in most cases, that corrects the endurance limit for a real part. Precisely: one of several factors for surface, size, load, temperature and reliability.
Surface factor ka
A correction for surface finish. Precisely: the ratio of the endurance limit with the actual finish to the value for a polished specimen.
Size factor kb
A correction for the diameter of a round part. Precisely: the ratio of the endurance limit of a part of diameter d to that of a small test specimen.
Load factor kc
A correction for the type of loading. Precisely: a factor that is 1 for bending, about 0.85 for axial loading, and about 0.59 for torsion in a common textbook form.
Low-cycle fatigue
Fatigue at high stress and few cycles, roughly below 103 cycles. Precisely: failure with significant plastic (permanent) strain in each cycle.
High-cycle fatigue
Fatigue at lower stress and many cycles, roughly above 103 cycles. Precisely: failure where the stress stays mostly elastic.
Log-log plot
A graph where both axes are logarithmic, so that a power law appears as a straight line. Precisely: a plot of log S against log N.

The S-N diagram: stress against number of cycles

To build an S-N diagram, an engineer takes a standard test piece and fixes it in a machine that bends or pulls it back and forth. The machine is set to a stress amplitude, say 400 MPa, and it runs until the piece breaks. The number of cycles is counted. The test is repeated at several stress amplitudes, and the points are plotted as S against N.

Look at the shape of the result. At high stress, the piece breaks after only a few hundred cycles. At lower stress, it lasts many more cycles. Lower still, it may last millions of cycles. Because N changes over many powers of ten, both axes are drawn on a logarithmic scale. A typical S-N curve for steel then looks like a sloping line that bends flat at around one million cycles.

  stress S (MPa)
    ^
800 |  x
    |     x
400 |        x
    |           x  x
200 |_______________________ x   (flat part: endurance limit)
    +---+------+-------+------+--------> cycles N (log scale)
        10^3   10^4    10^5   10^6

The picture is a sketch, not real data. The sloping part shows that more stress means less life. The flat part shows the endurance limit.

A useful way to think about the S-N line is that every doubling of stress cuts life sharply. Engineers use a straight line on log-log paper because a power law, S = a times Nb, becomes a straight line when both sides are logged. Write it as log S = log a + b log N, which is a line with slope b.

Key ideaThe S-N curve is a picture of a trade. Higher stress buys a shorter life. Lower stress buys a longer life, and below the endurance limit the life is, in principle, unlimited for steel.

Why are both axes of an S-N diagram usually logarithmic?

Because the number of cycles to failure can range from a few hundred to many millions, which spans several powers of ten. A log scale shows all of that range on one page, and it turns the sloping part into a straight line.

What does a flat part of the S-N curve mean?

It means the stress has reached the endurance limit. Below this stress the material is expected to survive a very large number of cycles without failing by fatigue, which is the case for many steels.

Endurance limit for steel and no endurance limit for non-ferrous metals

Steels are special. For many steels, the S-N curve flattens out near 106 cycles, and the stress at that flat part is the endurance limit, written Se. In the textbook estimate, the unmodified endurance limit of a steel is about half its ultimate tensile strength.

Se' = 0.5 Sut, for steel with Sut up to about 1400 MPa

Here Se' is the endurance limit of a small polished test specimen, before any correction. Sut is the ultimate tensile strength. The prime symbol means "for the test specimen, not for a real part".

Worked example. A steel has Sut = 600 MPa.

  1. Write the rule: Se' = 0.5 times Sut.
  2. Substitute: Se' = 0.5 times 600 = 300 MPa.
  3. So a polished test specimen of this steel can survive stress amplitudes up to 300 MPa for very many cycles.

Non-ferrous metals, such as aluminium alloys and copper alloys, do not usually show a flat part. Their S-N curves keep sloping downward, and they never clearly level off. For these metals, engineers quote a fatigue strength at a stated large number of cycles, such as 108 or 5 times 108 cycles. This is a chosen point on the curve, not a true limit.

TrapDo not assume every metal has an endurance limit. Steel usually does, and aluminium usually does not. If a GATE question says "aluminium" and asks for the endurance limit, look for a stated fatigue strength at a given number of cycles instead.

GATE loves thisThe rule Se' = 0.5 Sut applies to steels with Sut up to about 1400 MPa. Above that strength, the endurance limit is usually taken as a fixed value, not as half the tensile strength. Read the question for the given data.

A non-ferrous alloy has no clear endurance limit. What does the designer use instead?

A fatigue strength at a stated large number of cycles, read from the S-N curve. The designer chooses a life, such as 108 cycles, and uses the stress at that life.

If Sut = 800 MPa for a steel, what is Se'?

Se' = 0.5 times 800 = 400 MPa, provided 800 MPa is within the range of the rule (up to about 1400 MPa).

Modifying factors: surface, size and load

A test specimen is small, smooth and polished. A real part is different. It may be large, rough from machining, and loaded in a different way. Each difference reduces the real endurance limit. Engineers use modifying factors, usually called Marin factors, to correct the specimen value.

Se = ka times kb times kc times kd times ke times kf times Se'

Each factor is a number, usually at most 1. Here ka is the surface factor, kb is the size factor, kc is the load factor, kd is the temperature factor, ke is the reliability factor, and kf is a miscellaneous factor. In most GATE numericals only the first three are needed, and the others are given or equal to 1.

The three factors used most often are as follows.

  • Surface factor ka. A rough surface has small scratches and machining marks. Cracks start at those marks, so the endurance limit drops. A common textbook form for a machined surface is ka = 4.51 times Sut to the power -0.265, with Sut in MPa. A polished surface gives a value close to 1.
  • Size factor kb. A bigger round part has more material at high stress, so the chance of a crack in the highly stressed zone is greater. For bending and torsion of round sections, a common textbook form for diameters from about 2.8 mm to 51 mm is kb = 1.24 times d to the power -0.107, with d in mm.
  • Load factor kc. Bending is the reference, so kc = 1 for bending. For axial loading a common value is 0.85, and for torsion about 0.59.

Worked example. A machined steel part has Sut = 500 MPa, diameter d = 20 mm, and is loaded in bending. Find the modified endurance limit.

  1. Specimen value: Se' = 0.5 times 500 = 250 MPa.
  2. Surface factor: ka = 4.51 times 500 to the power -0.265 = 0.869.
  3. Size factor: kb = 1.24 times 20 to the power -0.107 = 0.900.
  4. Load factor for bending: kc = 1.
  5. Multiply: Se = 0.869 times 0.900 times 1 times 250 = 195.5 MPa.

So the real part can take about 195 MPa for very many cycles, which is much less than the specimen value of 250 MPa. The Python block below repeats this working, and it is also used later to estimate life.

TrapApply the factors to the specimen endurance limit Se', not to the tensile strength Sut. A common mistake is to multiply Sut by the factors directly.

Key ideaEvery factor is a penalty. A rough surface, a large diameter, or an axial load each lowers the endurance limit. The product of the factors tells you how far the real part falls below the ideal specimen.

Why does a larger diameter lower the endurance limit?

A larger part has more highly stressed material near its surface, so there is a greater chance that a crack starts somewhere. The size factor is less than 1 for larger d.

Why is the load factor for axial loading less than for bending?

In a bending test the stress is highest only at the outer surface, so the material near the surface carries the peak stress and the rest is less stressed. In axial loading the whole section carries the same stress, so a flaw anywhere can start a crack. The factor 0.85 reflects that difference.

Low-cycle and high-cycle fatigue

Not all fatigue looks the same. Consider two cases. In the first, a press forging is squeezed hard, a few hundred times a day, and each squeeze makes the metal yield a little. This is low-cycle fatigue. The stress is high, the cycle count is low, and each cycle leaves a small permanent deformation.

In the second, a motor shaft spins at 3000 revolutions per minute for years. The stress each turn is well below the yield strength, and the material stays elastic. The cycle count runs into millions. This is high-cycle fatigue, and it is the situation the S-N diagram with an endurance limit describes.

FeatureLow-cycle fatigueHigh-cycle fatigue
Rough number of cyclesBelow about 103Above about 103, often up to 106 or more
Stress levelHigh, often near yieldLower, mostly elastic
Plastic strain per cycleSignificantNegligible
Typical methodStrain-life curvesS-N curves with endurance limit
ExampleForging press, earthquake loadingRotating shaft, vibrating spring

The boundary between the two is not sharp, and textbooks draw it differently. In GATE questions, the S-N curve with an endurance limit is the usual tool for high-cycle work. For low-cycle work the question will give you what you need.

TipWhen a question mentions a few hundred cycles, think low-cycle first. When it mentions millions of cycles, think high-cycle, and use the S-N curve.

A press cycles 500 times in its life and yields each time. Which type of fatigue applies?

Low-cycle fatigue. The cycle count is low, and each cycle causes permanent strain.

What is the typical number of cycles that marks the start of the flat part of a steel S-N curve?

About 106 cycles. This is where the endurance limit is usually read.

Estimating life from an S-N line

Now the most useful GATE skill: finding the life N from a given stress, using two points on the S-N line. Suppose the line passes through two known points, (N1, S1) and (N2, S2). On log-log paper, the line is straight, so we can find its slope and then the life at any stress.

Slope b = log10(S2/S1) / log10(N2/N1)
Life at a stress S: N = N1 times (S/S1) to the power (1/b)

Here b is the slope of the line on log-log paper, which is negative for an S-N curve, and N is the number of cycles. The second formula comes from S = S1 (N/N1)b, rearranged to find N.

Worked example. The S-N line passes through (103, 450 MPa) and (106, 195.5 MPa), taking the modified endurance limit from the previous section. Find the life at 300 MPa.

  1. Ratio of stresses: S2/S1 = 195.5/450 = 0.4344.
  2. Ratio of lives: N2/N1 = 106/103 = 1000, so log10 = 3.
  3. Slope: b = log10(0.4344)/3 = -0.3621/3 = -0.1207.
  4. Life at 300 MPa: N = 1000 times (300/450) to the power (1/-0.1207). The ratio 300/450 is 0.6667, and 1/-0.1207 is about -8.285.
  5. Compute: 0.6667 to the power -8.285 is about 28.8, so N = 1000 times 28.8 = 28800 cycles.

A life of about 28800 cycles at 300 MPa is shorter than the endurance limit would suggest, which is what we expect: a stress above the endurance limit causes failure in finite life.

TrapCheck the sign of the slope. The slope of an S-N line is negative, because higher stress gives fewer cycles. If your b comes out positive, you have swapped the two points or the order of the ratio.

GATE loves thisWhen the S-N curve is given as a straight line on log-log paper with two marked points, use the slope formula. Do not interpolate on a linear scale, because that gives wrong answers.

On log-log axes, what shape does a power law S = a Nb take?

A straight line with slope b. Taking logs of both sides gives log S = log a + b log N.

If the stress amplitude is above the endurance limit, is the life finite or infinite?

Finite. Above the endurance limit, the S-N curve is still sloping down, so the part fails after a certain number of cycles.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original, written in the GATE style, and they are not copied from any real exam paper. Work through the "How to think about it" steps yourself before reading the answer.

Endurance limit of a steel bar

A steel has an ultimate tensile strength of Sut = 600 MPa. Using the usual textbook estimate for steel, what is the endurance limit Se' of the polished specimen, in MPa? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The endurance limit of the specimen, before any factors.
  2. Which idea applies. For steel, Se' = 0.5 Sut, provided Sut is at most about 1400 MPa.
  3. Work it out. 600 is below 1400, so the rule applies. Se' = 0.5 times 600 = 300 MPa.
  4. Check. The endurance limit must be less than the tensile strength. 300 is less than 600, so the answer is sensible.

Answer: 300 MPa.

Which metals have an endurance limit?

Which of the following statements about the S-N diagram are correct? Select all that apply. (MSQ)

  • (A) Many steels show a flat part of the S-N curve, which gives an endurance limit.
  • (B) Aluminium alloys always show a clear endurance limit at about 106 cycles.
  • (C) A rougher surface finish gives a smaller surface factor ka.
  • (D) The size factor kb rises as the diameter of a round part increases.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true. More than one may be correct.
  2. Which idea applies. The endurance limit belongs to steels, the surface factor falls with roughness, and the size factor falls as the diameter grows.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) False: non-ferrous alloys usually have no clear endurance limit. (C) True: roughness lowers ka. (D) False: kb = 1.24 d to the power -0.107 falls as d grows.
  4. Check. The size factor formula has a negative power, so a bigger d gives a smaller kb. This confirms (D) is wrong.

Answer: (A) and (C) are correct.

Stress at a given life

The S-N line of a material is straight on log-log axes. It passes through (103 cycles, 450 MPa) and (106 cycles, 225 MPa). What stress amplitude, in MPa, gives a life of 105 cycles? Give the answer to one decimal place. (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The stress S at N = 105, on the line through the two given points.
  2. Which idea applies. Use the slope b on log-log paper, then S = 450 times (N/103)b.
  3. Work it out. Slope: b = log10(225/450)/log10(103) = log10(0.5)/3 = -0.30103/3 = -0.10034. Then N/103 = 102 = 100. So S = 450 times 100 to the power -0.10034 = 450 times 0.6300 = 283.5 MPa.
  4. Check. 105 cycles lies between 103 and 106, so the answer must lie between 225 and 450. The value 283.5 does.

Answer: 283.5 MPa.

Life at a given stress

Using the same S-N line as the previous problem, through (103, 450 MPa) and (106, 225 MPa), how many cycles will the material last at a stress amplitude of 300 MPa? Give the answer to the nearest whole number of cycles. (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The life N at S = 300 MPa.
  2. Which idea applies. N = N1 (S/S1)1/b, with b = -0.10034 from the previous problem.
  3. Work it out. S/S1 = 300/450 = 0.6667. Then 1/b = 1/(-0.10034) = -9.966. So N = 1000 times 0.6667 to the power -9.966. The power of 0.6667 is about 56.9, so N is about 56900 cycles.
  4. Check. 300 MPa is below 450 MPa, so the life must be longer than 103 cycles. The answer 56900 is larger than 1000, as it should be.

Answer: about 56900 cycles.

Statements about fatigue

Which of the following statements about fatigue are correct? Select all that apply. (MSQ)

  • (A) Fatigue failure can happen below the ultimate tensile strength of the material.
  • (B) Low-cycle fatigue involves significant permanent strain in each cycle.
  • (C) The Marin factors always increase the endurance limit above the specimen value.
  • (D) Fatigue damage is a slow growth of cracks under repeated stress.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true. More than one may be correct.
  2. Which idea applies. Fatigue happens under repeated loads below strength. Low-cycle fatigue has plastic strain. Marin factors are usually at most 1.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) True. (C) False: the factors are usually at most 1, so they lower the endurance limit. (D) True: cracks grow a little on each cycle.
  4. Check. Factors such as ka and kb are below 1 for a real part, so they cannot raise the limit, which rules out (C).

Answer: (A), (B) and (D) are correct.

Modified endurance limit of a shaft

A machined steel shaft has Sut = 700 MPa and diameter d = 30 mm. It is loaded in axial tension and compression. Use ka = 4.51 Sut to the power -0.265, kb = 1.24 d to the power -0.107 and kc = 0.85. Find the modified endurance limit Se, in MPa, to one decimal place. (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The real endurance limit of the shaft after all three corrections.
  2. Which idea applies. First the specimen value, then multiply by ka, kb and kc.
  3. Work it out. Se' = 0.5 times 700 = 350 MPa. ka = 4.51 times 700 to the power -0.265 = 0.795. kb = 1.24 times 30 to the power -0.107 = 0.862. Then Se = 0.795 times 0.862 times 0.85 times 350 = 203.7 MPa.
  4. Check. Each factor is below 1, so the answer must be below 350 MPa. It is, and it is about 58 percent of the specimen value, which is in a sensible range.

Answer: 203.7 MPa.

Stress for a life of 105 cycles after correction

The S-N line of a steel passes through (103 cycles, 450 MPa) and through the modified endurance limit 195.5 MPa at 106 cycles. What stress amplitude gives a life of 105 cycles, in MPa, to one decimal place? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The stress at 105 cycles on the corrected S-N line.
  2. Which idea applies. Find the slope from the two points, then use S = 450 times (N/103) to the power b.
  3. Work it out. b = log10(195.5/450)/3 = log10(0.4344)/3 = -0.3621/3 = -0.1207. Then (105/103) = 100, and 100 to the power -0.1207 = 0.5736. So S = 450 times 0.5736 = 258.1 MPa.
  4. Check. The answer lies between 195.5 and 450, as it must for a life between 103 and 106 cycles.

Answer: 258.1 MPa.

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Basquin equation (NAT, 2 marks)

An S-N line passes through 0.9Sut at 10³ cycles and Se at 10⁶ cycles. Sut = 600 MPa and Se = 300 MPa. Find the fatigue strength at 10⁵ cycles, correct to 1 decimal place.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Log-log interpolation.
  2. Which idea applies. log S is linear in log N.
  3. Work it out. S(10³) = 540. Slope per decade = log(300/540)/3. At 10⁵ (2 decades): 540 · (300/540)2/3 = 364.9 MPa.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
print("%.1f" % (540 * (300 / 540) ** (2 / 3)))

364.9

Answer: about 364.9 MPa.

Exam-level 2: Miner's rule (NAT, 2 marks)

A part sees 10⁴ cycles at a level with life 5 × 10⁴, and 2 × 10⁵ cycles at a level with life 10⁶. What fraction of life is used?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Σ n/N.
  2. Which idea applies. Palmgren-Miner linear damage.
  3. Work it out. 0.2 + 0.2 = 0.4.
  4. Check. Failure is predicted at 1.

Answer: 0.4.

Exam-level 3: Fatigue facts (MSQ, 2 marks)

Which are TRUE? (A) Steels usually show an endurance limit. (B) Aluminium alloys usually lack a clear endurance limit. (C) Tensile mean stress lowers fatigue strength. (D) Shot peening lowers fatigue life.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Fatigue facts.
  2. Which idea applies. Compressive residual stress delays crack growth.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) True. (C) True. (D) Peening improves life. False.
  4. Check. Polishing also helps.

Answer: (A), (B) and (C).

Summary

Here are the one-line meanings of the main ideas on this page.

Fatigue
Failure under repeated loads, each below the material's strength.
Cycle
One full rise and fall of the stress.
Stress amplitude
Half the difference between the maximum and minimum stress in a cycle.
S-N diagram
A plot of stress amplitude against cycles to failure, usually on log-log axes.
Endurance limit Se
The stress below which steel is expected to survive very many cycles.
Fatigue strength
The stress a material can take for a stated number of cycles.
Specimen endurance limit Se'
For steel, about half of Sut, for a polished test piece.
Marin factors
Correction factors for surface, size, load and other differences from the specimen.
Surface factor ka
Lowers the endurance limit for rough machined surfaces.
Size factor kb
Lowers the endurance limit for larger round parts.
Load factor kc
One for bending, lower for axial loading and torsion.
Low-cycle fatigue
High stress and few cycles, with permanent strain each cycle.
High-cycle fatigue
Lower stress and many cycles, with mostly elastic behaviour.
Log-log plot
A graph on logarithmic axes where a power law is a straight line.
Slope b
The slope of the S-N line on log-log paper, negative for fatigue data.
Life N
The number of cycles to failure at a given stress amplitude.

Remember these

  1. For steel with Sut up to about 1400 MPa, Se' = 0.5 Sut.
  2. Aluminium and other non-ferrous metals usually have no clear endurance limit, so use a fatigue strength at a stated life.
  3. The real endurance limit is Se = ka kb kc Se'.
  4. Factors apply to the specimen limit, never directly to Sut.
  5. On log-log paper, an S-N line is straight, so use the slope formula with two points.
  6. The S-N slope is negative: more stress means fewer cycles.

Superposition & Max Power Transfer theorem

The superposition theorem lets you solve a circuit with several sources one source at a time, then add the results. The maximum power transfer theorem tells you which load resistance takes the most power from a source. Both are standard GATE RA and DA tools.

The idea in plain words

Imagine two cooks adding salt to one soup. Cook A adds a spoon, and the soup tastes a little salty. Cook B adds another spoon. The final saltiness is the sum of the two spoons, because salt does not interact with itself. The soup is simple enough that we can think about each cook separately and then add the effects.

Many electrical circuits behave the same way. If a circuit is linear, the response to several sources is the sum of the responses to each source alone. Linear means that doubling a source doubles every current and voltage in the circuit. Resistors, capacitors, inductors, ideal sources and dependent sources (when their rules are linear) are all linear.

The superposition theorem uses this idea. You keep one independent source active, switch off the others, solve the simpler circuit, then repeat for the next source. Finally you add all the answers.

The maximum power transfer theorem answers a different question. Suppose you have a source with a fixed internal resistance, and you can choose the load resistance. Which load takes the most power? The answer is simple and very useful. The load should match the source resistance.

Key ideaSuperposition is for voltages and currents. It is not for power. Maximum power transfer is for the power delivered to a load, and it depends on the load resistance.

Key terms

Each term has a plain meaning first, then a precise definition.

Linear circuit
A circuit in which the output is proportional to the input. Precisely: scaling every source by a factor scales every current and voltage by the same factor.
Superposition
Adding the effects of separate sources. Precisely: the response of a linear circuit to several independent sources is the sum of the responses to each source acting alone.
Deactivate a source
Switch a source off for the superposition step. Precisely: replace a voltage source by a short circuit and a current source by an open circuit.
Short circuit
A plain wire with zero resistance. Precisely: zero voltage across it, any current through it.
Open circuit
A break with no path. Precisely: zero current through it, any voltage across it.
Load
The part of the circuit that receives power. Precisely: the element whose resistance we choose or vary.
Thevenin voltage Vth
The voltage the source would show across open terminals. Precisely: the open-circuit voltage seen by the load terminals.
Thevenin resistance Rth
The resistance the source presents to the load. Precisely: the resistance seen from the load terminals with independent sources deactivated.
Power
The rate at which energy is delivered. Precisely: P = VI = I2R = V2/R for a resistor, measured in watt (W).
Efficiency
The fraction of power that reaches the load. Precisely: Pload divided by the total power supplied by the source.
Maximum power transfer
The condition that gives the largest power to the load. Precisely: RL = Rth.

Superposition: how it works

The four steps

  1. Pick one independent source. Leave all the others in the circuit, but deactivate them.
  2. Deactivate the other sources. A voltage source becomes a short circuit. A current source becomes an open circuit.
  3. Solve the circuit. Find the voltage or current you want, using any method you know.
  4. Repeat for each source, then add. The total is the sum of all the single-source results, with signs taken into account.

Why these replacements?

A voltage source keeps a fixed voltage. Setting that voltage to zero means a wire, which is a short circuit. A current source keeps a fixed current. Setting that current to zero means nothing flows, which is an open circuit. These replacements keep the circuit unchanged except for the switched-off source.

TrapDependent sources are never deactivated in superposition. Their value depends on the circuit, so they stay in the circuit at every step. Only independent sources are switched off.

Signs

Each single-source answer has a sign, decided by the direction of the source in the original circuit. If one source pushes a current to the left and another pushes it to the right, the second answer is subtracted from the first. Always write the reference direction before you start, and keep it the same for every step.

Self-check

In the superposition theorem, what replaces a current source that is switched off?

An open circuit. A current source keeps a fixed current, and setting it to zero means no current flows, which is the same as a break in the wire.

Can we use superposition to add the powers from two sources?

No. Power depends on the square of the current or voltage, so the power from two sources together is not the sum of their separate powers. Superposition adds voltages and currents only.

Worked example: superposition

The circuit

A single node V has three connections. A 3 A ideal current source pushes current into the node. A 2 ohm resistor goes from the node to ground. A branch goes from the node to a 12 V ideal voltage source, with a 4 ohm resistor in series, the plus terminal of the voltage source at the top. Find V.

        3 A pushes into node V
              |
   node V ----+---------[ 2 ohm ]--- ground
              |
              +---[ 4 ohm ]---(+ 12 V -)--- ground

Step 1: the voltage source alone

  1. Deactivate the current source. Replace the 3 A source with an open circuit.
  2. Write KCL at node V. The current through the 4 ohm branch from the source is (12 − V)/4, and the current to ground through 2 ohm is V/2. These must balance, so (12 − V)/4 = V/2.
  3. Solve. Multiply by 4: 12 − V = 2V, so 3V = 12 and V1 = 4 V.

Step 2: the current source alone

  1. Deactivate the voltage source. Replace the 12 V source with a short circuit. The 4 ohm branch now goes from node V straight to ground, so it is a 4 ohm resistor from node V to ground.
  2. Write KCL at node V. The current from the source, 3 A, flows out through the 2 ohm and 4 ohm resistors in parallel. So 3 = V/2 + V/4 = (3/4)V.
  3. Solve. V2 = 3 × 4/3 = 4 V.

Step 3: add

The total is V = V1 + V2 = 4 + 4 = 8 V. Check with the full circuit: (12 − 8)/4 = 1 A from the voltage source branch into the node, plus 3 A from the current source, gives 4 A. Out through the 2 ohm: 8/2 = 4 A. The two sides agree.

from fractions import Fraction as F
V_voltage_only = F(12, 4) / (F(1, 4) + F(1, 2))
V_current_only = F(3) / (F(1, 2) + F(1, 4))
print("from voltage source:", V_voltage_only, "V")
print("from current source:", V_current_only, "V")
print("total:", V_voltage_only + V_current_only, "V")
print("full circuit check:", (12 - (V_voltage_only + V_current_only)) / 4 + 3 == (V_voltage_only + V_current_only) / 2)
from voltage source: 4 V
from current source: 4 V
total: 8 V
full circuit check: True

TipWrite the first step's formula as a balance of currents, not as a list of resistances. It is then easy to see which current goes where, and the signs are less likely to go wrong.

Self-check

In the worked example, what is the voltage at node V if the voltage source is reversed (plus at the bottom)?

The voltage source contribution changes sign: it becomes −4 V. The current source contribution stays at 4 V. The total is 0 V.

Why is the 4 ohm resistor in parallel with the 2 ohm during step 2?

With the voltage source shorted, the branch with the 4 ohm resistor has no source in it. Its far end is the short, which goes to ground, so the 4 ohm connects node V to ground. Both resistors then join node V to ground in parallel.

Maximum power transfer

The setup

Any linear source, seen from a load, can be described by a simple model: a voltage Vth in series with a resistance Rth. This is called the Thevenin model. The load RL connects across it. (You will meet this model in full on the next topic.)

  +---[ R_th ]---+
  |              |
 (V_th)        [ R_L ]
  |              |
  +--------------+

The current in the loop is I = Vth/(Rth + RL). The power in the load is PL = I2RL. Putting these together:

PL = Vth2 RL / (Rth + RL)2

Here Vth is the source voltage, Rth is the source resistance, and RL is the load we choose.

Where the maximum is

Try a few values. If RL is very small, the current is large but the load resistance is small, so the power is small. If RL is very large, the resistance is large but the current is tiny, so the power is again small. Somewhere between, the product is largest. The theorem says that place is RL = Rth, and the largest power is:

Pmax = Vth2 / (4 Rth)

The next worked example checks this with numbers. Here is the table for Vth = 12 V and Rth = 4 ohm.

from fractions import Fraction as F
Vth = 12
Rth = 4
for RL in [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8]:
    P = F(Vth * Vth * RL) / F((Rth + RL) ** 2)
    print("RL =", RL, "ohm, P =", round(float(P), 3), "W")
RL = 1 ohm, P = 5.76 W
RL = 2 ohm, P = 8.0 W
RL = 3 ohm, P = 8.816 W
RL = 4 ohm, P = 9.0 W
RL = 5 ohm, P = 8.889 W
RL = 6 ohm, P = 8.64 W
RL = 8 ohm, P = 8.0 W

The largest value is at RL = 4 ohm, which equals Rth. The power there is 9 W, and the formula gives 122/(4 × 4) = 144/16 = 9 W. Both agree.

Why the power is only half of the source's output

At the maximum, the load and the source resistance are equal. They share the voltage equally, so the load gets half of the source voltage. The source resistance wastes the other half of the power as heat. The efficiency is therefore 50 percent at maximum power transfer. This is a key point: maximum power is not the same as maximum efficiency. A power system chooses high efficiency, while a signal or communication circuit often chooses maximum power.

TrapThe condition RL = Rth applies to the load only. It does not mean the load should be made equal to the source voltage or current. Also, Rth must be found by deactivating independent sources only. Dependent sources stay in place.

GATE loves thisGATE often gives a circuit with a source behind a fixed resistor, and asks for the load that takes the most power. Find Rth first, then set RL to that value, then use the power formula.

Self-check

A source has Vth = 20 V and Rth = 5 ohm. What load gives maximum power, and what is the maximum power?

The load should be RL = 5 ohm. The maximum power is 202/(4 × 5) = 400/20 = 20 W.

What is the efficiency at maximum power transfer?

Half, or 50 percent. The load and the source resistance are equal, so the load gets half of the source voltage and half of the total power.

Worked example: maximum power with a source resistance

The problem

A source has Vth = 24 V and Rth = 6 ohm. A 2 ohm wire resistance is in series between the source and the load. The load RL is what we choose. Find the value of RL that gives maximum power, and the maximum power.

The steps

  1. Find the total source resistance. The 2 ohm wire is in series with the source, so it adds to Rth. The new Rth is 6 + 2 = 8 ohm.
  2. Set the load. Maximum power occurs when RL equals the new Rth. So RL = 8 ohm.
  3. Find the current. I = 24/(8 + 8) = 1.5 A.
  4. Find the power. PL = I2RL = 1.52 × 8 = 2.25 × 8 = 18 W.
  5. Check. The formula gives 242/(4 × 8) = 576/32 = 18 W. Both agree.

TrapA common error is to use Rth = 6 ohm and choose RL = 6 ohm. The series wire belongs to the source side, so it must be included in Rth.

from fractions import Fraction as F
Vth, Rth_total = F(24), F(6 + 2)
RL = Rth_total
I = Vth / (Rth_total + RL)
P = I ** 2 * RL
print("RL =", RL, "ohm")
print("current =", I, "A")
print("power =", P, "W")
print("formula check:", Vth ** 2 / (4 * Rth_total) == P)
RL = 8 ohm
current = 3/2 A
power = 18 W
formula check: True

Self-check

If the wire resistance in the example were 0 ohm, what would RL be?

RL = Rth = 6 ohm. The maximum power would then be 242/(4 × 6) = 576/24 = 24 W.

Why does a series wire matter for maximum power?

It adds resistance on the source side. Maximum power depends on the total resistance the load sees, so the load must match that total, not just the resistance of the source itself.

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original problems in the GATE style, not past exam questions. Try each one on paper first.

Two opposing voltage sources in one loop

A 10 V source and a 4 V source are in one loop, with their voltages opposing. The total resistance of the loop is 3 ohm. Using superposition, find the current in ampere, flowing in the direction of the 10 V source.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The loop current, in the direction of the 10 V source.
  2. Which idea applies. Superposition: find the current from each source alone, then add with signs.
  3. Work it out. The 10 V source alone gives 10/3 A in its direction. The 4 V source alone gives 4/3 A, opposite. Total: 10/3 − 4/3 = 6/3 = 2 A.
  4. Check. The net source voltage is 10 − 4 = 6 V, and 6/3 = 2 A. The same answer.

Answer: 2 A.

from fractions import Fraction as F
print("current =", F(10, 3) - F(4, 3), "A")
current = 2 A

Maximum power from a simple source

A source has Vth = 10 V and Rth = 5 ohm. Find the maximum power that can be delivered to a load, in watt.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Pmax.
  2. Which idea applies. Pmax = Vth2/(4Rth).
  3. Work it out. Pmax = 100/(4 × 5) = 100/20 = 5 W.
  4. Check. At RL = 5 ohm, the current is 10/10 = 1 A, and the power is 12 × 5 = 5 W.

Answer: 5 W.

from fractions import Fraction as F
print("Pmax =", F(10 ** 2, 4 * 5), "W")
Pmax = 5 W

Statements about the two theorems (MSQ)

Select all that apply.

(A) In maximum power transfer, the load voltage equals the full Thevenin voltage.
(B) When a current source is deactivated for superposition, it is replaced by an open circuit.
(C) Maximum power transfer occurs when RL is twice Rth.
(D) At maximum power transfer, the efficiency is 50 percent.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true.
  2. Which idea applies. The load voltage in the Thevenin model, the deactivation rule, the maximum power condition, and efficiency.
  3. Work it out. (A) False: at RL = Rth the load gets half of Vth. (B) True. (C) False: the condition is RL = Rth. (D) True: efficiency is RL/(RL + Rth) = 1/2.
  4. Check. Test (A) with numbers: Vth = 12, Rth = RL = 4, so the load voltage is 12 × 4/8 = 6 V, which is half.

Answer: (B) and (D).

Superposition with a current and a voltage source (NAT)

A 6 A current source pushes current into a node V. From V to ground there is a 3 ohm resistor. A 9 V source is in series with a 6 ohm resistor, connected from the voltage source to node V (the plus terminal at the far end). Using superposition, find the part of V that comes from the 9 V source alone, in volt.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The voltage at V caused only by the 9 V source.
  2. Which idea applies. Deactivate the 6 A source (open circuit), then solve for V.
  3. Work it out. With the current source open, the 6 ohm branch and the 3 ohm resistor form a divider with the source. KCL: (9 − V)/6 = V/3. Multiply by 6: 9 − V = 2V, so 3V = 9 and V = 3 V.
  4. Check. Current from source: (9 − 3)/6 = 1 A. Current to ground: 3/3 = 1 A. They balance.

Answer: 3 V.

from fractions import Fraction as F
V_I = F(6) / (F(1, 3) + F(1, 6))
V_V = F(9, 6) / (F(1, 3) + F(1, 6))
print("from current source:", V_I, "V")
print("from voltage source:", V_V, "V")
print("total:", V_I + V_V, "V")
from current source: 12 V
from voltage source: 3 V
total: 15 V

Power into a load away from the match (NAT)

A source has Vth = 20 V and Rth = 8 ohm. A load of 2 ohm is connected. Find the power delivered to the load, in watt.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The power in a 2 ohm load, which is not the matched value.
  2. Which idea applies. I = Vth/(Rth + RL), then P = I2RL.
  3. Work it out. I = 20/(8 + 2) = 2 A. P = 22 × 2 = 8 W.
  4. Check. Total power from the source is 20 × 2 = 40 W. The load gets 8 W, which is 20 percent. That is less than the 50 percent efficiency at maximum transfer, as expected.

Answer: 8 W.

from fractions import Fraction as F
Vth, Rth, RL = F(20), F(8), F(2)
I = Vth / (Rth + RL)
print("current =", I, "A")
print("power =", I ** 2 * RL, "W")
current = 2 A
power = 8 W

Maximum power for a given source (NAT)

A source has Vth = 6 V and Rth = 3 ohm. The load is chosen to be 9 ohm. Find the power in the load, in watt.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. PL for RL = 9 ohm.
  2. Which idea applies. The load power formula.
  3. Work it out. I = 6/(3 + 9) = 0.5 A. P = 0.52 × 9 = 0.25 × 9 = 2.25 W.
  4. Check. Maximum would be 36/12 = 3 W at RL = 3 ohm. The value 2.25 W is below that, as it must be.

Answer: 2.25 W.

from fractions import Fraction as F
print("P =", F(6 ** 2 * 9, (3 + 9) ** 2), "W")
P = 9/4 W

Matching with a series wire (NAT)

A source has Vth = 24 V and Rth = 6 ohm. A 2 ohm wire is in series between the source and the load. The load is adjusted to take the most power. Find that maximum power, in watt.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The maximum power into the load.
  2. Which idea applies. The 2 ohm wire adds to Rth, so the effective source resistance is 8 ohm. Maximum power occurs when RL = 8 ohm.
  3. Work it out. Pmax = 242/(4 × 8) = 576/32 = 18 W.
  4. Check. With RL = 8 ohm, I = 24/16 = 1.5 A, and P = 1.52 × 8 = 18 W. Both agree.

Answer: 18 W.

from fractions import Fraction as F
Vth, Rtot = F(24), F(6 + 2)
I = Vth / (Rtot + Rtot)
print("Pmax =", I ** 2 * Rtot, "W")
Pmax = 18 W

Statements about superposition and power (MSQ)

Select all that apply.

(A) The formula Pmax = Vth2/(4Rth) gives the power into a load equal to Rth.
(B) Superposition can be applied to a linear circuit that contains a dependent source, with the dependent source kept active at every step.
(C) The power from two sources acting together equals the sum of the powers from each source alone.
(D) A voltage source is replaced by an open circuit when it is deactivated.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true.
  2. Which idea applies. The maximum power formula, the rule for dependent sources in superposition, why power does not add, and the deactivation rules.
  3. Work it out. (A) True: the formula is the power at the matched load. (B) True: dependent sources stay active. (C) False: power depends on the square of the total current, so it does not add. (D) False: a voltage source is replaced by a short circuit.
  4. Check. Test (C) with a 1 ohm resistor. Each source alone gives 1 A and 2 A, with powers 1 W and 4 W. Together the current is 3 A, giving 9 W, not 5 W. Power does not add.

Answer: (A) and (B).

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Superposition (NAT, 2 marks)

A 10 V source in series with 2 Ω feeds node A. A 3 A current source also feeds node A. A 3 Ω resistor connects A to ground. Find VA.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The node voltage by superposition.
  2. Which idea applies. Find each source's contribution with the other turned off.
  3. Work it out. Voltage source alone (current source open): 10 · 3/5 = 6 V. Current source alone (voltage source shorted): 3 · (2 ∥ 3) = 3 · 1.2 = 3.6 V. Total 9.6 V.
  4. Check. Nodal check: (V − 10)/2 + V/3 = 3 gives V(5/6) = 8, V = 9.6.

Answer: 9.6 V.

Exam-level 2: Maximum power (NAT, 2 marks)

A Thevenin source of 20 V with Rth = 5 Ω drives a load RL. Find the maximum power the load can receive.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Pmax = Vth²/(4Rth).
  2. Which idea applies. Maximum transfer happens at RL = Rth.
  3. Work it out. 400/20 = 20 W.
  4. Check. The code scans RL.
best = max((20 ** 2 * R / (5 + R) ** 2, R) for R in [k / 100 for k in range(1, 5001)])
print("Pmax = %.2f W at R = %.2f" % best)

Pmax = 20.00 W at R = 5.00

Answer: 20 W.

Exam-level 3: Superposition limits (MSQ, 2 marks)

Which are TRUE? (A) Superposition applies to voltages and currents in linear circuits. (B) It applies directly to power. (C) When turning off sources, voltage sources become shorts and current sources become opens. (D) Efficiency at maximum power transfer is 50%.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Correct uses of the two theorems.
  2. Which idea applies. Power is quadratic, so contributions do not add.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) False. (C) True. (D) Half the power is lost in Rth. True.
  4. Check. For example, two sources giving 1 A each give 4R watts, not 2R watts.

Answer: (A), (C) and (D).

Aptitude: quantitative reasoning

Aptitude rounds test quick arithmetic and logical reasoning. Most of the quantitative questions in campus and product-company tests come from a small set of topics: percentages, profit and loss, ratios, time and work, time speed distance, and simple and compound interest. The goal is not to memorise tricks. The goal is to know one formula per idea and to use it the same way every time, writing down each step so that a small slip is easy to spot.

A useful habit: before you calculate, say in one line what is being asked and which quantity is the base. Most wrong answers in aptitude come from using the wrong base, not from bad multiplication.

Percentages

A percentage is a fraction out of 100. The word comes from the Latin "per centum", meaning "per hundred". So 25% means 25 out of every 100, which is the fraction 25/100 or 0.25.

  • x% of N is x/100 multiplied by N. For example, 25% of 480 is 0.25 * 480 = 120.
  • What percent a is of b is a/b multiplied by 100. For example, 30 out of 120 is 30/120 * 100 = 25%.
  • Percent change is (new value minus old value) divided by the old value, multiplied by 100. The old value is always the base.

The most common trap is successive change. When a value changes by one percentage and then by another, you multiply the factors. An increase of 20% multiplies by 1.20. A decrease of 20% multiplies by 0.80. The two percentages do not simply add up to zero.

Worked example: a price of 500 rises by 20%, then falls by 20%. Step 1: after the rise, the price is 500 * 1.20 = 600. Step 2: after the fall, the price is 600 * 0.80 = 480. The net change is 480 minus 500, which is -20. As a percentage of the original 500, that is -4%. The answer is a 4% decrease, not "no change".

TrapSuccessive changes of +x% and -x% never cancel out. Multiply the factors (1 + x/100) and (1 - x/100). The product is always less than 1, because (1 + x)(1 - x) = 1 - x squared.

Profit, loss, markup and discount

The cost price (CP) is what the seller paid. The selling price (SP) is what the buyer pays. The marked price (MP) is the list price printed on the item, before any discount.

  • If SP is greater than CP, there is a profit of SP minus CP. If SP is less than CP, there is a loss of CP minus SP.
  • Profit percent is profit divided by CP, multiplied by 100. The base is always CP, even though the question is about the selling price.
  • Markup means the seller raises the cost price to get the marked price: MP = CP * (1 + markup/100).
  • Discount is taken off the marked price: SP = MP * (1 - discount/100).

Worked example 1: CP = 400, SP = 500. Profit = 500 minus 400 = 100. Profit percent = 100 / 400 * 100 = 25%.

Worked example 2: CP = 250, SP = 200. Loss = 250 minus 200 = 50. Loss percent = 50 / 250 * 100 = 20%.

Worked example 3: CP = 500, markup 40%, then discount 10%. Step 1: MP = 500 * 1.40 = 700. Step 2: SP = 700 * 0.90 = 630. Step 3: profit = 630 minus 500 = 130, which is 130 / 500 * 100 = 26% on the cost price.

TrapThe profit percent uses CP as the base, not SP. A profit of 100 on an SP of 500 is 25% profit on cost (100 / 400), not 20% (100 / 500).

Ratios and proportions

A ratio a:b compares two quantities. To use it, split the total into parts. If two numbers are in the ratio 3:5 and their sum is 96, the total number of parts is 3 + 5 = 8. Each part is 96 / 8 = 12. So the numbers are 3 * 12 = 36 and 5 * 12 = 60. Check: 36 + 60 = 96.

When the question changes the ratio after a fixed time, write the ratio with an unknown. Worked example: the ages of A and B are in the ratio 2:3. After 4 years, the ratio becomes 3:4. Let the present ages be 2x and 3x. After 4 years they are 2x + 4 and 3x + 4. Cross-multiply: 4(2x + 4) = 3(3x + 4), so 8x + 16 = 9x + 12, so x = 4. The present ages are 8 and 12. Check: after 4 years they are 12 and 16, which is the ratio 3:4.

Time and work

Treat the whole job as 1 unit of work. If A finishes a job in n days, A does 1/n of the job each day. This daily fraction is the work rate. When people work together, their rates add up. The time taken together is 1 divided by the sum of the rates.

Worked example 1: A finishes a job in 10 days and B in 15 days. Rates: 1/10 and 1/15. Together: 1/10 + 1/15 = 3/30 + 2/30 = 5/30 = 1/6. So they finish in 6 days.

Worked example 2: A takes 12 days and B takes 24 days. A works alone for 3 days, then B joins. In 3 days A does 3/12 = 1/4 of the job, so 3/4 remains. Together they do 1/12 + 1/24 = 3/24 = 1/8 per day. The remaining 3/4 takes (3/4) / (1/8) = 6 days. The total time is 3 + 6 = 9 days.

Time, speed and distance

The basic formula is speed = distance / time. Rearranged, distance = speed * time and time = distance / speed. The units must match. Most problems give speeds in km/h and lengths in metres, so you need a conversion.

  • km/h to m/s: multiply by 5/18. For example, 54 km/h is 54 * 5 / 18 = 15 m/s.
  • m/s to km/h: multiply by 18/5. For example, 15 m/s is 15 * 18 / 5 = 54 km/h.
  • Relative speed for objects moving towards each other is the sum of their speeds. For objects moving in the same direction, it is the difference.
  • A train crossing a pole covers only its own length. A train crossing a platform covers its length plus the platform length.

Worked example 1: a train 150 m long moves at 54 km/h. It crosses a pole (zero length) in 150 / 15 = 10 seconds.

Worked example 2: two cars start 300 km apart and drive towards each other at 60 km/h and 40 km/h. Their relative speed is 60 + 40 = 100 km/h. They meet after 300 / 100 = 3 hours.

Worked example 3: a man walks at 5 km/h for 30 minutes. Convert 30 minutes to 0.5 hours. Distance = 5 * 0.5 = 2.5 km.

TrapConvert minutes to hours by dividing by 60 before you multiply by a speed in km/h. A common slip is to multiply 5 km/h by 30 and report 150 km.

Simple and compound interest

Simple interest (SI) is paid only on the original amount, called the principal P. The formula is SI = P * R * T / 100, where R is the rate per year in percent and T is the time in years. The final amount is P + SI.

Compound interest (CI) is paid on the principal plus all interest already earned. With annual compounding, the amount after n years is A = P * (1 + R/100) raised to the power n. The interest earned is CI = A minus P.

Worked example 1: SI on 2000 at 5% for 3 years is 2000 * 5 * 3 / 100 = 300.

Worked example 2: CI on 10000 at 10% for 2 years. Year 1: 10000 * 1.10 = 11000. Year 2: 11000 * 1.10 = 12100. So CI = 12100 minus 10000 = 2100. Simple interest would give only 2000, because the second year's interest is also earned on the first year's interest.

Interviewers ask"Why does compound interest grow faster than simple interest?" Answer: because each period's interest is added to the principal, so the next period earns interest on a larger base. Simple interest always uses the same base.

The items are grouped by language. Each answer is written in the form an interviewer expects: a short definition, then one reason or example.

Python

What is the mutable default argument trap?

Default values are evaluated once, when the function is defined, not each time it is called. So def add(item, items=[]) shares one list across all calls. The fix is items=None and then if items is None: items = [] inside the body.

What does LEGB stand for, and in what order does Python search for a name?

Local, Enclosing, Global, Built-in. Python looks for a name in the current function first, then in each enclosing function scope, then in the module (global) scope, and finally in the built-in names such as len.

What is a closure, and why do lambdas created in a loop often surprise people?

A closure is a function that remembers variables from the scope where it was created. A closure captures the variable, not its value at creation time. So all the lambdas in a loop see the final value of the loop variable. The check block below shows this, and the fix.

Why does a class with __eq__ but no __hash__ stop being usable as a dictionary key?

In Python 3, defining __eq__ sets __hash__ to None unless you define it too. An unhashable object cannot be placed in a set or used as a dict key. A hash must be consistent with equality: equal objects must have equal hashes.

C

What is the difference between the stack and the heap in C?

Local variables live on the stack and are released automatically when the function returns. Memory from malloc, calloc or realloc lives on the heap and stays allocated until you call free. Forgetting to free heap memory is a leak.

What is a dangling pointer?

A pointer that still holds an address after the memory it points to has been freed or has gone out of scope, for example a pointer to a local variable returned from a function. Using it is undefined behaviour. The fix is to set the pointer to NULL after free and never return the address of a local variable.

Why can sizeof of a struct be larger than the sum of its members?

The compiler inserts padding bytes so that each member starts at an address that suits its alignment. For example, a 4-byte int usually starts at a multiple of 4. Reordering members from largest to smallest often reduces padding.

What happens to an array passed to a function?

The array decays to a pointer to its first element. Inside the function, sizeof gives the size of the pointer, not of the array. So the function must receive the length as a separate argument.

OOP and design

Name the four pillars of object-oriented programming.

Encapsulation (bundling data with the methods that act on it and hiding internal state), abstraction (exposing only what a user needs), inheritance (a class reuses and extends another class), and polymorphism (one interface, many implementations chosen at run time or compile time).

What is the difference between overloading and overriding?

Overloading means several functions share a name but have different parameter lists, all in one class; the call is chosen by the arguments. Overriding means a subclass gives a new implementation of a method with the same signature as in the parent; the call is chosen by the object's actual type at run time.

When do you choose an abstract class over an interface?

Use an abstract class when subclasses share some state or some concrete code, and you want to provide a partial implementation. Use an interface when unrelated classes must share a contract, or when a class must implement several contracts.

Java

What is the difference between == and equals in Java?

== compares references for objects (whether two variables point to the same object) and values for primitives. equals compares the contents, if the class defines it that way. A String created with new String("hi") is a different object from the literal "hi", so == returns false while equals returns true.

What does it mean that a Java String is immutable?

Once created, the characters of a String cannot change. Methods such as toUpperCase return a new String. This is why repeated concatenation in a loop is slow and StringBuilder is the better tool for building text.

What must be true of equals and hashCode for a HashMap key?

If two objects are equal under equals, they must return the same hashCode. If you override equals but not hashCode, equal keys can land in different buckets and lookups fail.

What is the difference between checked and unchecked exceptions?

Checked exceptions (for example IOException) must be caught or declared with throws, because the compiler enforces it. Unchecked exceptions (subclasses of RuntimeException, such as NullPointerException) need not be declared.

Resume and HR preparation

Technical rounds decide whether you can do the job. HR and behavioural rounds decide whether the interviewer wants to work with you, and they often come at the end of the day. Preparing for them is cheap, because the answers come from your own experience. The templates below have blanks marked in square brackets. Fill each blank with facts you can defend in a follow-up question. Do not invent numbers, tools or results. If you do not know a number, describe the change in words instead.

The STAR method

STAR is a structure for answering "tell me about a time when" questions. It stands for Situation, Task, Action and Result.

  • Situation. One or two sentences of context: where and when, and what was at stake.
  • Task. Your specific responsibility. Say "I" here, not "we".
  • Action. The steps you took, in order. This is the longest part, and it should be your choices and reasons.
  • Result. What happened, with a measurable outcome if you have one, and what you learnt.

Here is a sample story to show the shape. It is not a real account: a student group's demo kept failing at night. The student (the "I") was asked to find the cause before the demo. They added logging to each step, found that a file path was different on the server, fixed it, and wrote a checklist so the problem could not return. The result: the next three demos ran without a failure, and the checklist was adopted by the group. Notice that the story names one clear problem, one clear action, and one outcome.

TipPrepare one story for each of these themes: a conflict with a teammate, a mistake you made, a time you learnt something quickly, a time you led or took ownership, and a project you are proud of. Each story can be reused for several questions.

The two-minute self-introduction

"Tell me about yourself" is almost always the first question. Two minutes of speech is roughly 250 to 280 words. Use four short parts: present, past, projects, and why this role. Practise it out loud until it fits in two minutes without reading it.

  1. Present. "I am [your name], a [your year and degree] student at [your college], interested in [the area of this job]."
  2. Past. "In my studies I focused on [one or two subjects, for example Python, C and operating systems]. In [a course or activity], I [one concrete thing you did]."
  3. Projects. "My main project is [project name], which [one sentence on what it does and the tools you used]. I was responsible for [your part]. [One result, if you have a real one]."
  4. Why this role. "I want to join [company or team] because [a reason that connects to their work]. I would like to bring [a strength, with an example]."

TrapDo not recite your whole resume in the self-introduction. The interviewer has the resume. Choose the three or four facts that make them want to ask the next question.

Strengths and weaknesses

For a strength, name one quality and back it with a short example. "I am organised" is weak. "I keep a written plan for every project and I update it each week, which is how I finished the last project two days early" is strong, provided it is true.

For a weakness, pick a real one that you are improving. Then describe the steps you are taking. A good structure is: name the weakness, give one example, and then explain what you do about it now. Avoid fake weaknesses such as "I work too hard"; interviewers hear that answer often and it sounds rehearsed.

Mock interview: questions and model answers

This mock covers Python, C, OOP and Java, the four areas of week 1. Set a timer for 30 minutes, read each question aloud, answer in your own words, and then compare with the model answer. Good answers are short, correct, and followed by an example. If you cannot answer in under a minute, you have found a gap to revise.

Q1 (Python): What is the difference between a list, a tuple and a set?

A list is an ordered, mutable sequence (you can change it). A tuple is an ordered, immutable sequence, so it can be used as a dictionary key if its items are hashable. A set is an unordered collection of unique, hashable items with fast membership tests. Example: [1, 2] can change, (1, 2) cannot, and {1, 2} rejects duplicates.

Q2 (Python): What is a generator, and why would you use one?

A generator is a function that uses yield to produce values one at a time, pausing between them. It keeps only one value in memory at a time, so it suits large or endless sequences. Example: sum(x * x for x in range(10)) never builds the full list of squares.

Q3 (C): What is the difference between malloc and calloc?

malloc(n) allocates n bytes and leaves their contents indeterminate. calloc(count, size) allocates count * size bytes and sets them all to zero. Both return NULL on failure, so the return value must be checked before use.

Q4 (C): Why is returning the address of a local variable a bug?

Local variables live on the stack, and their memory is reused once the function returns. The caller receives a dangling pointer, and reading it gives undefined behaviour: it may appear to work, then fail later. The fix is to allocate on the heap with malloc, or let the caller pass in a buffer.

Q5 (OOP): Explain polymorphism with a short example.

Polymorphism lets code call one method name on different objects and get the behaviour of each object's own class. Example: a list of shapes, each with an area() method. A loop calls area() on every shape and the right formula runs for each, without the loop knowing the shape type.

Q6 (OOP): What is the Liskov Substitution Principle?

A subclass must be usable wherever its parent class is expected, without breaking the program. If a Square subclass of Rectangle changes the width when you set the height, code that relies on the rectangle's behaviour breaks. That is a violation.

Q7 (C++ or OOP): What is a virtual function, and what is a vtable?

A virtual function is a member function that a derived class can override, with the correct version chosen at run time through a pointer or reference to the base class. A vtable is a per-class table of function pointers that the compiler creates, and each object holds a pointer to its class's table. The call looks up the correct function through that table.

Q8 (Java): What does the final keyword do for a variable, a method and a class?

A final variable can be assigned only once. A final method cannot be overridden. A final class cannot be extended. Example: String is a final class, so no one can subclass it and change its behaviour.

Q9 (Java): Why would you choose a HashMap over a TreeMap?

A HashMap gives average constant-time lookup, insert and delete, but does not keep keys in order. A TreeMap keeps keys sorted and gives logarithmic time operations. Choose the HashMap for fast lookups, and the TreeMap when you need sorted keys or range queries.

Q10 (Java): What is the difference between ArrayList and LinkedList?

An ArrayList stores elements in an array, so reading by index is fast, but inserting in the middle needs shifting. A LinkedList stores nodes linked together, so inserting or removing at a known node is cheap, but reading by index walks the list. For most code, ArrayList is the better default.

DSA: timed mock set

Today's DSA practice is a mock, not a new topic. A mock simulates the real round: you get a set of problems, a timer, and no hints. Use the same routine each time so that it becomes automatic. Spend the first five minutes restating the problem and asking yourself about edge cases. Then choose an approach, write the code, and dry-run one example by hand before you say you are finished.

The three problems today are one medium on intervals, one medium on greedy reach, and one hard on greedy with two passes. Greedy means making the locally best choice at each step and proving that it leads to a globally good answer. Both mediums and the hard use only one pass or two simple passes over the input.

Pattern: sort, then sweep

Many interval problems become simple after sorting by the start value. Once the intervals are sorted, any overlap must be with the interval you just looked at. So a single left-to-right sweep is enough, and the sorting step costs O(n log n).

Pattern: track the farthest reach

In jump problems, keep one number: the farthest index you can reach so far. Each position you can stand on extends that reach. If you ever stand on a position beyond the reach, you are stuck.

Pattern: two passes for a neighbour rule

When a value depends on both left and right neighbours, one pass is not enough. Do one pass left to right to handle the left rule, then one pass right to left to handle the right rule, and keep the larger requirement each time.

Merge Intervals (LeetCode 56)

You are given a list of intervals, each written as [start, end]. Merge all intervals that overlap, and return the merged list sorted by start. Example: [[1,3],[2,6],[8,10],[15,18]] becomes [[1,6],[8,10],[15,18]], because [1,3] and [2,6] overlap.

How to think about it

  1. Understand. Two intervals overlap if the second starts before or at the end of the first. Touching intervals such as [1,4] and [4,5] also merge, so the test is "start less than or equal to the current end". An empty list returns an empty list.
  2. Brute force. Compare every pair of intervals, merge any that overlap, and repeat until nothing changes. This is O(n squared) or worse, because a merge can create a new overlap.
  3. Find the waste. Comparing every pair ignores the order. If the intervals are sorted by start, an interval can only overlap the most recent merged interval.
  4. Optimise. Sort by start. Keep a list of merged intervals. For each interval, either extend the last merged interval (if they overlap) or append a new one. Sorting costs O(n log n); the sweep costs O(n).
  5. Dry run. Input sorted already: [1,3], [2,6], [8,10], [15,18]. Start with [1,3]. Next is [2,6]: 2 is at most 3, so extend the end to max(3,6)=6, giving [1,6]. Next is [8,10]: 8 is more than 6, so append. Next is [15,18]: 15 is more than 10, so append. Result: [1,6], [8,10], [15,18].

Code

def merge(intervals):
    # Sort by start so that any overlap is with the last merged interval.
    intervals = sorted(intervals, key=lambda p: p[0])
    merged = []
    for start, end in intervals:
        if merged and start <= merged[-1][1]:
            # Overlap: extend the end of the last merged interval if needed.
            merged[-1][1] = max(merged[-1][1], end)
        else:
            merged.append([start, end])
    return merged

print(merge([[1, 4], [4, 5]]))
print(merge([[1, 4], [0, 0]]))

[[1, 5]]
[[0, 0], [1, 4]]

Time O(n log n) for the sort, then O(n) for the sweep · Space O(n) for the output (the sort may use extra memory as well)

Pattern to rememberWhen you see "merge overlapping ranges", sort by the start value and sweep once, comparing each start with the end of the last merged range.

Jump Game (LeetCode 55)

You are given an array nums. Each element is the maximum jump length from that index. You start at index 0. Return true if you can reach the last index. Example: [2,3,1,1,4] is true (jump 1 step to index 1, then 3 steps to the end). Example: [3,2,1,0,4] is false, because every route ends stuck at index 3, which has jump 0.

How to think about it

  1. Understand. A jump from index i can land on any index from i+1 to i+nums[i]. A single element array is already at the last index, so the answer is true. A jump of 0 is a dead end.
  2. Brute force. Recursion: from each index, try every possible jump length and recurse. This explores exponentially many paths.
  3. Find the waste. The recursion revisits the same indices again and again, and it does not need the path, only whether the end is reachable.
  4. Optimise. Track the farthest index reachable so far. Walk through the array. If the current index is beyond the farthest reach, you cannot get here, so return false. Otherwise update the reach with i + nums[i]. This is a greedy approach: taking the largest reach at each point is always safe. O(n) time and O(1) space.
  5. Dry run. nums = [3,2,1,0,4]. i=0: reach is 0, so index 0 is fine; reach = max(0, 0+3) = 3. i=1: 1 is at most 3; reach = max(3, 1+2) = 3. i=2: reach = max(3, 2+1) = 3. i=3: reach = max(3, 3+0) = 3. i=4: 4 is greater than reach 3, so return false.

Code

def can_jump(nums):
    farthest = 0  # the largest index we can reach so far
    for i, jump in enumerate(nums):
        if i > farthest:
            # Index i cannot be reached, so the last index cannot be reached either.
            return False
        farthest = max(farthest, i + jump)
    return True

print(can_jump([2, 3, 1, 1, 4]))
print(can_jump([3, 2, 1, 0, 4]))
print(can_jump([0]))

False
True

Time O(n) · Space O(1)

Pattern to rememberWhen you ask "can I reach the end?" and each position gives a range of moves, keep one running number: the farthest reach. If you are ever past that reach, stop.

TrapDo not simulate every path with recursion or BFS for this problem. It is correct but too slow, and interviewers expect the one-number greedy answer.

Candy (LeetCode 135)

There are n children standing in a line, each with a rating. You must give candies so that every child gets at least one candy, and any child with a higher rating than a neighbour gets more candies than that neighbour. Return the minimum total number of candies. Example: ratings [1,0,2] need 5 candies (2, 1, 2). Example: ratings [1,2,2] need 4 candies (1, 2, 1). The second example shows that equal neighbours have no rule between them.

How to think about it

  1. Understand. The rule applies to each pair of neighbours. A child with a higher rating than a left neighbour must have more candy than that neighbour. The same rule applies to the right neighbour. Equal ratings impose nothing. Every child needs at least one candy.
  2. Brute force. Start everyone at 1 and repeatedly scan the line, raising any child who breaks a rule, until a full scan changes nothing. This works, but it can take many scans on a long descending line, so it is slow in the worst case.
  3. Find the waste. The left rule and the right rule are separate. Fixing one can break the other, so the brute force keeps re-checking. A child's requirement depends on the left side and on the right side, and each side can be handled in one sweep.
  4. Optimise. Two passes. Pass 1, left to right: if a child rates higher than the left neighbour, give one more candy than the left neighbour. Pass 2, right to left: if a child rates higher than the right neighbour, the child needs at least one more candy than the right neighbour, so take the maximum of the current value and that new requirement. Keeping the maximum satisfies both rules at once. O(n) time and O(n) space.
  5. Dry run. ratings = [1,3,2,2,1]. Start candies = [1,1,1,1,1]. Pass 1 (left to right): index 1 rates 3 > 1, so candy = 2; index 2 rates 2 is not above 3, stays 1; index 3 rates 2 equals 2, stays 1; index 4 rates 1 is not above 2, stays 1. Now [1,2,1,1,1]. Pass 2 (right to left): index 3 rates 2 > 1 on the right, so candy = max(1, 1+1) = 2; index 2 rates 2 is not above 2 on the right, stays 1; index 1 rates 3 > 2 on the right, so candy = max(2, 1+1) = 2; index 0 rates 1 is not above 3, stays 1. Final: [1,2,1,2,1], total 7.

Code

def candy(ratings):
    n = len(ratings)
    give = [1] * n           # everyone gets at least one candy
    # Pass 1: left neighbour rule.
    for i in range(1, n):
        if ratings[i] > ratings[i - 1]:
            give[i] = give[i - 1] + 1
    # Pass 2: right neighbour rule, keeping the larger requirement.
    for i in range(n - 2, -1, -1):
        if ratings[i] > ratings[i + 1]:
            give[i] = max(give[i], give[i + 1] + 1)
    return sum(give)

print(candy([1, 0, 2]))
print(candy([1, 2, 2]))
print(candy([1, 3, 2, 2, 1]))

Time O(n) · Space O(n) for the candy array

TrapDo not overwrite the left result in pass 2. Use max, so that the left requirement is kept. Replacing it with give[i + 1] + 1 alone can break the left rule.

Pattern to rememberWhen a value depends on both neighbours, do two sweeps, one for each direction, and take the maximum of the requirements from each sweep.

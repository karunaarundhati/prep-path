Independent & mutually exclusive events

Mutually exclusive and independent sound alike, but they mean opposite things. Exclusive events cannot happen together, while independent events do not affect each other. Confusing the two is one of the most common traps in GATE DA probability questions.

31Unit 3 · Probability and statistics · Paper DA · New concept

  • Independent events
  • Telling the two apart
  • Pairwise and mutual independence for three or more events

The idea in plain words

Imagine two friends each flip a coin. Whatever one coin shows does not change the chance of the other coin showing heads. The two results are independent. Now imagine a single die roll. The result 1 and the result 2 cannot both happen on one roll. If you know the roll was 1, you know it was not 2. These two events are mutually exclusive.

Those two sentences describe different relationships. Exclusive events are about overlap: they never occur together. Independent events are about information: learning that one occurred does not change the probability of the other.

The confusing part is that exclusive events are usually not independent. If two events cannot happen together, then knowing one happened tells you a lot about the other. It tells you the other did not happen. That is a strong link, not an absence of link. This surprises most beginners, and GATE uses that surprise as a trap.

Why does this matter for GATE DA? Many questions give a probability of overlap and ask you to decide whether two events are independent. Others describe a situation as "unrelated" or "separate" and expect you to use the product rule. Knowing which property a question is testing decides the right formula.

Key ideaExclusive means the intersection is empty. Independent means the intersection has the product probability. Both are statements about P(A ∩ B), but they test different things.

Key terms

Event
A set of outcomes from a sample space, such as "the die shows an even number".
Intersection A ∩ B
The outcomes that are in both A and B. Plain meaning: A and B both happen.
Mutually exclusive (disjoint) events
Events with A ∩ B = ∅. They cannot both happen in one trial. Then P(A ∩ B) = 0.
Independent events
Events with P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B). Plain meaning: knowing one does not change the chance of the other.
Conditional probability P(B | A)
The probability of B given that A has happened. Defined as P(A ∩ B)/P(A) when P(A) > 0. Plain meaning: the share of A's outcomes that are also in B.
Pairwise independent
Every pair of events among a group is independent, taken two at a time.
Mutually independent
Every subset of the events multiplies. For three events A, B, C, this needs P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = P(A)P(B)P(C) as well as each pair.
Union A ∪ B
The outcomes in A or B or both.

Step 1: Mutually exclusive events

The meaning

Two events are mutually exclusive when they share no outcomes. In set language, A ∩ B = ∅. In probability language, P(A ∩ B) = 0. Such events are also called disjoint.

For exclusive events the addition rule has no overlap term, so it simplifies to the additivity axiom:

If A ∩ B = ∅, then P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)

Worked example: one roll of a die

Let A = "the roll is 1" and B = "the roll is 2". Find P(A ∪ B).

  1. Check exclusivity. A = {1} and B = {2} share nothing. So A ∩ B = ∅, and P(A ∩ B) = 0.
  2. Individual probabilities. P(A) = 1/6 and P(B) = 1/6.
  3. Addition. P(A ∪ B) = 1/6 + 1/6 = 2/6 = 1/3.
  4. Direct count. A ∪ B = {1, 2} has 2 of 6 equally likely outcomes, so 2/6 = 1/3.

Worked example: exclusive events with a larger probability

Let E = "the roll is even" = {2, 4, 6} and F = "the roll is 1" = {1}. Are they exclusive? Yes, because the set {2, 4, 6} does not contain 1. So P(E ∪ F) = 1/2 + 1/6 = 2/3. The four outcomes 1, 2, 4 and 6 make up the union, which is 4/6 = 2/3.

TrapExclusive events cannot both happen, so they cannot be independent unless one of them has probability zero. If P(A) > 0 and P(B) > 0 and A ∩ B = ∅, then P(A ∩ B) = 0 is not equal to P(A) P(B), which is positive. So they are dependent.

Question: Can a fair die roll be both even and a 3?

No. The even outcomes are 2, 4 and 6, and 3 is not among them. So the two events are mutually exclusive, and P(even and 3) = 0.

Question: If A and B are mutually exclusive, and P(A) = 0.3 and P(B) = 0.5, what is P(A ∪ B)?

Because they are exclusive, P(A ∪ B) = 0.3 + 0.5 = 0.8.

Step 2: Independent events

The meaning in plain words

Two events are independent when one tells you nothing about the other. Two fair dice are the classic case. Knowing the first die shows 6 tells you nothing about what the second die shows. The chance of an even second die is still 1/2.

In formulas, A and B are independent exactly when

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)

An equivalent way to say the same thing uses conditional probability. Independence means that P(B | A) = P(B). Learning A changes nothing about the chance of B. Both forms are equivalent when P(A) > 0.

Worked example: two dice

Roll two fair dice. Let A = "the first die shows 6" and B = "the second die shows an even number". Are A and B independent?

  1. Individual probabilities. P(A) = 1/6, since one of the six faces of the first die is 6. P(B) = 3/6 = 1/2, since 2, 4 and 6 are even.
  2. Product. P(A) × P(B) = 1/6 × 1/2 = 1/12.
  3. Intersection. A ∩ B means first die 6 and second die even. The outcomes are (6,2), (6,4) and (6,6), so 3 outcomes out of 36. P(A ∩ B) = 3/36 = 1/12.
  4. Compare. P(A ∩ B) = 1/12 equals P(A) × P(B) = 1/12. They are independent.

Worked example: a conditional check

Using the same events, compute P(B | A). The condition A says the first die is 6. Among the 6 outcomes with first die 6, the second die is even in 3 cases. So P(B | A) = 3/6 = 1/2. This equals P(B) = 1/2, which confirms the independence.

Independence is a claim about the situation

Sometimes a question states independence directly, as in "two machines fail independently". Then you can multiply without checking the intersection. The product rule is a consequence of the definition, so it is safe to use. Never assume independence unless the problem says it or the numbers confirm it.

GATE loves thisIf a question gives P(A), P(B) and P(A ∩ B), test independence by checking whether P(A ∩ B) equals the product. Do this comparison before any other step.

TrapIndependent events can overlap. Independence does not mean "separate" in the sense of having no common outcomes. The overlap is simply the size that the product predicts.

Question: P(A) = 0.5, P(B) = 0.4 and P(A ∩ B) = 0.2. Are A and B independent?

The product is 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.2, which equals P(A ∩ B). So yes, they are independent.

Question: If A and B are independent, are A and Bc independent?

Yes. Independence of A and B implies independence of A and the complement of B. Proof: P(A ∩ Bc) = P(A) - P(A ∩ B) = P(A) - P(A)P(B) = P(A)(1 - P(B)) = P(A)P(Bc).

Step 3: Telling the two apart

The key contrast

Put the two properties side by side. The table shows what each one says about P(A ∩ B).

PropertyTestMeaning for overlapMeaning for information
Mutually exclusiveP(A ∩ B) = 0No overlap at allKnowing A tells you B did not happen
IndependentP(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B)Overlap equals the product predictionKnowing A tells you nothing about B

Worked example: both properties, one pair of events

Roll a die. Let A = {1, 2, 3} and B = {4, 5, 6}. Then A ∩ B = ∅, so the events are mutually exclusive. Are they independent? P(A) = 1/2 and P(B) = 1/2, so the product is 1/4. But P(A ∩ B) = 0, which is not 1/4. So they are not independent. This is the usual pattern: exclusive events, with nonzero probability, are dependent.

Worked example: independent events that overlap

Roll a die. Let A = {1, 2} and B = {2, 3, 4}. They overlap at 2, so they are not exclusive. P(A) = 2/6 = 1/3, P(B) = 3/6 = 1/2, and P(A ∩ B) = 1/6. The product P(A) P(B) = 1/3 × 1/2 = 1/6 equals P(A ∩ B). So they are independent even though they overlap.

Key ideaOverlap and independence are different. Exclusive events have no overlap. Independent events have an overlap that matches the product. An overlap of zero is independent only if one of the events has probability zero.

TrapStudents often write "A and B are independent, since they have nothing in common". That is the definition of mutually exclusive, not independent. Always compute P(A ∩ B) and compare it with the product.

Question: Can two events with positive probability be both exclusive and independent?

No. If they are exclusive, P(A ∩ B) = 0, but independence would need P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B), which is positive. The two cannot hold together.

Question: Give an example of events that are neither exclusive nor independent.

On one die, let A = {1, 2, 3, 4} and B = {4, 5, 6}. They overlap at 4, so they are not exclusive. P(A) = 2/3, P(B) = 1/2, so the product is 1/3. But P(A ∩ B) = 1/6, which is not 1/3. So they are not independent.

Step 4: Pairwise and mutual independence

Why three events need more care

For three events A, B and C, you can check independence in two levels. The first level asks about pairs: is A independent of B, is A independent of C, and is B independent of C? Such a group is pairwise independent. The second level asks about all three together: must P(A ∩ B ∩ C) equal P(A) P(B) P(C)? If yes, the group is mutually independent.

Mutual independence is the stronger property. It implies pairwise independence, but not the other way around. A classic example shows the gap.

Worked example: two fair coins

Toss two fair coins. The sample space is {HH, HT, TH, TT}, each with probability 1/4. Let:

  • A = "the first coin is H" = {HH, HT}
  • B = "the second coin is H" = {HH, TH}
  • C = "both coins show the same face" = {HH, TT}
  1. Single probabilities. P(A) = P(B) = P(C) = 2/4 = 1/2.
  2. Pair A and B. A ∩ B = {HH}, so P = 1/4 = (1/2)(1/2). Independent.
  3. Pair A and C. A ∩ C = {HH}, so P = 1/4 = (1/2)(1/2). Independent.
  4. Pair B and C. B ∩ C = {HH}, so P = 1/4 = (1/2)(1/2). Independent.
  5. All three together. A ∩ B ∩ C = {HH}, so P = 1/4. But P(A) P(B) P(C) = 1/8. These differ.
  6. Conclusion. The three events are pairwise independent but not mutually independent.

The Python check below confirms each step.

from fractions import Fraction
from itertools import product

S = set(product("HT", repeat=2))
A = {s for s in S if s[0] == "H"}
B = {s for s in S if s[1] == "H"}
C = {s for s in S if s[0] == s[1]}

def P(X):
    return Fraction(len(X), len(S))

print("pairwise:", P(A & B) == P(A) * P(B), P(A & C) == P(A) * P(C), P(B & C) == P(B) * P(C))
print("triple:", P(A & B & C), P(A) * P(B) * P(C))
print("mutual:", P(A & B & C) == P(A) * P(B) * P(C))
pairwise: True True True
triple: 1/4 1/8
mutual: False

Mutual independence in general

For events A, B and C to be mutually independent, all of these must hold: each pair multiplies, and the triple multiplies. For more events the rule grows: every subset must multiply. In GATE problems you are usually told that events are mutually independent, which lets you multiply the probabilities of all of them at once.

TrapChecking only the pairs is not enough to conclude that three events are mutually independent. The triple product condition is separate and must also be checked, or stated in the question.

Question: Three machines work independently with probabilities 0.9, 0.8 and 0.5. What is the probability that all three work?

Mutual independence lets you multiply: 0.9 × 0.8 × 0.5 = 0.36.

Question: Why does pairwise independence not guarantee mutual independence?

Each pair can be independent while the three-way overlap is affected by a joint structure. In the coin example, C is determined by A and B together (both the same), so knowing A and B fixes C. The triple overlap is then not the product predicted from the three separate probabilities.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original GATE-style questions written for practice. They are not past exam questions.

Two exclusive events

Events A and B are mutually exclusive, with P(A) = 0.3 and P(B) = 0.4. Find P(A ∪ B) (NAT style, as a decimal).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The probability that A or B happens.
  2. Which idea applies. Exclusive events have no overlap, so the addition has no subtraction.
  3. Work it out. P(A ∪ B) = 0.3 + 0.4 = 0.7.
  4. Check. The result is below 1, as it must be. The complement has probability 0.3.

Answer: 0.7.

Which statements are true?

Events A and B have P(A) = 0.5, P(B) = 0.4 and P(A ∩ B) = 0.2. Which of the following are true? Select all that apply.

(A) A and B are independent. (B) A and B are mutually exclusive. (C) P(A ∪ B) = 0.7. (D) P(B | A) = 0.4.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Test each claim.
  2. Which idea applies. Independence test, exclusivity test, addition rule, conditional probability.
  3. Work it out. (A): 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.2 = P(A ∩ B), so true. (B): P(A ∩ B) = 0.2, not 0, so false. (C): 0.5 + 0.4 - 0.2 = 0.7, so true. (D): P(B | A) = 0.2/0.5 = 0.4, so true.
  4. Check. Claims (A) and (D) are the same fact in two forms, which is a useful consistency check.

Answer: (A), (C) and (D).

Overlapping but independent

A fair die is rolled. Let A = {1, 2} and B = {2, 3, 4}. Find P(A ∪ B) (NAT style, as a fraction).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The probability of A or B.
  2. Which idea applies. The overlap is {2}, so use the general addition rule.
  3. Work it out. P(A) = 1/3, P(B) = 1/2, P(A ∩ B) = 1/6. So P(A ∪ B) = 1/3 + 1/2 - 1/6 = 2/6 + 3/6 - 1/6 = 4/6 = 2/3.
  4. Check. A ∪ B = {1, 2, 3, 4}, which has 4 of 6 outcomes, giving 2/3. The events are also independent, because P(A) P(B) = 1/6 = P(A ∩ B).

Answer: 2/3.

Sum seven and first die three

Two fair dice are rolled. Let A = "the sum is 7" and B = "the first die shows 3". Find P(A ∩ B) (NAT style, as a fraction). Then decide whether A and B are independent.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The intersection probability, and an independence decision.
  2. Which idea applies. List the outcomes in both events, then compare with the product.
  3. Work it out. A has 6 outcomes: (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1). B has 6 outcomes: (3,1) to (3,6). The common outcome is (3,4) only. So P(A ∩ B) = 1/36. Also P(A) = 6/36 = 1/6 and P(B) = 1/6, so P(A) P(B) = 1/36. Equal, so they are independent.
  4. Check. Knowing the first die is 3 changes the chance that the sum is 7 from 1/6 to P(second die is 4) = 1/6, which is unchanged. So the information is irrelevant.

Answer: P(A ∩ B) = 1/36, and the events are independent.

Exclusive events and conditional probability

Events E and F satisfy P(E) = 0.3, P(F) = 0.5 and E ∩ F = ∅. Which of the following are true? Select all that apply.

(A) E and F are mutually exclusive. (B) E and F are independent. (C) P(E ∪ F) = 0.8. (D) P(E | F) = 0.3.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Test each claim.
  2. Which idea applies. Exclusivity, the product test, the addition rule, and conditional probability.
  3. Work it out. (A) is the definition, true. (B): P(E ∩ F) = 0, but P(E) P(F) = 0.15, so false. (C): 0.3 + 0.5 = 0.8, true. (D): P(E | F) = P(E ∩ F)/P(F) = 0/0.5 = 0, so false.
  4. Check. Knowing F happened makes E impossible, so P(E | F) = 0, which shows the strong dependence.

Answer: (A) and (C).

Pairwise but not mutually independent

Two fair coins are tossed. Let A = "first is H", B = "second is H" and C = "both show the same face". Which of the following are true? Select all that apply.

(A) A, B and C are pairwise independent. (B) A, B and C are mutually independent. (C) P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = 1/4. (D) P(A ∪ B) = 3/4.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Test pairwise and triple conditions, and two probabilities.
  2. Which idea applies. Check each pair with the product, then check the triple.
  3. Work it out. Each pair has intersection {HH} with probability 1/4, which equals 1/2 × 1/2, so (A) is true. The triple intersection is {HH}, with probability 1/4, but the product of the three is 1/8, so (B) is false and (C) is true. A ∪ B = {HH, HT, TH}, with probability 3/4, so (D) is true.
  4. Check. The result matches the earlier worked example, which showed pairwise but not mutual independence.

Answer: (A), (C) and (D).

Exactly one of two machines works

Machine X works with probability 0.9 and machine Y works with probability 0.8. The machines work independently. Find the probability that exactly one of them works (NAT style, as a decimal).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Exactly one works: X works and Y fails, or X fails and Y works.
  2. Which idea applies. Independence lets you multiply, and the two cases are exclusive, so you add them.
  3. Work it out. P(X works, Y fails) = 0.9 × 0.2 = 0.18. P(X fails, Y works) = 0.1 × 0.8 = 0.08. These two cases are mutually exclusive, so the answer is 0.18 + 0.08 = 0.26.
  4. Check. The four cases (both work, one works in each of two ways, neither works) have probabilities 0.72, 0.18, 0.08 and 0.02. They sum to 1.00, so the calculation is consistent.

Answer: 0.26.

Neither of two independent events

Events A and B are independent, with P(A) = 0.5 and P(B) = 0.2. Find the probability that neither A nor B occurs (NAT style, as a decimal).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. P(Ac ∩ Bc).
  2. Which idea applies. Independence passes to complements, so P(Ac ∩ Bc) = P(Ac) P(Bc). Alternatively, use De Morgan and the addition rule.
  3. Work it out. P(Ac) = 0.5 and P(Bc) = 0.8, so the product is 0.4.
  4. Check. With the addition rule: P(A ∪ B) = 0.5 + 0.2 - 0.1 = 0.6, using P(A ∩ B) = 0.5 × 0.2 = 0.1. Then P(neither) = 1 - 0.6 = 0.4, which agrees.

Answer: 0.4.

Exam-level 1: two independent components (NAT, 2 marks)

Two components in a circuit fail independently during a test. Component A fails with probability 0.3 and component B fails with probability 0.4. Find the probability that both fail, the probability that at least one fails, and the probability that neither fails. Use the rule P(A and B) = P(A) x P(B), which holds because the components are independent. Give the probability that neither fails as a decimal.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The probability that neither component fails.
  2. Which idea applies. Independence gives the product rule for "both". The addition rule and the complement rule then give the other two.
  3. Work it out. P(both fail) = 0.3 x 0.4 = 0.12. P(at least one fails) = 0.3 + 0.4 - 0.12 = 0.58. P(neither fails) = 1 - 0.58 = 0.42.
  4. Check. The Python check uses exact fractions. A direct count, P(neither) = 0.7 x 0.6 = 0.42, must agree.
from fractions import Fraction as F

pA, pB = F(3, 10), F(2, 5)
inter = pA * pB
union = pA + pB - inter
print("P(A and B) =", inter, "=", float(inter))
print("P(A or B) =", union, "=", float(union))
print("P(neither) =", 1 - union, "=", float(1 - union))
P(A and B) = 3/25 = 0.12
P(A or B) = 29/50 = 0.58
P(neither) = 21/50 = 0.42

Answer: 0.42.

Exam-level 2: exclusive or independent (MSQ, 2 marks)

Let A and B be events with P(A) = 0.3 and P(B) = 0.4. Which of the following statements are true? Select all that apply.

(A) If A and B are mutually exclusive, they cannot be independent. (B) If A and B are independent, P(A and B) = 0.12. (C) If A and B are mutually exclusive, P(A or B) = 0.7. (D) If A and B are mutually exclusive, P(A given B) = P(A).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which of the four statements hold, given the two different assumptions.
  2. Which idea applies. Mutually exclusive means P(A and B) = 0. Independent means P(A and B) = P(A) x P(B).
  3. Work it out. (A) is true: if exclusive, P(A and B) = 0, but independence needs 0.3 x 0.4 = 0.12, so they cannot be both. (B) is true by the product rule. (C) is true: 0.3 + 0.4 - 0 = 0.7. (D) is false: if exclusive, P(A given B) = P(A and B)/P(B) = 0, which is not 0.3.
  4. Check. The Python check prints each quantity under the two assumptions.
from fractions import Fraction as F

pA, pB = F(3, 10), F(2, 5)
print("product if independent:", pA * pB)
print("P(A and B) if exclusive:", F(0))
print("P(A or B) if exclusive:", pA + pB)
print("P(A given B) if exclusive:", F(0) / pB)
product if independent: 3/25
P(A and B) if exclusive: 0
P(A or B) if exclusive: 7/10
P(A given B) if exclusive: 0

Answer: (A), (B) and (C). (D) is wrong, because P(A given B) is 0 when the events are exclusive, not 0.3.

Exam-level 3: pairwise but not mutual independence (NAT, 2 marks)

Two fair coins are tossed. Let A be the event that the first coin shows heads, B the event that the second coin shows heads, and C the event that exactly one coin shows heads. Check whether each pair of these events is independent. Then compute P(A and B and C) minus P(A) x P(B) x P(C), and give the difference as a decimal. This tests whether the three events are mutually independent.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Whether the pairs are independent, and the difference for the triple.
  2. Which idea applies. Pairwise independence is checked pair by pair. Mutual independence also needs the triple product rule to hold.
  3. Work it out. The four equally likely outcomes are HH, HT, TH and TT. Each of P(A), P(B), P(C) is 1/2. Each pair, such as A and C, has probability 1/4 of both happening (HT only), which equals (1/2)(1/2). So the pairs are independent. But A, B and C all happen would need exactly one head and two heads at the same time, which is impossible, so P(A and B and C) = 0. The product is (1/2)^3 = 1/8. The difference is 0 - 1/8 = -0.125.
  4. Check. The Python check lists the outcomes and computes every quantity with exact fractions.
from itertools import product
from fractions import Fraction as F

S = list(product('HT', repeat=2))

def P(ev):
    return F(sum(1 for s in S if ev(s)), len(S))

A = lambda s: s[0] == 'H'
B = lambda s: s[1] == 'H'
C = lambda s: (s[0] == 'H') != (s[1] == 'H')
print("pairwise A and C:", P(lambda s: A(s) and C(s)), "vs", P(A) * P(C))
print("pairwise B and C:", P(lambda s: B(s) and C(s)), "vs", P(B) * P(C))
print("pairwise A and B:", P(lambda s: A(s) and B(s)), "vs", P(A) * P(B))
print("triple:", P(lambda s: A(s) and B(s) and C(s)), "vs", P(A) * P(B) * P(C))
print("difference:", P(lambda s: A(s) and B(s) and C(s)) - P(A) * P(B) * P(C))
pairwise A and C: 1/4 vs 1/4
pairwise B and C: 1/4 vs 1/4
pairwise A and B: 1/4 vs 1/4
triple: 0 vs 1/8
difference: -1/8

Answer: -0.125. The pairs are independent, but the three events are not mutually independent.

Exam-level 4: two sensors, one fault (NAT, 2 marks)

A machine has two independent fault sensors, X and Y. When a fault is present, sensor X raises an alarm with probability 0.9, and sensor Y raises an alarm with probability 0.8, and the two sensors act independently. Given that a fault is present, find the probability that exactly one sensor raises an alarm, and the probability that at least one does. Give the probability that exactly one raises an alarm as a decimal.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The probability of exactly one alarm, given a fault.
  2. Which idea applies. Independence gives products. "Exactly one" is two disjoint cases that are added.
  3. Work it out. Exactly one alarm: X alarms and Y does not, or Y alarms and X does not. The probability is 0.9 x 0.2 + 0.1 x 0.8 = 0.18 + 0.08 = 0.26. At least one alarm is the complement of neither: 1 - 0.1 x 0.2 = 0.98.
  4. Check. The Python check uses exact fractions, and the two cases (exactly one, and neither) must add to 1 minus the probability of both alarms.
from fractions import Fraction

px, py = Fraction(9, 10), Fraction(8, 10)
exactly_one = px * (1 - py) + (1 - px) * py
at_least_one = 1 - (1 - px) * (1 - py)
print("exactly one:", exactly_one, "=", float(exactly_one))
print("at least one:", at_least_one, "=", float(at_least_one))
exactly one: 13/50 = 0.26
at least one: 49/50 = 0.98

Answer: 0.26.

A ∩ B, the outcomes that are in both events.
Mutually exclusive
A ∩ B = ∅, so P(A ∩ B) = 0. The events cannot both happen.
Addition for exclusive events
P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B).
Independent
P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B). Knowing one tells nothing about the other.
Conditional probability
P(B | A) = P(A ∩ B)/P(A), when P(A) > 0.
Independence in conditional form
P(B | A) = P(B).
Exclusive and independent
Both hold only if one event has probability zero.
Overlap and independence
Independent events can overlap, as long as the overlap equals the product.
Pairwise independence
Every pair of events in a group is independent.
Mutual independence
Every subset multiplies. For three events this includes the triple product.
Complements and independence
If A and B are independent, A and Bc are independent too.
General addition rule
P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A ∩ B).

Remember these

  • Exclusive means no overlap. Independent means the overlap equals the product.
  • Exclusive events with positive probability are never independent.
  • Always compute P(A ∩ B) and compare it with P(A) P(B) before deciding.
  • Independent events can overlap.
  • Pairwise independence does not guarantee mutual independence for three or more events.
  • Multiply for "and" with independent events, and add for "or" with exclusive events.

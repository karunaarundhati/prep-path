Design for dynamic loading

Many machine parts never feel a steady load. A shaft turns, a spring is compressed again and again, and a robot joint reverses its torque many times a second. Under these fluctuating loads, a part can break at a stress far below its static strength. This page explains fatigue from zero, then builds the endurance limit, stress concentration, the Goodman and Soderberg criteria, and the sizing of parts with GATE-style numbers.

The idea in plain words

Bend a paper clip back and forth. Each bend is small, and the clip looks fine at first. After enough bends, it snaps. Each bend did a little damage, and the damage added up. This is fatigue: failure caused by repeated loading, even when no single load is large enough to break the part.

Static design, from the previous page, asks whether a steady load is too big. Dynamic design asks a harder question: what happens when the load goes up and down many times? A shaft carrying a gear, a connecting rod in an engine and a robot joint all see such loads. Their designs must allow for fatigue, or the part may fail after months of safe-looking service.

The key idea is that the strength we need is no longer the yield strength. It is the endurance limit, the stress amplitude a part can take for a very large number of cycles. In GATE, this topic appears in the Robotics and Automation (RA) paper through shafts and joints, and in the machine design material.

Key terms

Each term gives its plain meaning first, then the precise definition.

Fatigue
Progressive damage and fracture caused by repeated or fluctuating stress. Plain meaning: wear from many small loads.
Fluctuating stress
A stress that changes with time, such as a sine wave between two values.
Maximum stress (σmax) and minimum stress (σmin)
The largest and smallest values of the stress in one cycle, in MPa.
Mean stress (σm)
The average of the maximum and minimum stresses: σm = (σmax + σmin)/2.
Alternating stress (σa)
Half the range of the stress: σa = (σmax - σmin)/2. Plain meaning: how far the stress swings either side of the mean.
Stress ratio (R)
The minimum stress divided by the maximum stress, R = σmin/σmax.
Endurance limit (Se)
The alternating stress below which a steel part can survive an effectively unlimited number of cycles. Also written Se for the corrected value.
Uncorrected endurance limit (Se')
The endurance limit of a standard polished test specimen, before the correction factors are applied.
Marin factors (ka, kb, kc, kd, ke)
Correction factors for surface finish, size, load type, temperature and reliability. Each is less than or equal to about 1 in ordinary parts.
Ultimate strength (Sut) and yield strength (Sy)
The static strengths of the material, in MPa.
Stress concentration factor (Kt)
The ratio of the peak stress at a notch or hole to the nominal stress.
Fatigue stress concentration factor (Kf)
The reduced stress concentration effect that applies to fatigue, Kf = 1 + q(Kt - 1).
Notch sensitivity (q)
A number from 0 to 1 that says how much a material is affected by a notch. Zero means no effect, one means the full effect.
Goodman criterion
A straight line from the endurance limit on the alternating axis to the ultimate strength on the mean axis.
Soderberg criterion
A straight line from the endurance limit to the yield strength. It is more conservative than Goodman.
Factor of safety in fatigue (n)
The factor by which both stress components can grow before the criterion is reached.
S-N diagram
A plot of alternating stress against the number of cycles to failure, on logarithmic scales.
Infinite life
Survival for a very large number of cycles, usually taken as 106 or more for steels, when the alternating stress is below Se.
Miner's rule
A rule that adds the fractions of life used by each stress block. Failure occurs when the sum reaches 1.

TrapThe mean stress does not cause fatigue damage on its own in the same way as the alternating stress. But it changes how much alternating stress a part can take, which is why the Goodman and Soderberg lines include it.

Fluctuating stress: mean and alternating components

Why we split the stress into two parts

A stress that goes up and down can be described by two numbers: where it sits on average, and how far it swings. The average is the mean stress, and the swing is the alternating stress. Once we have these two numbers, we can compare any cycle with the strength of the material.

   stress
     ^
     |  sigma_max  ..............................
     |            /\        /\        /\
     |  sigma_m  ----/--\----/--\----/--\---------  (mean line)
     |          /      \  /      \  /      \
     |  sigma_min ......\/........\/........\/....
     +-----------------------------------------------> time
              one cycle: sigma_a is half the distance from max to min

The formulas

σm = (σmax + σmin)/2 and σa = (σmax - σmin)/2

Read each symbol. σmax is the largest stress in the cycle, σmin is the smallest, σm is the mean, and σa is the alternating part. Adding the two gives back the maximum, and subtracting gives back the minimum:

σmax = σm + σa, σmin = σm - σa

Worked example

A shaft sees a bending stress that varies between +120 MPa and -40 MPa. Find the mean stress, the alternating stress and the stress ratio.

Step 1. Mean stress: σm = (120 + (-40))/2 = 80/2 = 40 MPa.

Step 2. Alternating stress: σa = (120 - (-40))/2 = 160/2 = 80 MPa.

Step 3. Stress ratio: R = -40/120 = -1/3.

Step 4. Check: σm + σa = 40 + 80 = 120 = σmax, and σm - σa = -40 = σmin. Both checks pass.

smax, smin = 120.0, -40.0
sm = (smax + smin) / 2
sa = (smax - smin) / 2
R = smin / smax
print(f"mean = {sm:.1f} MPa, alternating = {sa:.1f} MPa")
print(f"R = {R:.4f}")
print("check:", sm + sa == smax and sm - sa == smin)
mean = 40.0 MPa, alternating = 80.0 MPa
R = -0.3333
check: True

Key ideaEvery fluctuating stress is a mean plus an alternating part. Fatigue design compares these two parts with the material's strength.

Self-check: a stress goes from 0 to 100 MPa. What are σm and σa?

σm = 50 MPa and σa = 50 MPa. The stress ratio R is 0, which is called repeated loading.

Self-check: a stress goes from -60 to +60 MPa. What is R?

R = -1, and the mean is zero. This is called fully reversed loading, which is the simplest fatigue case.

Endurance limit and the corrected endurance limit

What the endurance limit is

Take a polished steel test bar and bend it back and forth with a fixed alternating stress. Count the cycles until it breaks. A large stress breaks it after a few thousand cycles. A smaller stress takes millions of cycles. For many steels, there is a stress below which the bar does not break at all, however many cycles are applied. This stress is the endurance limit.

The results are often plotted as an S-N diagram, with alternating stress on the vertical axis and cycles to failure on the horizontal axis (both on logarithmic scales). For steel, the curve levels off at high cycle counts, and the level it reaches is the endurance limit.

The uncorrected endurance limit

For a standard test specimen, the textbook estimate for steel is

Se' ≈ 0.5 Sut, for steel with Sut up to about 1400 MPa

This is a useful rule of thumb. GATE questions usually give Se' directly, or give it through this rule.

The corrected endurance limit

A real part differs from the test specimen. Its surface may be rougher, it may be larger, it may carry a different type of load, or it may run hot. Each difference is corrected with a Marin factor:

Se = ka × kb × kc × kd × ke × Se'

Each factor is a number that is usually below 1. The surface factor ka accounts for finish, the size factor kb for diameter, the load factor kc for bending, axial or torsional loading, the temperature factor kd for heat, and the reliability factor ke for the chance of failure.

TipIn GATE questions, the Marin factors are usually given. Multiply them all together with Se' and do not try to recalculate them from formulas unless the question asks you to.

Worked example

A steel part has Sut = 800 MPa. Use Se' = 0.5 Sut. The surface factor is ka = 0.8, the size factor kb = 0.9, the load factor kc = 1, the temperature factor kd = 1 and the reliability factor ke = 0.87. Find Se.

Step 1. Uncorrected: Se' = 0.5 × 800 = 400 MPa.

Step 2. Product of factors: 0.8 × 0.9 × 1 × 1 × 0.87 = 0.6264.

Step 3. Corrected: Se = 0.6264 × 400 = 250.56 MPa.

Step 4. Check: the corrected value is smaller than the test value, which is expected, since every factor is at most 1.

Sut = 800.0
Se_prime = 0.5 * Sut
factors = [0.8, 0.9, 1.0, 1.0, 0.87]
Se = Se_prime
for f in factors:
    Se *= f
print(f"S_e' = {Se_prime:.0f} MPa")
print(f"S_e = {Se:.2f} MPa")
S_e' = 400 MPa
S_e = 250.56 MPa
Self-check: why is the corrected endurance limit always smaller than the test value in this kind of problem?

Each Marin factor is at most 1, so their product reduces the test value. Real parts are rougher, larger or more variable than the test bar.

Self-check: does a higher ultimate strength always give a higher endurance limit?

For steel, yes, through the rule Se' = 0.5 Sut. The Marin factors may still reduce it, so the final value depends on the part's condition.

Stress concentration and the fatigue factor Kf

Why holes and notches matter

A hole, groove or sharp corner in a part makes the stress flow around it, and the stress at the edge of the notch can be much larger than the average stress. This peak is the source of most fatigue cracks. The ratio of the peak stress to the nominal stress is the theoretical stress concentration factor Kt.

     nominal stress (average over the section)
     ----->  ----->  ----->
     ____________________________
    |____________   ____________|     a notch or hole in a bar
                 \_/                   stress at the notch edge
                                       is Kt times the nominal stress

Fatigue stress concentration

Ductile materials can yield a little at the notch, which relieves the peak. Fatigue is less sensitive to the peak than the theoretical factor suggests. We use a reduced factor, the fatigue stress concentration factor Kf, given by

Kf = 1 + q(Kt - 1), where 0 ≤ q ≤ 1

Here q is the notch sensitivity. When q = 0, Kf = 1 and the notch has no effect. When q = 1, Kf = Kt, the full effect. For steels, q is often between 0.5 and 0.9, and the value is usually given in the question.

Key ideaMultiply the alternating stress by Kf. In most textbook fatigue designs, the mean stress is also multiplied by Kf as a conservative step.

Worked example

A shaft has a shoulder with Kt = 2.5 and notch sensitivity q = 0.8. Find Kf. If the nominal alternating stress is 60 MPa, find the design alternating stress.

Step 1. Kf = 1 + 0.8 × (2.5 - 1) = 1 + 0.8 × 1.5 = 1 + 1.2 = 2.2.

Step 2. Design alternating stress: σa = Kf × 60 = 2.2 × 60 = 132 MPa.

Step 3. Check: Kf is between 1 and Kt, here 2.2 lies between 1 and 2.5, as required.

Kt, q, sa_nom = 2.5, 0.8, 60.0
Kf = 1 + q * (Kt - 1)
print(f"Kf = {Kf:.2f}")
print(f"design alternating stress = {Kf * sa_nom:.1f} MPa")
Kf = 2.20
design alternating stress = 132.0 MPa

TrapDo not use Kt in the fatigue check when q is given. Use Kf. Also do not apply Kf to the static yield check of a ductile part, where the notch is relieved by yielding.

Self-check: q = 0 for a material. What is Kf for a notch with Kt = 3?

Kf = 1. The material does not feel the notch.

Self-check: is Kf ever larger than Kt?

No. With q between 0 and 1, Kf lies between 1 and Kt.

Goodman and Soderberg criteria for combined mean and alternating stress

The problem of a mean stress

An alternating stress is safe on its own if it is below Se. But a part that already carries a large mean stress fails earlier. Engineers handle this with a failure line on a diagram that has mean stress on one axis and alternating stress on the other.

   alternating stress sigma_a
     |
  S_e +--.
     |    '--.
     |        '--.      Goodman line: from (0, S_e) to (S_ut, 0)
     |            '--.
     |                '--.
     |                    '--.   Soderberg line: from (0, S_e) to (S_y, 0),
     |                        '--+   lies below Goodman
     +-------------------+----------+------->  mean stress sigma_m
                       S_y        S_ut

The safe region lies under each line. A design point (σm, σa) is safe if it falls below the line.

The Goodman criterion

The Goodman line joins the endurance limit on the alternating axis with the ultimate strength on the mean axis. The factor of safety is

1/n = σa/Se + σm/Sut, so n = 1/(σa/Se + σm/Sut)

Read the symbols: Se is the corrected endurance limit, Sut is the ultimate strength, and n is the factor of safety. The idea is that the stresses can be multiplied by n before they reach the line.

The Soderberg criterion

The Soderberg line joins the endurance limit with the yield strength. It is more conservative, because yield is always lower than ultimate strength:

1/n = σa/Se + σm/Sy

Key ideaGoodman uses ultimate strength and Soderberg uses yield strength for the mean stress. Since Sy is less than Sut, Soderberg always gives a smaller factor of safety for the same stresses.

Worked example

A part has σa = 80 MPa, σm = 40 MPa, Se = 200 MPa, Sut = 500 MPa and Sy = 350 MPa. Find the factor of safety by both criteria.

Step 1. Goodman: σa/Se + σm/Sut = 80/200 + 40/500 = 0.40 + 0.08 = 0.48. So n = 1/0.48 = 2.08.

Step 2. Soderberg: σa/Se + σm/Sy = 0.40 + 40/350 = 0.40 + 0.1143 = 0.5143. So n = 1/0.5143 = 1.94.

Step 3. Check: Soderberg gives the smaller factor, 1.94 compared with 2.08. This matches the rule that Soderberg is more conservative.

sa, sm = 80.0, 40.0
Se, Sut, Sy = 200.0, 500.0, 350.0
n_goodman = 1 / (sa / Se + sm / Sut)
n_soderberg = 1 / (sa / Se + sm / Sy)
print(f"Goodman n = {n_goodman:.2f}")
print(f"Soderberg n = {n_soderberg:.2f}")
Goodman n = 2.08
Soderberg n = 1.94
Self-check: a part has σm = 0. Which criterion gives n = Se/σa?

Both do. With σm = 0 the mean term vanishes, so 1/n = σa/Se in either criterion.

Self-check: a point lies exactly on the Goodman line. What is n?

n = 1. The part is on the edge of failure by this criterion.

Sizing a component under fluctuating load

From stresses to a size

Most GATE questions ask for a size, not a factor of safety. The procedure is to write the stresses in terms of the unknown size, then put them into the criterion. For a rod of area A carrying an axial load that fluctuates between Fmin and Fmax, the forces are Fa = (Fmax - Fmin)/2 and Fm = (Fmax + Fmin)/2. With stress concentration applied:

σa = Kf Fa/A and σm = Kf Fm/A

Substitute into the Goodman criterion with 1/n on the left. Then solve for A:

A = n Kf (Fa/Se + Fm/Sut) (Goodman)
A = n Kf (Fa/Se + Fm/Sy) (Soderberg)

The area is then converted to a diameter with d = √(4A/π) for a round rod.

Worked example

A round rod carries an axial load that varies from 10 kN to 30 kN. The corrected endurance limit is Se = 300 MPa, the ultimate strength is Sut = 600 MPa, and the yield strength is Sy = 400 MPa. Use Kf = 1.5 and n = 2 with the Goodman criterion. Find the required area and diameter.

Step 1. Forces: Fa = (30 - 10)/2 = 10 kN and Fm = (30 + 10)/2 = 20 kN.

Step 2. Bracket term: Fa/Se + Fm/Sut = 10 000/300 + 20 000/600 = 33.33 + 33.33 = 66.67 mm².

Step 3. Area: A = n Kf × 66.67 = 2 × 1.5 × 66.67 = 200 mm².

Step 4. Diameter: d = √(4 × 200/π) = √254.6 = 15.96 mm, so use 16 mm.

Step 5. Check: at A = 200 mm² and Kf = 1.5, σa = 1.5 × 10 000/200 = 75 MPa, and σm = 1.5 × 20 000/200 = 150 MPa. Goodman: 75/300 + 150/600 = 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5 = 1/n with n = 2. Correct.

import math

Fa, Fm = 10000.0, 20000.0       # N
Se, Sut = 300.0, 600.0          # MPa
n, Kf = 2.0, 1.5

A = n * Kf * (Fa / Se + Fm / Sut)
d = math.sqrt(4 * A / math.pi)
print(f"area = {A:.0f} mm^2")
print(f"diameter = {d:.2f} mm")
sa, sm = Kf * Fa / A, Kf * Fm / A
print(f"check: 1/n = {sa / Se + sm / Sut:.3f}")
area = 200 mm^2
diameter = 15.96 mm
check: 1/n = 0.500

TrapUse the mean and alternating forces, not the maximum force, in the sizing formula. Using Fmax in place of Fa gives a far too large part.

Self-check: the mean force is zero (fully reversed). Which term disappears from the sizing formula?

The mean term Fm/Sut is zero, so A = n Kf Fa/Se. The strength criterion then depends only on the endurance limit.

Self-check: why do we multiply the forces by Kf when sizing?

Because the notch raises the local stress. The sizing equation must make the local stress, not the nominal stress, safe against fatigue.

Cumulative fatigue damage (Miner's rule)

Life is used up in pieces

A machine rarely sees one stress level for its whole life. A crane lifts heavy loads on some days and light loads on others. Miner's rule adds up the damage from each block of cycles. For a block of ni cycles at a stress that would cause failure after Ni cycles, the fraction of life used is ni/Ni. The total damage is

D = Σ ni/Ni

Failure is predicted when D = 1. Here ni is the number of cycles applied at stress level i, and Ni is the number of cycles that stress level alone would cause to fail. The rule is simple and useful, though it is only an approximation because it ignores the order of the loads.

Key ideaEach block uses up the fraction n/N of the life. When the fractions add to 1, the part fails.

Worked example

A part is loaded in two blocks. Block 1 has 10 000 cycles at a stress that gives N1 = 100 000 cycles to failure. Block 2 has 20 000 cycles at a higher stress that gives N2 = 40 000 cycles to failure. Find the damage and the remaining life.

Step 1. Damage from block 1: 10 000/100 000 = 0.1.

Step 2. Damage from block 2: 20 000/40 000 = 0.5.

Step 3. Total damage: D = 0.1 + 0.5 = 0.6, so 60 per cent of the life is used.

Step 4. Remaining life fraction: 1 - 0.6 = 0.4. If block 2 continues at the same stress, the part fails after 0.4 × 40 000 = 16 000 more cycles of block 2.

blocks = [(10000, 100000), (20000, 40000)]   # (n_i, N_i)
D = sum(n / N for n, N in blocks)
print(f"damage D = {D:.2f}")
print(f"remaining life fraction = {1 - D:.2f}")
print(f"more block-2 cycles = {(1 - D) * 40000:.0f}")
damage D = 0.60
remaining life fraction = 0.40
more block-2 cycles = 16000

TrapMiner's rule uses the fraction n/N, not the number of cycles. Adding 10 000 and 20 000 cycles to get 30 000 and comparing with N gives the wrong answer.

Self-check: a part has D = 0.85 after a loading history. Is it safe to continue?

The rule predicts survival because D is below 1, but 85 per cent of the life is used. A designer would check the reserve and inspect the part before continuing.

Self-check: what is the damage if 1000 cycles are applied at a stress with N = 1000?

D = 1000/1000 = 1, so the part fails at that stress.

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original GATE-style problems for practice, not real past paper questions. Work each one on paper before reading the answer.

Mean and alternating stress

A component is cycled between a maximum stress of 100 MPa and a minimum stress of 20 MPa. Find the mean stress and the alternating stress in MPa.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. σm and σa in MPa.
  2. Which idea applies. σm is the average of the extremes, and σa is half the range.
  3. Work it out. σm = (100 + 20)/2 = 60 MPa. σa = (100 - 20)/2 = 40 MPa.
  4. Check. 60 + 40 = 100 and 60 - 40 = 20, which give back the maximum and minimum.

Answer: mean 60 MPa, alternating 40 MPa.

Corrected endurance limit

A steel part has Sut = 600 MPa. Use Se' = 0.5 Sut. The factors are ka = 0.8, kb = 0.9, kc = 1.0, kd = 1.0 and ke = 0.9. Find Se in MPa (numerical answer, one decimal place).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The corrected endurance limit Se.
  2. Which idea applies. Se = (product of Marin factors) × Se'.
  3. Work it out. Se' = 300 MPa. Product = 0.8 × 0.9 × 1 × 1 × 0.9 = 0.648. Se = 0.648 × 300 = 194.4 MPa.
  4. Check. The answer is below Se', as it must be when all factors are at most 1.
factors = [0.8, 0.9, 1.0, 1.0, 0.9]
Se = 0.5 * 600.0
for f in factors:
    Se *= f
print(f"S_e = {Se:.1f} MPa")
S_e = 194.4 MPa

Answer: 194.4 MPa.

Which statements are true?

Consider these statements about fatigue design.

(A) Stress concentration increases only the mean stress, not the alternating stress.
(B) The Goodman line joins the endurance limit on the alternating axis with the ultimate strength on the mean axis.
(C) The Soderberg criterion gives a smaller factor of safety than Goodman for the same stresses and strengths.
(D) In Miner's rule, failure is predicted when the sum of ni/Ni reaches 1.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true (multiple selection).
  2. Which idea applies. Kf multiplies the alternating stress, the Goodman and Soderberg lines, and Miner's rule.
  3. Work it out. (A) is false, because Kf is applied to the alternating stress. (B) is true. (C) is true, because Sy is less than Sut, which makes the mean term larger. (D) is true.
  4. Check. The worked example gave 2.08 for Goodman and 1.94 for Soderberg, which confirms (C).

Answer: (B), (C) and (D).

Factor of safety for a mean-loaded part

A part has σa = 60 MPa and σm = 50 MPa. Its Se = 200 MPa and Sut = 500 MPa. Using the Goodman criterion, find the factor of safety (numerical answer, one decimal place).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. n by Goodman.
  2. Which idea applies. 1/n = σa/Se + σm/Sut.
  3. Work it out. 60/200 = 0.30 and 50/500 = 0.10. Sum = 0.40. n = 2.5.
  4. Check. The stresses are a fifth of the strengths in sum, so n = 2.5 is a sensible margin.
sa, sm, Se, Sut = 60.0, 50.0, 200.0, 500.0
print(f"n = {1 / (sa / Se + sm / Sut):.1f}")
n = 2.5

Answer: 2.5.

Remaining cycles after two blocks

A component first sees 5000 cycles at a stress that would fail it after 50 000 cycles. It then sees 2000 cycles at a higher stress that would fail it after 10 000 cycles. Using Miner's rule, how many more cycles at the first stress can it take before failure (numerical answer)?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Extra cycles at the first stress.
  2. Which idea applies. D = Σ n/N. The remaining fraction 1 - D is spent on the first stress.
  3. Work it out. D = 5000/50 000 + 2000/10 000 = 0.1 + 0.2 = 0.3. Remaining fraction = 0.7. Extra cycles = 0.7 × 50 000 = 35 000.
  4. Check. Using all 35 000 cycles at the first stress adds 35 000/50 000 = 0.7, which makes the total exactly 1.
blocks = [(5000, 50000), (2000, 10000)]
D = sum(n / N for n, N in blocks)
print(f"extra cycles at first stress = {(1 - D) * 50000:.0f}")
extra cycles at first stress = 35000

Diameter of a rod under a fluctuating load

A round rod carries an axial load that varies from 5 kN to 25 kN. The corrected endurance limit is Se = 300 MPa, the ultimate strength is Sut = 600 MPa, and Kf = 1.5. Use the Goodman criterion with n = 2. Find the diameter of the rod in mm (numerical answer, two decimal places).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The rod diameter in mm.
  2. Which idea applies. Fa = (Fmax - Fmin)/2 and Fm = (Fmax + Fmin)/2. Then A = nKf(Fa/Se + Fm/Sut).
  3. Work it out. Fa = 10 kN and Fm = 15 kN. The bracket is 10 000/300 + 15 000/600 = 33.33 + 25 = 58.33 mm². A = 2 × 1.5 × 58.33 = 175 mm². d = √(4 × 175/π) = 14.93 mm.
  4. Check. At A = 175 mm², σa = 1.5 × 10 000/175 = 85.7 MPa and σm = 1.5 × 15 000/175 = 128.6 MPa. Goodman: 85.7/300 + 128.6/600 = 0.286 + 0.214 = 0.5, so n = 2.
import math

Fa, Fm = 10000.0, 15000.0
Se, Sut, n, Kf = 300.0, 600.0, 2.0, 1.5
A = n * Kf * (Fa / Se + Fm / Sut)
print(f"area = {A:.1f} mm^2")
print(f"diameter = {math.sqrt(4 * A / math.pi):.2f} mm")
area = 175.0 mm^2
diameter = 14.93 mm

Answer: 14.93 mm.

Soderberg check of the same rod

Repeat the previous problem with the Soderberg criterion instead of Goodman. The yield strength is Sy = 400 MPa, and all other data are the same (loads 5 kN to 25 kN, Se = 300 MPa, Kf = 1.5, n = 2). Find the diameter in mm (numerical answer, two decimal places) and say whether it is larger or smaller than with Goodman.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The Soderberg diameter and a comparison.
  2. Which idea applies. A = nKf(Fa/Se + Fm/Sy).
  3. Work it out. The bracket is 10 000/300 + 15 000/400 = 33.33 + 37.5 = 70.83 mm². A = 3 × 70.83 = 212.5 mm². d = √(4 × 212.5/π) = 16.45 mm.
  4. Check. 16.45 mm is larger than 14.93 mm. Soderberg uses the smaller yield strength for the mean stress, so it needs more material, which fits its conservative nature.
import math

Fa, Fm = 10000.0, 15000.0
Se, Sy, n, Kf = 300.0, 400.0, 2.0, 1.5
A = n * Kf * (Fa / Se + Fm / Sy)
print(f"area = {A:.1f} mm^2")
print(f"diameter = {math.sqrt(4 * A / math.pi):.2f} mm")
area = 212.5 mm^2
diameter = 16.45 mm

Answer: 16.45 mm, which is larger than the Goodman value of 14.93 mm.

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Goodman line (NAT, 2 marks)

σa = 100 MPa and σm = 150 MPa. Se = 250 MPa and Sut = 600 MPa. Find the factor of safety by the Goodman criterion.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. n from σa/Se + σm/Sut = 1/n.
  2. Which idea applies. Modified Goodman line.
  3. Work it out. 0.4 + 0.25 = 0.65. n = 1.54.
  4. Check. Soderberg would give a lower value.

Answer: about 1.54.

Exam-level 2: Soderberg (NAT, 2 marks)

Same stresses, with Sy = 400 MPa. Find the Soderberg factor of safety, correct to 2 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. 1/n = σa/Se + σm/Sy.
  2. Which idea applies. Soderberg uses yield instead of ultimate.
  3. Work it out. 0.4 + 0.375 = 0.775. n = 1.29.
  4. Check. Lower than Goodman, as expected.

Answer: about 1.29.

Exam-level 3: Endurance limit modifiers (NAT, 2 marks)

Se′ = 300 MPa. Surface factor 0.8, size factor 0.85, reliability factor 0.9. Find Se.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The corrected endurance limit.
  2. Which idea applies. Multiply the factors.
  3. Work it out. 300 · 0.8 · 0.85 · 0.9 = 183.6 MPa.
  4. Check. Real parts are much weaker in fatigue than polished test bars.

Answer: 183.6 MPa.

Summary

Fatigue
Failure caused by repeated loading, often below the static strength.
Mean stress
σm = (σmax + σmin)/2, the average of the cycle.
Alternating stress
σa = (σmax - σmin)/2, half the swing of the cycle.
Stress ratio
R = σmin/σmax, which classifies the type of loading.
Endurance limit
The alternating stress below which steel survives indefinitely, Se' = 0.5 Sut as a rule.
Marin factors
ka, kb, kc, kd, ke correct the test value for a real part.
Corrected endurance limit
Se = (product of Marin factors) × Se'.
Stress concentration
Kt is the peak-to-nominal stress ratio at a notch.
Fatigue stress concentration
Kf = 1 + q(Kt - 1), with q the notch sensitivity.
Goodman criterion
1/n = σa/Se + σm/Sut.
Soderberg criterion
1/n = σa/Se + σm/Sy, more conservative.
Goodman sizing
A = nKf(Fa/Se + Fm/Sut) for a rod.
Soderberg sizing
A = nKf(Fa/Se + Fm/Sy) for a rod.
S-N diagram
A plot of alternating stress against cycles to failure.
Infinite life
Survival at very many cycles when the alternating stress is below Se.
Miner's rule
D = Σ ni/Ni, and failure is predicted at D = 1.

Remember these

  • Split every fluctuating stress into a mean part and an alternating part.
  • Fatigue design uses the endurance limit, not the yield strength.
  • Multiply the alternating stress by Kf for notches, and keep Kf between 1 and Kt.
  • Goodman uses Sut for the mean stress, and Soderberg uses Sy, so Soderberg is the safer line.
  • When sizing, use the alternating and mean forces, not the maximum force.
  • Miner's rule adds fractions of life, and failure is at a total of 1.

Design of bolted, riveted & welded joints

Almost every machine is held together by bolts, rivets or welds. This page shows how each joint carries load, how to size it, and which failure you must check first, because GATE Robotics and Automation questions on joints are usually short calculations with clear steps.

The idea in plain words

Welcome to joints. A joint is the place where two parts of a machine are fixed together, and its job is to carry load from one part to the other without failing. Think of a chain of people passing a bucket. Each person is a link. If one link is weak, the whole chain fails at that link. A joint is the same: the joint is only as strong as its weakest failure mode.

There are three common joint types. A bolted joint uses a threaded bolt and nut, and it can be taken apart again. A riveted joint uses a metal pin with a head, closed at the other end by hammering or pressing, and it is permanent. A welded joint melts the edges of two parts so that they fuse into one piece, and it is also permanent.

Why does this matter for GATE? In the Machine Design and CIM part of the Robotics and Automation paper, joint questions usually ask for one of three things: the load in a bolt, the efficiency of a riveted plate, or the size of a weld. Each of these is a few lines of arithmetic once you know which formula and which failure mode to use. The most common mistake is to check only one failure mode. Good students check all of them and take the weakest.

We will go slowly. Each section defines the words first, then gives a formula, then shows a small example with every step written out.

Key terms

Read this list once now. Come back to it when a word feels unfamiliar.

Joint
A connection between two or more machine parts. Precisely: a region where load passes from one member to another through a fastener or a weld.
Preload
The tension you put into a bolt when you tighten it. Precisely: the initial clamping force, written Fi, that squeezes the joined parts together before any external load acts.
External load
A load applied to the joint from outside, such as the pull on a bracket. Written P.
Proof strength (Sp)
The stress a bolt can take without permanent stretch. Precisely: a standard bolt property, in MPa, used with the tensile stress area to find the proof load.
Tensile stress area (At)
The effective area of a threaded bolt in tension. Precisely: a standard value in mm2 for each thread size, somewhat smaller than the bolt's plain cross section.
Stiffness constant (C)
The fraction of an external load that the bolt itself feels. Precisely: C = kb/(kb + km), where kb is bolt stiffness and km is member stiffness.
Rivet
A short metal pin with a head, closed at the other end to make a permanent joint. Precisely: a fastener that carries load mainly by shear across its diameter.
Pitch (p)
The distance from the centre of one rivet to the centre of the next. Precisely: the spacing of the rivets in a row, in mm.
Joint efficiency (eta)
How strong a riveted joint is compared with a solid plate of the same length. Precisely: joint strength per pitch divided by the strength of solid plate per pitch.
Shear
A force that slides one part across another. Precisely: force parallel to a cross section, divided by that area, written tau.
Crushing (bearing)
Local squashing of the rivet against the plate hole. Precisely: compressive stress on the projected area of the rivet, equal to diameter times plate thickness.
Fillet weld
A roughly triangular weld bead placed in the corner where two plates meet. Precisely: a weld with two equal legs, of length h, that joins two surfaces at an angle, usually 90 degrees.
Throat
The shortest distance across a fillet weld, from the corner to the weld face. Precisely: for an equal-leg fillet, throat = 0.707 times leg size h.
Butt weld
A weld that joins two plates end to end, in the same plane. Precisely: a weld whose throat equals the plate thickness.
Permissible stress
The stress a designer allows in a part, usually strength divided by a safety factor. Precisely: the design limit for the chosen material and load.

Bolted joints: preload, stress and load sharing

Think of tightening a bolt as stretching a rubber band and then pressing the two plates together with the stretched band. When you turn the nut, the bolt stretches a little, like a spring, and it pulls the plates tight. This pulling force is the preload, Fi. The preload is useful because it holds the plates together even before any external load arrives. The plates stay closed, and the bolt does not have to carry much extra load.

The stress in the bolt from preload alone is the preload divided by the tensile stress area.

sigmab = Fi / At

Here sigmab is the bolt stress in MPa (N/mm2), Fi is the preload in newtons, and At is the tensile stress area in mm2. For an M12 coarse thread bolt, a common textbook value is At = 84.3 mm2.

Worked example. A bolt is tightened to a preload of Fi = 25 kN = 25000 N. Its tensile stress area is 84.3 mm2.

  1. Write the formula sigmab = Fi/At.
  2. Substitute: sigmab = 25000/84.3.
  3. Divide: 25000/84.3 = 296.6 MPa.

How much preload should you use? A common textbook rule is based on the proof load, Fp = Sp times At. For a joint that will be taken apart and reused, use about 75 percent of the proof load. For a permanent joint, use about 90 percent. The reason is simple: too little preload lets the joint open, and too much can break the bolt when it is tightened.

TipAlways convert kN to N before dividing by mm2, so that the answer comes out in MPa. One kN is 1000 N.

TrapUse the tensile stress area At, not the plain bolt area. The thread cuts the bolt down, and At is the effective area that carries the tension.

A bolt has a proof strength of 600 MPa and At = 84.3 mm2. What is its proof load?

Fp = Sp times At = 600 times 84.3 = 50580 N, which is 50.58 kN.

Why do engineers prefer a high preload?

It keeps the joint clamped, so the external load mainly changes the bolt only slightly. A closed joint is also more resistant to fatigue. The limit is that the bolt must not yield during tightening.

Joint stiffness and how the external load is shared

Now add an external load P that tries to pull the joint apart. Here is the key idea. The bolt and the clamped plates are both springs, and they are squeezed by the same joint. When the external load arrives, it is shared between the bolt and the plates. The share depends on their stiffness. A stiff bolt takes more of the load, a soft bolt takes less.

Stiffness (k) is how much force a spring needs for one unit of stretch. A stiff spring is hard to stretch. For a simple bar of length L and cross-sectional area A, made from material with Young's modulus E, the stiffness is k = AE/L. The stiffness constant C says what fraction of the external load goes into the bolt.

C = kb / (kb + km)
Bolt load: Fb = Fi + C P
Member load: Fm = Fi − (1 − C) P

In these formulas kb is the bolt stiffness, km is the stiffness of the clamped members, Fb is the total load in the bolt, and Fm is the compressive load left in the plates. The member load falls as P rises, because the plates are being pulled apart a little. As long as Fm stays positive, the joint stays closed.

Worked example. A joint has preload Fi = 10 kN, external load P = 5 kN and stiffness constant C = 0.2.

  1. Bolt load: Fb = 10 + 0.2 times 5 = 10 + 1 = 11 kN.
  2. Member load: Fm = 10 − (1 − 0.2) times 5 = 10 − 0.8 times 5 = 10 − 4 = 6 kN.
  3. Check: the member load is still positive (6 kN), so the joint stays closed.

If the bolt is much stiffer than the members, is C large or small?

C is large, close to 1. A stiff bolt takes most of the external load. This is usually undesirable for fatigue, so designers often make the bolt more flexible, for example with a long, thin shank.

What happens to the member load when P grows and Fm reaches zero?

The plates are no longer squeezed, and the joint opens. The bolt then carries the whole external load, which is dangerous. Designers keep Fm positive.

Riveted joints: shear, tearing and crushing

Riveted joints are old, reliable and easy to understand once you see the picture. Two plates overlap, and a row of rivets passes through both. Imagine a row of paper clips pushed through two sheets. Load tries to pull the plates apart. Each rivet is pushed sideways across its own diameter, the plate is torn between the holes, and the rivet pushes into the wall of its hole. These are three different ways to fail.

  plate 1   ------[ o ]-----[ o ]-----[ o ]------ ... pulled right
                    rivet holes in a row, pitch p
  plate 2   ------[ o ]-----[ o ]-----[ o ]------ ... pulled left

The three failure modes are as follows.

  • Shearing of the rivets. The rivet is cut across its diameter. For one rivet in single shear, the strength is (pi/4) d2 times tau, where d is the rivet diameter and tau is the allowable shear stress of the rivet.
  • Tearing of the plate. The plate is pulled apart along the line of holes. In one pitch length p, the net width is (p − d), so the strength is (p − d) times t times sigmat, where t is the plate thickness and sigmat the allowable tensile stress.
  • Crushing of the rivet. The rivet presses into the hole wall. The projected bearing area is d times t, so the strength is d times t times sigmac, where sigmac is the allowable crushing stress.

A riveted joint can fail in any of the three ways. The joint strength is the smallest of the three values, and this smallest mode is the one that governs.

Worked example. A single-row lap joint has rivet diameter d = 20 mm, pitch p = 60 mm, plate thickness t = 10 mm, sigmat = 100 MPa, tau = 80 MPa and sigmac = 160 MPa, with one rivet per pitch.

  1. Tearing: (60 − 20) times 10 times 100 = 40000 N = 40 kN.
  2. Shearing of one rivet in single shear: (pi/4) times 202 times 80 = 25133 N = 25.13 kN.
  3. Crushing: 20 times 10 times 160 = 32000 N = 32 kN.
  4. The smallest value is 25.13 kN, from shearing. So shearing governs.

TrapDo not add the three values. The joint fails at the weakest mode, so you take the minimum. Adding them would overstate the joint's strength.

In a double strap butt joint, what changes for the rivets?

Each rivet has two shear planes, one on each side of the butt. So the shear strength per rivet is doubled: 2 times (pi/4) d2 times tau.

Which failure is caused by the plate being pulled across the holes?

Tearing of the plate. The net width of the plate between the holes is smaller than the full pitch, so the plate is weakest along the line of holes.

Joint efficiency of a riveted joint

Efficiency is a simple idea. Suppose you cut a plate of length p and thickness t, with no holes at all. It can carry a load of p times t times sigmat. Now punch holes in it to make a riveted joint. The joint is weaker than the solid plate, because the holes remove material and the rivets have their own limits. Efficiency tells you what fraction of the solid strength the joint keeps.

eta = (strength of the riveted joint per pitch) / (strength of the solid plate per pitch) = Pjoint / (p t sigmat)

Efficiency is written as a fraction or as a percentage. A joint with 100 percent efficiency is as strong as solid plate. Real riveted joints are usually between 60 and 85 percent efficient, and the single-row lap joint is often much lower.

The efficiency of the plate alone, when only tearing is considered, is etat = (p − d)/p. This is a useful quick check, but it is not the final answer, because shearing of rivets may govern.

Worked example. Use the same joint as before: Pjoint = 25.13 kN, solid plate per pitch = 60 times 10 times 100 = 60000 N = 60 kN.

  1. Tearing efficiency: (60 − 20)/60 = 40/60 = 0.667, which is 66.7 percent.
  2. Actual joint efficiency: 25.13/60 = 0.419, which is 41.9 percent.
  3. Compare: the actual efficiency is lower than the tearing efficiency, because shearing of the rivets is the weakest mode.

GATE loves thisWhen a question asks for the efficiency of a riveted joint, compute the strength by all three modes, take the smallest, and then divide by the solid plate strength. Many wrong answers come from using the tearing efficiency alone.

Can a riveted joint have efficiency above 100 percent?

No. The joint has holes and rivets, so it cannot be stronger than the solid plate it came from. A value above 100 percent means a mistake in the working.

Fillet welds and butt welds

A weld fuses the parts together. In a fillet weld, a triangle of molten metal sits in the corner between two plates. In a butt weld, the two plate edges are melted together end to end. The key question for a weld is the throat: the thinnest cross section through which the load must pass. We then find the shear or normal stress on this throat area.

For an equal-leg fillet weld of leg size h, the throat is the short side of the triangle that faces the load. A right isosceles triangle with legs h has its altitude equal to h divided by the square root of 2, which is 0.707 times h.

Throat tw = 0.707 h, weld area Aw = tw times l, and tau = F / (0.707 h l)

Here h is the leg size in mm, l is the weld length in mm, F is the load in N, and tau is the stress in MPa. Two welds on the two sides of a plate share the load, so the total length is doubled.

Worked example, fillet. A fillet weld has leg h = 8 mm and length l = 100 mm, and the load is F = 50 kN = 50000 N.

  1. Throat: tw = 0.707 times 8 = 5.656 mm.
  2. Area: Aw = 5.656 times 100 = 565.6 mm2.
  3. Stress: tau = 50000/565.6 = 88.4 MPa.
  4. Two welds, each 100 mm long, share the load equally: the total length is 200 mm, so the stress is 50000/(2 times 565.6) = 44.2 MPa.

Worked example, butt. A butt weld joins two plates of thickness t = 12 mm and width 150 mm. The load is 360 kN = 360000 N. The throat of a full penetration butt weld equals the plate thickness.

  1. Area: 12 times 150 = 1800 mm2.
  2. Stress: 360000/1800 = 200 MPa.

TrapDo not use the leg size h as the throat. The throat of a fillet weld is 0.707 times h. Using h directly makes the weld look about 40 percent stronger than it is.

Key ideaFor welds, first find the throat. Then the rest is a stress calculation: force divided by the throat area, compared with the permissible stress for the weld.

What is the throat of a fillet weld with leg 10 mm?

0.707 times 10 = 7.07 mm.

Why is a butt weld with the same throat stronger per millimetre than a fillet?

It is not always stronger. The comparison depends on the throat size. A butt weld's throat is the full plate thickness, which is often larger than 0.707 times the fillet leg, so it often carries more per mm of length.

Sizing a joint step by step

Most GATE questions on joints are sizing problems: you know the load and you must find the size. The method is always the same, and it is worth writing it down each time.

  1. List the failure modes. Bolts: tension and preload. Rivets: shear, tearing and crushing. Welds: throat stress.
  2. Find the load in each element. For a bolt, use Fb = Fi + C P. For a rivet row, use the load per rivet.
  3. Compute the strength of each mode. Divide the allowable stress into the load to find the required area or size, or multiply the stress by the area to find the strength.
  4. Take the governing mode. For a riveted joint, the smallest strength governs. For a weld, the largest stress governs.
  5. Check the result. Compare with the given allowable value, and confirm the units.

Here is a quick example of sizing. A fillet weld must carry F = 60 kN, with allowable shear stress 80 MPa, and two welds of total length 240 mm are available. The required throat is F/(tau times L) = 60000/(80 times 240) = 3.125 mm. Then the leg size is h = 3.125/0.707 = 4.42 mm.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original, written in the GATE style, and they are not copied from any real exam paper. Try the "How to think about it" steps yourself before reading the answer.

Bolt stress from a preload

An M12 bolt with tensile stress area 84.3 mm2 is tightened to a preload of 25 kN. What is the bolt stress, in MPa, to one decimal place? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The stress in the bolt caused by the preload alone.
  2. Which idea applies. sigmab = Fi/At, with F in newtons and A in mm2.
  3. Work it out. Convert 25 kN to 25000 N. Then 25000/84.3 = 296.56, which is 296.6 MPa to one decimal.
  4. Check. A bolt stress of about 300 MPa is a sensible size for a steel bolt, so the answer is reasonable.

Answer: 296.6 MPa.

Tearing efficiency of a row of rivets

A single-row riveted lap joint has pitch p = 50 mm and rivet diameter d = 20 mm. Considering only the tearing of the plate, what is the efficiency, in percent? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The tearing efficiency, not the full joint efficiency.
  2. Which idea applies. The tearing efficiency is (p − d)/p.
  3. Work it out. (50 − 20)/50 = 30/50 = 0.6, which is 60 percent.
  4. Check. The efficiency is below 100 percent, as it must be for a joint with holes.

Answer: 60 percent.

Bolt and member loads with a larger stiffness constant

A bolted joint has preload Fi = 8 kN, an external load P = 4 kN, and stiffness constant C = 0.25. What is the total load in the bolt, in kN? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The bolt load Fb, which includes the preload.
  2. Which idea applies. Fb = Fi + C P.
  3. Work it out. C P = 0.25 times 4 = 1 kN. Then Fb = 8 + 1 = 9 kN. For the members, Fm = 8 − 0.75 times 4 = 5 kN, which is still positive, so the joint stays closed.
  4. Check. The bolt load should be a little more than the preload, and it is.

Answer: 9 kN.

Stress in a fillet weld

A fillet weld with equal legs of 6 mm and length 80 mm carries a load of 30 kN. What is the shear stress on the weld throat, in MPa, to one decimal place? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The shear stress on the throat of one fillet weld.
  2. Which idea applies. Throat = 0.707 h, area = throat times l, stress = F/area.
  3. Work it out. Throat = 0.707 times 6 = 4.242 mm. Area = 4.242 times 80 = 339.4 mm2. Stress = 30000/339.4 = 88.4 MPa.
  4. Check. The stress is close to the earlier 88.4 MPa result for a different weld. The numbers are in a sensible range.

Answer: 88.4 MPa.

Statements about riveted joints

Which of the following statements about riveted joints are correct? Select all that apply. (MSQ)

  • (A) Tearing of the plate happens along the line of rivet holes.
  • (B) The efficiency of a riveted joint can be more than 100 percent.
  • (C) In a double strap butt joint, each rivet has two shear planes.
  • (D) Crushing is a bending failure of the rivet shank.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true. More than one may be correct.
  2. Which idea applies. The three failure modes, efficiency, and the number of shear planes in a butt joint.
  3. Work it out. (A) True: tearing runs across the holes. (B) False: holes weaken the plate, so efficiency is below 100 percent. (C) True: a double strap has the rivet passing through two interfaces. (D) False: crushing is local squashing against the hole wall, not bending.
  4. Check. Each true statement describes a real feature of the joint, and each false one contradicts the picture of the joint.

Answer: (A) and (C) are correct.

Bolt load with a proof-based preload

A reused bolted joint uses a bolt with proof strength Sp = 600 MPa and tensile stress area At = 84.3 mm2. The preload is set to 75 percent of the proof load. The joint then carries an external load P = 10 kN with stiffness constant C = 0.2. Find the bolt load and the member load, in kN, to two decimal places. (NAT: enter the bolt load.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The total bolt load Fb, after the external load is applied. The member load is also needed as a check.
  2. Which idea applies. Proof load Fp = Sp At. Preload Fi = 0.75 Fp. Then Fb = Fi + C P and Fm = Fi − (1 − C) P.
  3. Work it out. Fp = 600 times 84.3 = 50580 N = 50.58 kN. Then Fi = 0.75 times 50.58 = 37.935 kN. Bolt load: Fb = 37.935 + 0.2 times 10 = 37.935 + 2 = 39.94 kN. Member load: Fm = 37.935 − 0.8 times 10 = 37.935 − 8 = 29.94 kN.
  4. Check. The bolt load is the preload plus a fifth of the external load. The member load is still positive, so the joint stays closed.

Answer: about 39.94 kN for the bolt, and about 29.94 kN for the members.

Choosing a fillet leg size

A bracket carries a load of 60 kN through two equal fillet welds, each 120 mm long. The allowable shear stress on the weld throat is 80 MPa. What is the smallest standard leg size h that gives a stress at or below 80 MPa? Give the minimum leg size in mm, to two decimal places. (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The leg size h that makes the stress exactly at the allowable value, which is the minimum size.
  2. Which idea applies. The required throat area is F divided by the allowable stress. Then throat = 0.707 h.
  3. Work it out. Total weld length L = 2 times 120 = 240 mm. Required area = 60000/80 = 750 mm2. Required throat = 750/240 = 3.125 mm. Required leg = 3.125/0.707 = 4.42 mm.
  4. Check. A leg of 4.42 mm gives a throat of 0.707 times 4.42 = 3.125 mm, and 60000/(3.125 times 240) = 80 MPa. The check works.

Answer: 4.42 mm.

Which mode governs a riveted joint?

A single-row lap joint has rivet diameter d = 25 mm, pitch p = 70 mm, plate thickness t = 8 mm, sigmat = 120 MPa, tau = 60 MPa and sigmac = 150 MPa, with one rivet per pitch in single shear. Find the joint efficiency, in percent, to one decimal place, after taking the governing mode. (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The efficiency, which needs the governing mode first.
  2. Which idea applies. Compute tearing, shearing and crushing. The smallest is the joint strength. Then divide by the solid plate strength per pitch, p t sigmat.
  3. Work it out. Tearing: (70 − 25) times 8 times 120 = 43200 N = 43.2 kN. Shearing: (pi/4) times 252 times 60 = 29452 N = 29.45 kN. Crushing: 25 times 8 times 150 = 30000 N = 30 kN. The smallest is 29.45 kN (shearing). Solid plate: 70 times 8 times 120 = 67200 N = 67.2 kN. Efficiency = 29.45/67.2 = 0.438, which is 43.8 percent.
  4. Check. The efficiency is between 0 and 100 percent, as it should be, and it is below the tearing efficiency (45/70 = 64.3 percent), because shearing governs.

Answer: 43.8 percent (shearing governs).

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Bolt in tension (NAT, 2 marks)

A bolt must carry 30 kN. The allowable tensile stress is 100 MPa. Find the required core area in mm², and the core diameter in mm, correct to 2 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A = P/σ, d = √(4A/π).
  2. Which idea applies. Design on the root area.
  3. Work it out. A = 300 mm². d = 19.54 mm.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
import math
A = 30e3 / 100
print(A, "%.2f mm" % math.sqrt(4 * A / math.pi))

300.0 19.54 mm

Answer: 300 mm² and about 19.54 mm.

Exam-level 2: Fillet weld (NAT, 2 marks)

A parallel fillet weld of leg 8 mm and total length 150 mm carries 60 kN. Find the shear stress in the throat in MPa.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. τ = P/(0.707 s L).
  2. Which idea applies. The throat is 0.707 times the leg.
  3. Work it out. 0.707 · 8 · 150 = 848.4 mm². τ = 70.72 MPa.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
print("%.2f MPa" % (60e3 / (0.707 * 8 * 150)))

70.72 MPa

Answer: about 70.72 MPa.

Exam-level 3: Riveted joint efficiency (NAT, 2 marks)

A single riveted lap joint uses plates 10 mm thick, rivets 20 mm diameter at pitch 60 mm. Allowable: tension 120 MPa, shear 90 MPa, crushing 180 MPa. Find the joint efficiency.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Smallest strength over solid plate strength.
  2. Which idea applies. Tearing (p − d)tσt, shearing (π/4)d²τ, crushing dtσc.
  3. Work it out. Tearing 48 kN, shearing 28.27 kN, crushing 36 kN. Solid plate 72 kN. Efficiency 28.27/72 = 39.3%.
  4. Check. The code computes them.
import math
p, d, t = 60, 20, 10
vals = {"tear": (p - d) * t * 120, "shear": math.pi / 4 * d * d * 90, "crush": d * t * 180}
print({k: round(v / 1000, 2) for k, v in vals.items()}, "eff = %.3f" % (min(vals.values()) / (p * t * 120)))

{'tear': 48.0, 'shear': 28.27, 'crush': 36.0} eff = 0.393

Answer: about 39.3%.

Summary

Here are the one-line meanings of the main ideas on this page.

Joint
A connection that passes load from one part to another.
Preload Fi
The tension put into a bolt when it is tightened.
Tensile stress area At
The effective area of a threaded bolt in tension, in mm2.
Proof load
Proof strength times tensile stress area, the load a bolt takes without permanent stretch.
Stiffness constant C
The fraction of the external load that goes into the bolt.
Bolt load Fb
Preload plus C times the external load.
Member load Fm
Preload minus (1 minus C) times the external load, which must stay positive.
Rivet
A metal pin with a head, closed at the far end to make a permanent joint.
Pitch p
Centre-to-centre distance between neighbouring rivets in a row.
Shearing
The rivet is cut across its diameter, with strength (pi/4) d squared tau per rivet.
Tearing
The plate is pulled apart along the holes, with strength (p minus d) t sigmat.
Crushing
The rivet presses into the hole wall, with strength d t sigmac.
Joint efficiency
Joint strength per pitch divided by solid plate strength per pitch.
Fillet weld
A triangular weld bead in the corner between two plates.
Throat
The shortest distance across a fillet weld, equal to 0.707 times the leg size.
Butt weld
A weld joining two plate edges end to end, with throat equal to the plate thickness.
Permissible stress
The design limit stress for a part, from strength and a safety factor.

Remember these

  1. Preload stress is Fi divided by At, with the units converted to N and mm2.
  2. A reused bolt is usually preloaded to about 75 percent of its proof load.
  3. Only the fraction C of the external load goes into the bolt.
  4. Check all three riveted failure modes and take the smallest.
  5. Joint efficiency is always below 100 percent for a joint with holes.
  6. For a fillet weld, use the throat 0.707 h, not the leg size h.

Complex power & power factor

In an AC circuit, power is not simply voltage times current. Part of the power does useful work, and part just moves back and forth between the source and the load. This topic shows how the three kinds of power fit together in one triangle, and what the power factor tells you about how well a load uses the current it draws.

The idea in plain words

Imagine a playground swing. When you push it, you do useful work. The swing then pushes back as it returns. Over one full swing, the energy goes out and comes back, so the net useful work is small, yet the swing still needs the push. Circuits with coils and capacitors behave in a similar way. Some power turns into real work, such as turning a motor or heating a kettle. Some power just moves back and forth between the source and the storage parts of the load. We call that second part reactive power.

Why does this matter? The wires must carry the total current, including the current that only supports the back and forth movement. More current means thicker wires, more heating and a bigger generator. That is why many electricity tariffs charge extra when the power factor is low. The power factor measures how much of the current does useful work.

In GATE Robotics and Automation (RA) and Data Science and AI (DA), this topic appears as numerical questions. You are given a voltage, a current and an angle, or a load with a stated power factor, and you must find P, Q, S or the power factor. Sometimes you must find the capacitor that corrects the power factor. Each question becomes easy once you follow the steps.

Key terms

Read this list once now, and come back to it whenever a word feels unfamiliar. Each term has a plain meaning first, then the precise definition.

AC (alternating current)
Current that changes direction in a repeating pattern, like the wall socket. Precise: a sinusoidal voltage or current, usually at 50 Hz or 60 Hz.
RMS value
Root mean square value, the "equivalent DC" size of an AC quantity. Precise: for a sine wave, Vrms = Vm/√2, where Vm is the peak value.
Phase angle (φ)
How far the current is shifted in time compared with the voltage, as an angle.
Phasor
A fixed arrow that stands for a sine wave. Its length is the RMS value and its direction is the phase angle.
Active (real) power, P
Power that does useful work, such as heat, light or motion. Precise: P = VI cos φ, in watts (W).
Reactive power, Q
Power that moves back and forth and does no average work. Precise: Q = VI sin φ, in volt-amperes reactive (VAR).
Apparent power, |S|
Total power the wires must carry. Precise: |S| = VI, in volt-amperes (VA).
Complex power, S
One number that holds P and Q together, written S = P + jQ. Precise: S = V I*, where I* is the complex conjugate of the current phasor.
j
The engineering symbol for the square root of minus one. Precise: j² = -1. Engineers use j because i already stands for current.
Power triangle
A right-angled triangle with P, Q and |S|. Precise: |S|² = P² + Q².
Power factor (pf)
The fraction of apparent power that does useful work. Precise: pf = P/|S| = cos φ, a number from 0 to 1.
Lagging power factor
The current is behind the voltage. Typical of inductive loads such as motors. Precise: φ is positive and Q is positive.
Leading power factor
The current is ahead of the voltage. Typical of capacitive loads. Precise: φ is negative and Q is negative.
Inductive load
A load with coils, such as a motor or a transformer.
Capacitive load
A load with capacitors.
Power factor correction
Adding a capacitor, usually in parallel with the load, so the source supplies less reactive power.

Step 1: Voltage, current and the angle between them

In a single-phase AC supply, the voltage and the current both rise and fall like sine waves. If the current peaks at the same moment as the voltage, the two are in step and the phase angle is zero. If the current peaks a little later, it lags. If it peaks a little earlier, it leads. The angle between the two waves is the phase angle, written φ (the Greek letter phi).

Engineers rarely use peak values in power work. They use the RMS value. For a sine wave with peak value Vm:

Vrms = Vm / √2

Symbols: Vm is the peak voltage, Vrms is the RMS voltage, and √2 is about 1.414. Example: a peak of 325 V gives 325 / 1.414 = 229.8 V, which we call 230 V. This is why a household supply is quoted as 230 V even though its peak is higher.

We write voltage and current as phasors, with the voltage on the reference line at 0 degrees:

V = 230 ∠ 0° V, and I = 10 ∠ -30° A

Read this as: the voltage has RMS value 230 V and angle 0. The current has RMS value 10 A and angle minus 30 degrees, so it lags the voltage by 30 degrees. The phase angle is φ = 30°.

TipDecide first whether the current is ahead of or behind the voltage. Everything else follows from that decision.

Worked example: reading a phasor

Suppose the current is 4 ∠ 20° A. Step by step:

  1. The length is 4, so the RMS current is 4 A.
  2. The angle is +20°, which is positive, so the current leads the voltage by 20 degrees.
  3. A leading current means a capacitive load, so the power factor is leading.
If the current is 4 ∠ -20° A, is the load capacitive or inductive?

The angle is negative, so the current lags. A lagging current means an inductive load, such as a motor.

Why do we use 230 V RMS and not the peak value?

RMS is the DC value that gives the same heating effect. Heating is what we care about for real work, so RMS is the useful number.

Step 2: Real, reactive and apparent power

Each kind of power answers a different question:

  • Apparent power answers: how much power must the wires carry? It is the RMS voltage times the RMS current.
  • Real power answers: how much power does useful work? It is the part of the apparent power that is in step with the voltage.
  • Reactive power answers: how much power is just moving back and forth? It is the part a quarter cycle out of step.
|S| = V I
P = V I cos φ
Q = V I sin φ

Symbols: V is the RMS voltage in volts, I is the RMS current in amperes, and φ is the phase angle. The units are VA for |S|, W for P and VAR for Q. Note that VA and VAR are not the same as W, even though all three look like "volts times amps". This is a common source of confusion.

Worked example with numbers

Take V = 230 V, I = 10 A and φ = 30° lagging.

  1. Apparent power. |S| = 230 × 10 = 2300 VA.
  2. Cosine. cos 30° = 0.866025. Use degree mode on your calculator.
  3. Real power. P = 2300 × 0.866025 = 1991.9 W.
  4. Sine. sin 30° = 0.5 exactly.
  5. Reactive power. Q = 2300 × 0.5 = 1150 VAR.
  6. Check. Square and add: 1991.9² is about 3,967,700 and 1150² is 1,322,500. The sum is about 5,290,200, which agrees with 2300² = 5,290,000 to within rounding.

The check in step 6 is the habit to build. Whenever you find P and Q, confirm that P² + Q² equals |S|².

TrapDo not mix up W, VA and VAR. A question about power used usually wants watts. A question about a kVA rating wants apparent power.

A load has V = 200 V, I = 5 A and pf = 0.8 lagging. What is the real power?

P = V I cos φ = 200 × 5 × 0.8 = 800 W. The apparent power is 1000 VA and the reactive power is 600 VAR.

Why is Q zero when the power factor is 1?

A power factor of 1 means φ = 0, so sin φ = 0 and Q = VI × 0 = 0. Voltage and current are in step, so nothing moves back and forth.

Step 3: The power triangle and complex power

The three kinds of power form a right-angled triangle, called the power triangle. Real power P is the horizontal side, reactive power Q is the vertical side, and apparent power |S| is the slanted side.

Q (VAR) ^ | / | / | / |S| (VA) | / | / φ +------------> P (W)

Pythagoras gives the rule, and the triangle gives the trigonometry: cos φ = P/|S| and sin φ = Q/|S|.

|S|² = P² + Q²

Engineers combine P and Q into one complex number, the complex power:

S = P + jQ

Symbols: P is the real part, Q is the imaginary part, and j is the square root of minus one. The length of S is the slanted side of the triangle, and its angle is φ. The complex power can also be found directly from phasors:

S = V I*

Symbols: V is the voltage phasor, I* is the complex conjugate of the current phasor (same length, opposite angle), and S is the complex power. In Python, the conjugate is I.conjugate().

import cmath
import math

V = complex(230, 0)                    # voltage phasor, reference angle 0 degrees
I = cmath.rect(10, -math.radians(30))  # current of 10 A lagging the voltage by 30 degrees
S = V * I.conjugate()                  # complex power S = V I*

print("S =", round(S.real, 1), "+", round(S.imag, 1), "j")
print("|S| =", round(abs(S), 1), "VA")
print("pf =", round(math.cos(cmath.phase(S)), 3))
S = 1991.9 + 1150.0 j
|S| = 2300.0 VA
pf = 0.866

The complex power is 1991.9 + 1150.0 j, so the real power is 1991.9 W and the reactive power is 1150 VAR. The length is 2300 VA, and the power factor is 0.866, which is cos 30°. Everything agrees with Step 2.

Why the conjugate? Without it, a lagging load would appear to have negative reactive power. That is the most common sign mistake in this topic, so remember: use the conjugate of the current.

What is the complex conjugate of 4 ∠ 20°?

It is 4 ∠ -20°. The length stays the same and the angle changes sign.

If S = 375.9 + j136.8, is the reactive power positive or negative?

Positive, Q = 136.8 VAR. The load is inductive and the power factor is lagging.

Step 4: Leading and lagging power factor

The power factor is a number, but it also carries a word: lagging or leading. The word tells you which way the current is shifted, and so what kind of load you have.

pf = P / |S| = cos φ

Symbols: P is real power, |S| is apparent power, and φ is the phase angle. Because P is never bigger than |S|, the power factor is always between 0 and 1.

A memory aid helps: "ELI the ICE man". In an inductor (L), voltage (E) leads current (I). In a capacitor (C), current (I) leads voltage (E). So:

  • Inductive load (coils, motors): current lags. Power factor lagging. Q positive.
  • Capacitive load (capacitors): current leads. Power factor leading. Q negative.
  • Pure resistor: in step. Power factor 1. Q zero.

Key ideaThe sign of Q tells you the type of load. Positive Q means lagging and inductive. Negative Q means leading and capacitive.

Worked example: two loads in parallel

Two loads share a 230 V supply. Load 1 is 10 kVA at 0.8 lagging. Load 2 is 5 kW at 0.5 leading. Find the total power factor. Add the real powers, add the reactive powers, then find the new apparent power.

  1. Load 1. P1 = 10 000 × 0.8 = 8000 W. Q1 = 10 000 × 0.6 = 6000 VAR, positive because it lags.
  2. Load 2. P2 = 5000 W. Since cos φ2 = 0.5, φ2 = 60°. Leading, so Q2 = -5000 × tan 60° = -5000 × 1.732 = -8660 VAR.
  3. Total real power. P = 8000 + 5000 = 13 000 W.
  4. Total reactive power. Q = 6000 + (-8660) = -2660 VAR. Negative, so the total is leading.
  5. Total apparent power. |S| = √(13 000² + 2660²) = √176 075 600 = 13 269 VA, to the nearest VA.
  6. Total power factor. pf = 13 000 / 13 269 = 0.9797, leading.
import math
P1, Q1 = 8000.0, 6000.0    # load 1: S1 = 10 kVA at pf 0.8 lagging
P2 = 5000.0
phi2 = math.acos(0.5)      # 60 degrees
Q2 = -P2 * math.tan(phi2)  # leading, so Q2 is negative
P = P1 + P2
Q = Q1 + Q2
S = math.hypot(P, Q)
print("P =", P, "W")
print("Q =", round(Q, 1), "VAR")
print("|S| =", round(S, 1), "VA")
print("pf =", round(P / S, 4), "leading" if Q < 0 else "lagging")
P = 13000.0 W
Q = -2660.3 VAR
|S| = 13269.4 VA
pf = 0.9797 leading

TrapNever add apparent powers directly. 10 kVA + 5 kW does not give 15 kVA. Add P and Q separately, then use Pythagoras.

GATE loves thisQuestions often describe loads in words, such as "a capacitor bank" or "an induction motor". Translate the words into the sign of Q before any arithmetic.

A motor has a lagging power factor of 0.7. Is its reactive power positive or negative?

Positive. Lagging means inductive, and inductive loads have positive Q in the S = P + jQ convention.

Can the power factor be 1.2?

No. P can never be bigger than |S|, so the power factor is always between 0 and 1.

Step 5: Power factor correction

Suppose a factory has motors with a lagging power factor. The real power P is fixed by the work the motors do, but the reactive power is large, so the current is large and the wires and transformers must be oversized. A common fix is a bank of capacitors connected in parallel. A capacitor is a leading load with negative Q, so it cancels part of the motors' positive Q. The real power does not change, because the capacitor does no useful work on average. The supply now sees less reactive power, so the current falls.

Let the old angle be φ1 and the new angle be φ2. The reactive power the capacitor must supply is:

Qc = P (tan φ1 − tan φ2)

Symbols: P is the fixed real power, and tan φ is sine divided by cosine. The product P tan φ is simply the reactive power at that angle.

The capacitance follows from the reactive power of a capacitor at supply frequency f:

Qc = ω C V² with ω = 2π f

Symbols: ω is the angular frequency in radians per second, C is capacitance in farads, V is the RMS voltage across the capacitor. Solve with C = Qc / (ω V²).

Worked example: correcting a 40 kW load

A 40 kW load has a power factor of 0.6 lagging. We want 0.95 lagging.

import math
P = 40000
Q1 = P * math.sqrt(1 - 0.6**2) / 0.6     # tan of the old angle
cos2 = 0.95
Q2 = P * math.sqrt(1 - cos2**2) / cos2   # tan of the new angle
Qc = Q1 - Q2
print("Q1 =", round(Q1, 1), "VAR")
print("Q2 =", round(Q2, 1), "VAR")
print("Qc =", round(Qc, 1), "VAR")
Q1 = 53333.3 VAR
Q2 = 13147.4 VAR
Qc = 40186.0 VAR

The capacitor bank must supply about 40.19 kVAR. P stays at 40 kW, while the apparent power falls from about 66.7 kVA to about 42.1 kVA.

TipFrom a power factor, get tan φ with sin φ = √(1 − pf²) and tan φ = sin φ / cos φ. For pf = 0.6, sin φ = 0.8 and tan φ = 4/3.

TrapCorrection does not change the real power. If a question says P changes, check whether the load itself changed.

Why does correcting the power factor reduce the supply current?

Current is I = |S| / V. Correction lowers Q, which lowers |S| because P is fixed. A smaller |S| at the same voltage means a smaller current.

What must a capacitor supply to correct a load to unity power factor?

Exactly Qc = P tan φ1, the whole reactive power of the load, because tan φ2 = 0 at unity power factor.

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original GATE-style questions written for practice. They are not real past paper questions. Try each one on paper before reading the answer.

Power from volts, amps and power factor

A single-phase load is connected to a 200 V RMS supply. It draws 5 A RMS at a power factor of 0.8 lagging. Find the apparent power, the real power and the reactive power.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. |S|, P and Q.
  2. Which idea applies. |S| = VI. Then P = |S| × pf. For Q use sin φ = √(1 − pf²), with the sign from "lagging".
  3. Work it out. |S| = 200 × 5 = 1000 VA. P = 1000 × 0.8 = 800 W. sin φ = √(1 − 0.64) = 0.6, so Q = 1000 × 0.6 = 600 VAR, positive.
  4. Check. 800² + 600² = 640 000 + 360 000 = 1 000 000 = 1000².
import math
V = 200.0
I = 5.0
pf = 0.8
S = V * I                     # apparent power in VA
P = S * pf                    # real power in W
Q = S * math.sqrt(1 - pf**2)  # reactive power in VAR (sin of the angle)
print("S =", round(S, 1), "VA")
print("P =", round(P, 1), "W")
print("Q =", round(Q, 1), "VAR")
S = 1000.0 VA
P = 800.0 W
Q = 600.0 VAR

Answer: |S| = 1000 VA, P = 800 W, Q = 600 VAR (positive, lagging).

Which statements describe a leading load?

A load has a leading power factor. Select all that apply.
(A) The current leads the voltage.
(B) The load is capacitive.
(C) Q is negative in the convention S = P + jQ.
(D) The load is inductive.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true for a leading load.
  2. Which idea applies. ELI the ICE man, and the sign rule for Q.
  3. Work it out. (A) is the definition of leading, so true. (B) follows from ICE, so true. (C) leading means φ is negative, so Q is negative, so true. (D) inductive loads lag, so false.
  4. Check. A capacitor is the classic leading load, with negative Q.

Answer: (A), (B) and (C). Option (D) is wrong.

Reactive power from real power and power factor

A 400 V RMS load absorbs 40 kW at a power factor of 0.6 lagging. Find the reactive power Q in kVAR, correct to two decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Q in kVAR.
  2. Which idea applies. Q = P tan φ, and tan φ = sin φ / cos φ.
  3. Work it out. cos φ = 0.6, so sin φ = 0.8 and tan φ = 0.8 / 0.6 = 4/3. Then Q = 40 000 × 4/3 = 53 333.33 VAR, which is 53.33 kVAR. The voltage is not needed in this form.
  4. Check. Here tan φ is above 1, so Q is bigger than P. 53.33 is bigger than 40, which is reasonable.
from fractions import Fraction
P = 40000
pf = Fraction(3, 5)           # cos(phi) = 0.6
sin_phi = Fraction(4, 5)      # sin(phi) = 0.8, since 0.6^2 + 0.8^2 = 1
Q = P * sin_phi / pf          # Q = P tan(phi)
print("Q =", round(float(Q), 2), "VAR")
Q = 53333.33 VAR

Answer: Q = 53.33 kVAR (lagging, so positive).

Capacitor bank for power factor correction

The 40 kW load from the previous problem is corrected to a power factor of 0.95 lagging by a capacitor bank in parallel. Find the reactive power the capacitor bank must supply, in kVAR, correct to two decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Qc.
  2. Which idea applies. Qc = P (tan φ1 − tan φ2), with P fixed.
  3. Work it out. The old Q is 53.33 kVAR. For pf 0.95, sin φ2 = √(1 − 0.9025) = 0.3122, so tan φ2 = 0.3122 / 0.95 = 0.3287 and Q2 = 40 000 × 0.3287 = 13.15 kVAR. Then Qc = 53.33 − 13.15 = 40.19 kVAR.
  4. Check. The capacitor must be smaller than the load's own Q, since the new Q is not zero. 40.19 is less than 53.33.
import math
P = 40000
Q1 = P * math.sqrt(1 - 0.6**2) / 0.6     # tan of the old angle
cos2 = 0.95
Q2 = P * math.sqrt(1 - cos2**2) / cos2   # tan of the new angle
Qc = Q1 - Q2
print("Q1 =", round(Q1, 1), "VAR")
print("Q2 =", round(Q2, 1), "VAR")
print("Qc =", round(Qc, 1), "VAR")
Q1 = 53333.3 VAR
Q2 = 13147.4 VAR
Qc = 40186.0 VAR

Answer: Qc = 40.19 kVAR.

Complex power from phasors

The voltage phasor is V = 100 ∠ 0° V (RMS) and the current phasor is I = 4 ∠ -20° A (RMS). Find S = V I*. State P and Q, and say whether the power factor is leading or lagging.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. S, then P and Q.
  2. Which idea applies. S = V I*. The conjugate of 4 ∠ -20° is 4 ∠ +20°.
  3. Work it out. S = 100 ∠ 0° × 4 ∠ 20° = 400 ∠ 20°. P = 400 cos 20° = 400 × 0.9397 = 375.9 W. Q = 400 sin 20° = 400 × 0.3420 = 136.8 VAR.
  4. Check. |S| = √(375.9² + 136.8²) is about √160 000 = 400, which matches the length 400 ∠ 20°.
import cmath, math
V = cmath.rect(100, 0)
I = cmath.rect(4, math.radians(-20))
S = V * I.conjugate()
print("S =", round(S.real, 1), round(S.imag, 1))
print("pf =", round(math.cos(cmath.phase(S)), 3), "lagging")
S = 375.9 136.8
pf = 0.94 lagging

Answer: S = 375.9 + j136.8 VA. P = 375.9 W, Q = 136.8 VAR (positive), power factor 0.94 lagging.

Total power factor of two parallel loads

Load 1 is rated 10 kVA at a power factor of 0.8 lagging. Load 2 absorbs 5 kW at a power factor of 0.5 leading. Both are in parallel across the same 230 V supply. Find the total power factor to four decimal places, and state whether it is leading or lagging.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The total power factor.
  2. Which idea applies. Add P and Q separately, then pf = P / |S|.
  3. Work it out. Load 1: P = 8000 W, Q = 6000 VAR. Load 2: P = 5000 W, Q = -5000 × tan 60° = -8660 VAR. Totals: P = 13 000 W, Q = -2660 VAR, |S| = 13 269 VA. pf = 13 000 / 13 269 = 0.9797, leading.
  4. Check. The combined power factor should be higher than the weaker load's 0.5, and it is. The sign is leading because the capacitive load won the reactive contest.
import math
P1, Q1 = 8000.0, 6000.0    # load 1: S1 = 10 kVA at pf 0.8 lagging
P2 = 5000.0
phi2 = math.acos(0.5)      # 60 degrees
Q2 = -P2 * math.tan(phi2)  # leading, so Q2 is negative
P = P1 + P2
Q = Q1 + Q2
S = math.hypot(P, Q)
print("P =", P, "W")
print("Q =", round(Q, 1), "VAR")
print("|S| =", round(S, 1), "VA")
print("pf =", round(P / S, 4), "leading" if Q < 0 else "lagging")
P = 13000.0 W
Q = -2660.3 VAR
|S| = 13269.4 VA
pf = 0.9797 leading

Answer: 0.9797 leading (about 0.98 leading).

Statements about the parallel loads

Using the two loads in the previous problem, select all that apply.
(A) The total reactive power is negative.
(B) The total power factor is leading.
(C) The total apparent power is less than the sum of the two separate apparent powers.
(D) The total real power is 15 kW.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true for the combined load.
  2. Which idea applies. The totals from the previous problem: P = 13 kW, Q = -2.66 kVAR, |S| = 13.27 kVA, pf 0.98 leading.
  3. Work it out. (A) Q = -2660 VAR, so true. (B) Negative Q means leading, so true. (C) The separate apparent powers are 10 kVA and 10 kVA (5 kW divided by 0.5). Their sum is 20 kVA, and the total is 13.27 kVA, so true. (D) 8 kW + 5 kW = 13 kW, not 15 kW, so false.

Capacitor to correct a load to unity power factor

A 230 V, 50 Hz load absorbs 1.5 kW at a power factor of 0.6 lagging. A capacitor in parallel brings the power factor to 1.0. Find the capacitance in microfarads, correct to one decimal place.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. C in microfarads.
  2. Which idea applies. At unity power factor the capacitor supplies the whole reactive power of the load. Then Qc = ω C V², so C = Qc / (ω V²).
  3. Work it out. Q1 = 1500 × (0.8 / 0.6) = 2000 VAR. ω = 2π × 50 = 314.16 rad/s. C = 2000 / (314.16 × 52 900) = 2000 / 16 619 025 = 1.2034 × 10⁻⁴ F, which is 120.3 µF.
  4. Check. Multiplying back: 314.16 × 52 900 × 1.2034 × 10⁻⁴ is about 2000. The Python code confirms this.
import math
P = 1500.0
pf1 = 0.6
Q1 = P * math.sqrt(1 - pf1**2) / pf1   # reactive power before correction
w = 2 * math.pi * 50
V = 230.0
C = Q1 / (w * V**2)                    # Qc = omega C V^2, so C = Qc / (omega V^2)
print("Q1 =", round(Q1, 1), "VAR")
print("C =", round(C * 1e6, 1), "microfarad")
Q1 = 2000.0 VAR
C = 120.3 microfarad

Answer: C = 120.3 µF.

Exam-level 3: Power triangle facts (MSQ, 2 marks)

Which are TRUE? (A) |S|² = P² + Q². (B) A pure capacitor absorbs negative reactive power. (C) Power factor can exceed 1. (D) Real power is the average of instantaneous power.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Properties of AC power.
  2. Which idea applies. S = P + jQ.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) True with the usual sign convention. (C) cos φ ≤ 1. False. (D) True.
  4. Check. Reactive power averages to zero over a cycle.

Answer: (A), (B) and (D).

Summary

Active power P
Power that does useful work, in watts. P = VI cos φ.
Reactive power Q
Power that moves back and forth, in VAR. Q = VI sin φ.
Apparent power |S|
Total power the wires carry, in VA. |S| = VI.
Power triangle
Right triangle with P, Q and |S|. |S|² = P² + Q².
Complex power S
S = P + jQ, or S = V I* with phasors.
Power factor
pf = P / |S| = cos φ, between 0 and 1.
Lagging
Current behind voltage. Inductive. Q positive.
Leading
Current ahead of voltage. Capacitive. Q negative.
ELI the ICE man
Voltage leads current in an inductor. Current leads voltage in a capacitor.
Parallel loads
Add P and Q separately, then find |S| with Pythagoras.
Power factor correction
A parallel capacitor reduces the reactive power drawn from the supply.
Correction formula
Qc = P (tan φ1 − tan φ2).
Capacitor reactive power
Qc = ω C V², with ω = 2π f.
RMS value
Vrms = Vm / √2 for a sine wave.
Phase angle φ
The angle between voltage and current phasors.
Units
W for real power, VA for apparent power, VAR for reactive power.
Conjugate
Same length, opposite angle. Gives the correct sign of Q in S = V I*.

Remember these

  1. Real power P does the work, reactive power Q is the back and forth part, and |S| is what the wires carry.
  2. |S|² = P² + Q² is your check every time.
  3. Positive Q means lagging and inductive. Negative Q means leading and capacitive.
  4. For parallel loads, add P and Q separately. Never add apparent powers directly.
  5. Power factor correction changes Q and |S| but not P.
  6. Make sure your calculator is in degree mode before taking the cosine of an angle.

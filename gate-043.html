Correlation and covariance

Covariance and correlation measure whether two quantities tend to rise and fall together. Correlation rescales covariance into a number between -1 and 1, which makes it easy to compare. GATE DA questions use both to test whether you understand what the numbers mean, not only how to calculate them.

The idea in plain words

Think about hours of revision and marks in a test. If students who revise more usually score higher, the two quantities move together. If revision made no difference, knowing one tells you nothing about the other. Covariance and correlation are two numbers that describe this pattern.

Covariance says whether the two quantities move in the same direction, and roughly how strongly. Its size depends on the units, though. Measure hours in minutes and the covariance becomes 60 times larger, even though the relationship is the same. Correlation fixes this by dividing out the spreads, so the result is always between -1 and 1.

In GATE DA, you will meet both in data analysis, regression, and feature selection. Expect questions that ask you to compute them from data or from a joint table, and questions that test what a value like 0 or 0.9 actually tells you.

Key terms

Each entry gives the plain meaning first and the precise definition second.

Random variable
A quantity whose value depends on chance. Precise definition: a function from outcomes to numbers, such as X or Y.
Mean (expectation)
The long-run average. Precise definition: E[X] = sum of x times P(X = x).
Deviation
How far a value is from its mean: x minus E[X].
Covariance
The average of the product of the two deviations. Precise definition: Cov(X, Y) = E[(X - E[X])(Y - E[Y])].
Standard deviation
The typical distance from the mean, in the units of the variable. Precise definition: sigma = square root of Var(X).
Correlation coefficient
Covariance scaled to a fixed range. Precise definition: rho = Cov(X, Y) / (sigma_X times sigma_Y).
Positive correlation
High values of one variable tend to go with high values of the other.
Negative correlation
High values of one tend to go with low values of the other.
Independence
Knowing one variable tells you nothing about the other. Precise definition: P(X = x, Y = y) = P(X = x) times P(Y = y) for all x and y.
Joint distribution
The probabilities of pairs (X = x, Y = y), usually shown as a table.
Causation
One variable actually produces changes in the other. Correlation alone does not prove it.

Covariance: do two variables move together?

Building the idea from deviations

Take five students. Let X be hours of revision and Y be marks. The data are: X = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and Y = 2, 4, 5, 4, 5. The means are E[X] = 3 and E[Y] = 4.

For each student, look at the deviation of X from its mean and the deviation of Y from its mean. Multiply them.

224-100

A positive product means both deviations have the same sign, so both values are on the same side of their means. A negative product means they are on opposite sides. The covariance is the average of these products:

Cov(X, Y) = sum of (x - E[X]) times (y - E[Y]) / n = (4 + 0 + 0 + 0 + 2) / 5 = 6 / 5 = 1.2

Symbols: x and y are the values for one observation, E[X] and E[Y] are their means, and n is the number of observations. The result 1.2 is positive, so the two variables tend to rise together.

The shortcut formula

Cov(X, Y) = E[XY] - E[X] times E[Y]

Here E[XY] is the average of the products x times y, without subtracting the means. For the same data, the products x times y are 2, 8, 15, 16 and 25. Their average is 66 / 5 = 13.2. Then Cov = 13.2 - 3 times 4 = 13.2 - 12 = 1.2. The shortcut gives the same answer, and it is faster for GATE-style calculations.

Key ideaCovariance is positive when large X tends to pair with large Y, negative when large X pairs with small Y, and near zero when the pattern has no direction.

TrapCovariance depends on units. If X is measured in minutes instead of hours, the covariance is multiplied by 60. Its size alone does not say how strong the relationship is.

Self-check: what is Cov(X, X)?

It equals Var(X), because each deviation is multiplied by itself. Covariance of a variable with itself is its variance, which is never negative.

Self-check: E[X] = 2, E[Y] = 3 and E[XY] = 7. What is Cov(X, Y)?

Cov = 7 - 2 times 3 = 1.

Correlation coefficient and its range

Why we rescale

Covariance is hard to read because it carries the units of X times the units of Y. Correlation removes the units by dividing by the two standard deviations:

rho = Cov(X, Y) / (sigma_X times sigma_Y)

Here sigma_X is the standard deviation of X and sigma_Y is the standard deviation of Y. Dividing by them measures covariance in units of "how many standard deviations apart", which is comparable across any pair of variables.

Worked example with the same data

The sum of squared deviations of X is 4 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 4 = 10. For Y it is 4 + 0 + 1 + 0 + 1 = 6. So the population variances are Var(X) = 10 / 5 = 2 and Var(Y) = 6 / 5 = 1.2. Then:

sigma_X = sqrt(2) = 1.4142 and sigma_Y = sqrt(1.2) = 1.0954
rho = 1.2 / (1.4142 times 1.0954) = 1.2 / 1.5492 = 0.7746

The correlation is about 0.77. That is a fairly strong positive relationship. The Python program at the end of this page confirms the value.

The range from -1 to 1

The correlation is always between -1 and 1. A value of 1 means the points lie exactly on a rising straight line. A value of -1 means they lie exactly on a falling line. A value of 0 means no linear pattern. The sign tells you the direction, and the size tells you how tightly the points follow a straight line. A correlation of 0.9 is strong, 0.3 is weak, and -0.8 is strong but negative.

Key ideaCorrelation measures only straight-line association. A strong curved pattern can still give a correlation near zero.

GATE loves thisIf a question asks for correlation and gives covariance and standard deviations, divide. If it asks for covariance and gives correlation and standard deviations, multiply: Cov = rho times sigma_X times sigma_Y.

Self-check: Cov(X, Y) = -4, sigma_X = 2 and sigma_Y = 4. What is rho?

rho = -4 / (2 times 4) = -0.5. The negative sign means X and Y move in opposite directions.

Self-check: if you double every value of X, does rho change?

No. Doubling X doubles both Cov and sigma_X, so the ratio stays the same. Correlation is unchanged by changes of scale.

Reading correlation carefully: causation and zero correlation

Correlation is not causation

Suppose ice cream sales and drowning counts both rise in summer. They are strongly correlated. But ice cream does not cause drowning. The hot weather raises both. A third variable that drives both is called a confounder. Correlation shows that two things move together. It does not show which one, if either, causes the other.

TrapNever write "X causes Y" just because the correlation is high. A correlation can come from a common cause, from chance in a small sample, or from the way the data were collected.

Zero correlation does not mean independence

Correlation only detects straight-line patterns. Consider X taking the values -1, 0 and 1 with equal probability, and let Y = X squared. So Y is 1, 0, 1 for those values. Knowing X tells you Y exactly, so they are clearly dependent. Yet the covariance is zero:

E[X] = 0, E[XY] = E[X^3] = (-1 + 0 + 1) / 3 = 0, so Cov(X, Y) = 0 - 0 times E[Y] = 0

The relationship is a symmetric curve, so the rising and falling parts cancel. The correlation is 0, even though Y depends completely on X.

Key ideaIndependence implies zero correlation, but zero correlation does not imply independence. The reverse direction is false.

Self-check: if X and Y are independent, what is Cov(X, Y)?

Zero. For independent variables, E[XY] = E[X] times E[Y], so the shortcut gives 0.

Self-check: a study finds rho = 0.85 between hours of exercise and mood. Can we conclude exercise causes better mood?

Not from correlation alone. Other factors, or the reverse direction, could explain the pattern. A designed experiment would be needed to claim causation.

Covariance and correlation from a joint distribution

Using probabilities instead of data

In many GATE questions you do not get raw observations. You get a joint table of probabilities. The method is the same, but each average is a weighted average using the probabilities. Here is a small table. X takes values 0 and 1, and Y takes values 0 and 1:

P(X = x, Y = y)Y = 0Y = 1P(X = x)
X = 00.40.10.5
X = 10.10.40.5
P(Y = y)0.50.51.0

Step 1: the means. E[X] = 0 times 0.5 + 1 times 0.5 = 0.5. E[Y] = 0.5 by the same reasoning.

Step 2: E[XY]. Only the pair (1, 1) gives a nonzero product, with value 1 times 1 times 0.4 = 0.4. So E[XY] = 0.4.

Step 3: covariance. Cov = 0.4 - 0.5 times 0.5 = 0.4 - 0.25 = 0.15.

Step 4: variances. Var(X) = E[X^2] - 0.25. Since X takes only 0 and 1, X^2 = X, so E[X^2] = 0.5 and Var(X) = 0.25. The same holds for Y, so Var(Y) = 0.25.

Step 5: correlation. rho = 0.15 / (0.5 times 0.5) = 0.15 / 0.25 = 0.6.

TipWhen X takes only 0 and 1, the variance is P(X = 1) times P(X = 0). This quick rule saves time in exam questions.

TrapE[XY] is not E[X] times E[Y]. Those two are equal only when X and Y are independent, which is exactly what covariance measures departure from.

Self-check: in the table above, is X independent of Y?

No. P(X = 0, Y = 0) = 0.4, but P(X = 0) times P(Y = 0) = 0.25. Since covariance is 0.15, not 0, the variables are dependent.

Self-check: what is the correlation if covariance is 0.15 and both variances are 0.09?

The standard deviations are both 0.3. rho = 0.15 / (0.3 times 0.3) = 0.15 / 0.09 = 1.67, which is impossible. So the covariance cannot be 0.15 with these variances. Correlation must lie between -1 and 1, so check your numbers.

A Python check

This program recomputes the covariance, the shortcut, the correlation, and the zero-covariance example. Exact fractions are used where possible.

from fractions import Fraction as F
import math

x = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]
y = [2, 4, 5, 4, 5]
n = len(x)
mx = F(sum(x), n)
my = F(sum(y), n)
print("mean of X =", mx, "| mean of Y =", my)
cov = sum((a - mx) * (b - my) for a, b in zip(x, y)) / n
print("covariance (divide by n) =", cov)
exy = F(sum(a * b for a, b in zip(x, y)), n)
print("E[XY] - E[X]E[Y] =", exy - mx * my)
sxx = sum((a - mx) ** 2 for a in x)
syy = sum((b - my) ** 2 for b in y)
print("sum of squared deviations X, Y =", sxx, syy)
r = float(cov) / math.sqrt(float(sxx / n) * float(syy / n))
print("correlation =", round(r, 4))

xs = [-1, 0, 1]
ys = [a * a for a in xs]
m1 = F(sum(xs), 3)
m2 = F(sum(ys), 3)
c2 = sum((a - m1) * (b - m2) for a, b in zip(xs, ys)) / 3
print("dependent example covariance =", c2)

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original, GATE-style exercises written for practice. They are not past exam questions. Try each one on paper before reading the answer.

Covariance from E[XY]

E[X] = 2, E[Y] = 5 and E[XY] = 12. Find Cov(X, Y).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The covariance from the given means and E[XY].
  2. Which idea applies. Cov = E[XY] - E[X] times E[Y].
  3. Work it out. E[X] times E[Y] = 2 times 5 = 10. Cov = 12 - 10 = 2.
  4. Check. The covariance is positive, which fits E[XY] being larger than the product of the means.

Answer: 2

Direction of a relationship

Which of the following describes a covariance of -3?

  • (A) X and Y tend to rise together
  • (B) X and Y tend to move in opposite directions
  • (C) X and Y are independent
  • (D) X and Y have equal variances

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The meaning of a negative covariance.
  2. Which idea applies. The sign of covariance gives the direction.
  3. Work it out. A negative average product means deviations usually have opposite signs.
  4. Check. Option (C) would need covariance exactly 0, and (D) says nothing about covariance.

Answer: (B)

Correlation from covariance and SDs

Cov(X, Y) = 6, sigma_X = 3 and sigma_Y = 4. Find the correlation rho. (Give a decimal.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The correlation coefficient.
  2. Which idea applies. rho = Cov / (sigma_X times sigma_Y).
  3. Work it out. rho = 6 / (3 times 4) = 6 / 12 = 0.5.
  4. Check. 0.5 lies between -1 and 1, as it must.

Answer: 0.5

Which statements about correlation are true?

Select all that apply.

  • (A) Correlation is always between -1 and 1.
  • (B) If X and Y are independent, their correlation is 0.
  • (C) If the correlation is 0, X and Y must be independent.
  • (D) Multiplying X by 10 changes the correlation.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Judge each statement.
  2. Which idea applies. The range of correlation, the link between independence and covariance, and scale invariance.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) True, because independence gives zero covariance. (C) False: the example with Y = X squared has zero correlation but is dependent. (D) False: scaling X changes Cov and sigma_X by the same factor.
  4. Check. The counterexample for (C) is X in -1, 0, 1 with Y = X squared.

Answer: (A) and (B). Select all that apply.

Covariance from a joint table

X takes values 0 and 1, and Y takes values 0 and 1. P(X = 0, Y = 0) = 0.3, P(X = 0, Y = 1) = 0.2, P(X = 1, Y = 0) = 0.2 and P(X = 1, Y = 1) = 0.3. Find Cov(X, Y). (Give a decimal.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The covariance from a probability table.
  2. Which idea applies. Compute the marginals, then E[XY] and the shortcut formula.
  3. Work it out. P(X = 1) = 0.5 and P(Y = 1) = 0.5, so E[X] = E[Y] = 0.5. Only the pair (1, 1) has a nonzero product, so E[XY] = 0.3. Cov = 0.3 - 0.5 times 0.5 = 0.05.
  4. Check. The variables agree more often than not, so a small positive covariance is expected.

Answer: 0.05

Covariance of a transformed variable

Let Cov(X, Y) = 4, Var(X) = 9 and Var(Y) = 16. Let U = 2X + 5 and V = 3Y - 1. Find the correlation between U and V. (Give a decimal.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The correlation of two transformed variables.
  2. Which idea applies. Adding a constant changes neither covariance nor variance. Multiplying by a constant multiplies covariance by the product of the constants, and multiplies the SDs by their absolute values.
  3. Work it out. Cov(U, V) = 2 times 3 times Cov(X, Y) = 6 times 4 = 24. sigma_U = 2 times 3 = 6. sigma_V = 3 times 4 = 12. rho = 24 / (6 times 12) = 24 / 72 = 0.3333.
  4. Check. Correlation is unchanged by the constants 5 and minus 1 as well, so it equals the correlation of X and Y, which is 4 / (3 times 4) = 0.3333.

Answer: 0.3333

Which statements are consistent?

Select all that apply. Let X and Y be random variables with Var(X) = 4 and Var(Y) = 9.

  • (A) Cov(X, Y) = 7 is possible.
  • (B) Cov(X, Y) = 6 is possible.
  • (C) rho = 1.5 is possible.
  • (D) Cov(X, Y) = -6 is possible.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which covariances are compatible with these variances.
  2. Which idea applies. Correlation must lie in [-1, 1], so |Cov| must be at most sigma_X times sigma_Y.
  3. Work it out. sigma_X = 2 and sigma_Y = 3, so the largest possible |Cov| is 6. (A) 7 is impossible. (B) 6 gives rho = 1, which is possible. (C) rho = 1.5 is impossible. (D) -6 gives rho = -1, which is possible.
  4. Check. Each possible value of covariance must give a correlation between -1 and 1. Only 6 and minus 6 do so at the boundary.

Answer: (B) and (D). Select all that apply.

Exam-level 1: Correlation of four pairs (NAT, 2 marks)

Four students each record a pair of values: hours of study X and test score Y. The pairs are (1, 2), (2, 4), (3, 5) and (4, 9). Treat the four pairs as equally likely outcomes. Find the correlation coefficient r between X and Y, to 3 decimal places. The correlation is the same whether you divide by n or by n - 1, so use whichever method you find easier.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The correlation coefficient r, which is covariance divided by the two standard deviations.
  2. Which idea applies. Work with deviations from the means, then divide the covariance by the square roots of the two sums of squares.
  3. Work it out. The mean of X is 2.5 and the mean of Y is 5. The products of deviations are (-1.5)(-3) = 4.5, (-0.5)(-1) = 0.5, (0.5)(0) = 0 and (1.5)(4) = 6, which add to 11. The sums of squares are 5 for X and 26 for Y. So r = 11 / square root of (5 times 26) = 11 / square root of 130, which is 0.965.
  4. Check. r lies between -1 and 1, and a value near 1 matches the data rising steadily from left to right.

Answer: r = 0.965

from fractions import Fraction as F
xs = [1, 2, 3, 4]
ys = [2, 4, 5, 9]
mx = F(sum(xs), len(xs))
my = F(sum(ys), len(ys))
sxy = sum((x - mx) * (y - my) for x, y in zip(xs, ys))
sxx = sum((x - mx) ** 2 for x in xs)
syy = sum((y - my) ** 2 for y in ys)
print(f"population covariance = {float(sxy / 4)}")
print(f"sample covariance = {round(float(sxy / 3), 3)}")
print(f"correlation r = {round(float(sxy) / (float(sxx) * float(syy)) ** 0.5, 3)}")
population covariance = 2.75
sample covariance = 3.667
correlation r = 0.965

Exam-level 2: Which correlation statements hold (MSQ, 2 marks)

Let X and Y be random variables with finite variances. Select all that apply.

  • (A) If Cov(X, Y) = 0, then X and Y are independent.
  • (B) If Y is replaced by 5Y + 3, the correlation between X and Y does not change.
  • (C) The correlation coefficient equals -1 only when Y = aX + b for some constant a below 0 and some constant b.
  • (D) Cov(2X, 3Y) = 5 Cov(X, Y).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements about covariance and correlation hold in general.
  2. Which idea applies. Covariance is bilinear, correlation ignores positive scaling and shifting, and a counterexample can disprove a claim.
  3. Work it out. (A) False: take X equal to -1, 0 or 1 with equal chance and Y = X^2. Then Cov(X, Y) = 0, but Y depends on X, as the Python block shows. (B) True: positive scaling and shifting leave correlation unchanged. (C) True: correlation -1 is a perfect straight line with negative slope. (D) False: Cov(2X, 3Y) = 2 times 3 times Cov(X, Y) = 6 Cov(X, Y), not 5.
  4. Check. The counterexample in (A) has Cov = 0 but P(X = 0 and Y = 0) = 1/3, which is not 1/9 = P(X = 0) times P(Y = 0), so X and Y are dependent.

Answer: (B) and (C). Select all that apply.

from fractions import Fraction as F
pts = [(-1, 1), (0, 0), (1, 1)]
p = F(1, 3)
EX = sum(x * p for x, _ in pts)
EY = sum(y * p for _, y in pts)
EXY = sum(x * y * p for x, y in pts)
pX0 = sum(p for x, _ in pts if x == 0)
pY0 = sum(p for _, y in pts if y == 0)
pBoth = sum(p for x, y in pts if x == 0 and y == 0)
print(f"Cov(X, Y) = {EXY - EX * EY}")
print(f"P(X = 0 and Y = 0) = {pBoth}, but P(X = 0) times P(Y = 0) = {pX0 * pY0}")
Cov(X, Y) = 0
P(X = 0 and Y = 0) = 1/3, but P(X = 0) times P(Y = 0) = 1/9

Exam-level 3: Variance of a difference (NAT, 2 marks)

The joint probabilities of two binary variables X and Y are P(X = 0, Y = 0) = 0.4, P(X = 0, Y = 1) = 0.1, P(X = 1, Y = 0) = 0.1 and P(X = 1, Y = 1) = 0.4. Find the variance of the random variable 2X - Y, using Var(aX + bY) = a^2 Var(X) + b^2 Var(Y) + 2ab Cov(X, Y). Give a decimal.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The variance of one combined variable, 2X - Y.
  2. Which idea applies. The variance formula for a linear combination, which needs Var(X), Var(Y) and Cov(X, Y) from the joint table.
  3. Work it out. E[X] = 0.5 and E[Y] = 0.5. Since X is 0 or 1, E[X^2] = E[X] = 0.5, so Var(X) = 0.5 - 0.25 = 0.25. The same holds for Y, so Var(Y) = 0.25. E[XY] = P(X = 1, Y = 1) = 0.4, so Cov(X, Y) = 0.4 - 0.25 = 0.15. Then Var(2X - Y) = 4 times 0.25 + 0.25 + 2 times 2 times (-1) times 0.15 = 1 + 0.25 - 0.6 = 0.65.
  4. Check. A positive covariance means X and Y tend to move together, which reduces the spread of the difference 2X - Y, so 0.65 is less than the 1.25 that the variances alone would give.

Answer: 0.65

from fractions import Fraction as F
joint = {(0, 0): F(4, 10), (0, 1): F(1, 10), (1, 0): F(1, 10), (1, 1): F(4, 10)}
EX = sum(x * p for (x, y), p in joint.items())
EY = sum(y * p for (x, y), p in joint.items())
EXY = sum(x * y * p for (x, y), p in joint.items())
EX2 = sum(x * x * p for (x, y), p in joint.items())
EY2 = sum(y * y * p for (x, y), p in joint.items())
VarX = EX2 - EX ** 2
VarY = EY2 - EY ** 2
Cov = EXY - EX * EY
print(f"Cov(X, Y) = {float(Cov)}")
print(f"Var(X) = {float(VarX)}, Var(Y) = {float(VarY)}")
print(f"Var(2X - Y) = {float(4 * VarX + VarY - 4 * Cov)}")
Cov(X, Y) = 0.15
Var(X) = 0.25, Var(Y) = 0.25
Var(2X - Y) = 0.65

Exam-level 4: Correlation of two combinations (NAT, 2 marks)

The random variables X and Y have Var(X) = 4, Var(Y) = 9 and Cov(X, Y) = 3. Define U = X - Y and V = 2X + Y. Find the correlation coefficient between U and V, to 3 decimal places. Use bilinearity of covariance: Cov(aX + bY, cX + dY) = ac Var(X) + (ad + bc) Cov(X, Y) + bd Var(Y). Remember that the correlation divides the covariance by both standard deviations.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The correlation between two new variables built from X and Y.
  2. Which idea applies. Compute Var(U), Var(V) and Cov(U, V) with the bilinearity formula, then divide.
  3. Work it out. Var(U) = 4 + 9 - 2 times 3 = 7. Var(V) = 4 times 4 + 9 + 2 times 2 times 1 times 3 = 16 + 9 + 12 = 37. Cov(U, V) with a = 1, b = -1, c = 2, d = 1 is 2 times 4 + (1 - 2) times 3 + (-1) times 9 = 8 - 3 - 9 = -4. So corr(U, V) = -4 / square root of (7 times 37) = -4 / 16.093 = -0.249.
  4. Check. The covariance is negative, so the sign of the correlation must be negative, and its size is below 1.

Answer: -0.249

import math
vx, vy, c = 4, 9, 3
vu = vx + vy - 2 * c
vv = 4 * vx + 4 * c + vy
cuv = 2 * vx + (1 - 2) * c - vy
print(f"Var(U) = {vu}")
print(f"Var(V) = {vv}")
print(f"Cov(U, V) = {cuv}")
print(f"corr(U, V) = {round(cuv / math.sqrt(vu * vv), 3)}")
Var(U) = 7
Var(V) = 37
Cov(U, V) = -4
corr(U, V) = -0.249

Summary

Covariance
Average product of deviations from the two means. Sign gives direction.
Covariance shortcut
Cov(X, Y) = E[XY] - E[X] times E[Y].
Correlation
Cov divided by the product of the two SDs. Always between -1 and 1.
Positive correlation
High values tend to pair with high values.
Negative correlation
High values tend to pair with low values.
Zero correlation
No straight-line pattern. It does not rule out a curved one.
Independence
Knowing X tells nothing about Y. Implies zero covariance.
Causation
One variable produces changes in the other. Needs more than correlation to show.
Confounder
A third variable that drives both X and Y.
Scale invariance
Correlation does not change when X or Y is multiplied by a positive constant.
Shift invariance
Adding constants to X or Y does not change covariance or correlation.
Joint table
Probabilities of pairs, used to compute E[XY] and covariance.
Cov(X, X)
Equals Var(X).
Bound on covariance
|Cov(X, Y)| is at most sigma_X times sigma_Y.

Remember these

  1. Covariance tells direction and depends on units. Correlation is unitless and lies between -1 and 1.
  2. Use Cov = E[XY] - E[X] times E[Y] for joint tables.
  3. Correlation measures straight-line association only.
  4. Correlation is not causation.
  5. Zero covariance does not imply independence. Independence does imply zero covariance.
  6. Shifts do not change covariance or correlation. Scaling changes covariance, but correlation is unchanged by positive scaling.

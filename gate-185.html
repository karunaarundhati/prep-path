Failure theories

A failure theory is a rule that tells us when a part made of a given material will fail under a complicated state of stress. Four classic rules exist, and GATE Robotics and Automation questions ask you to pick the right one and use it to check safety.

The idea in plain words

Welcome. This page is about one simple question that engineers ask every day: "Will this part break?" If a rope is pulled with 500 newtons, we can check whether it snaps. But a machine part is rarely pulled in just one direction. It is pushed, twisted and bent at the same time. So we need a rule that takes all of those stresses together and answers the question "safe or not safe?" Such a rule is called a failure theory (also called a failure criterion).

Think of a sponge in your hand. You can squeeze it, twist it and stretch it. The sponge does not break in the same way each time. A failure theory is like a set of simple guesses about when the sponge has had enough. Engineers have four classic guesses for metals. Each one works well for some materials and poorly for others. Choosing the right guess is part of the skill.

Why does this matter for GATE? In the Machine Design and CIM part of the Robotics and Automation paper, you will often see a stress state given as numbers, a material strength given, and a question asking for the factor of safety "using the maximum shear stress theory" or "using the distortion energy theory". If you know the formula and the picture behind each theory, these questions become a few clean steps of arithmetic.

We will go slowly. First we define the words, then we study each theory with one formula and one small example, then we compare them and practise. Every number in the worked examples is small, and every step is shown.

Key terms

Read this list once now, and come back to it when a word feels new. Each term has a plain meaning first, then a precise definition.

Stress
The internal force per unit area inside a material. Precisely: force divided by the area it acts on, measured in N/mm2 or MPa (1 MPa = 1 N/mm2).
Principal stress
The stress in a special direction where there is no shear stress. Precisely: the normal stresses sigma1, sigma2, sigma3 on planes where the shear stress is zero, with sigma1 the largest.
Shear stress
A stress that tries to slide one layer of material past the next. Precisely: force parallel to a surface, divided by that surface area. Written tau.
Failure theory
A rule that combines several stresses into one check. Precisely: a condition, in terms of stresses and a strength value, that predicts failure.
Ductile material
A material that stretches a lot before breaking, like mild steel or aluminium. Precisely: a material with a clear yield point and a large elongation at fracture.
Brittle material
A material that breaks suddenly with little stretching, like cast iron or glass. Precisely: a material with little or no yield region, whose failure is by fracture.
Yield strength (Sy)
The stress at which a ductile material starts to deform permanently. Precisely: the stress at the yield point, often defined as the stress giving 0.2 percent permanent strain.
Ultimate tensile strength (Sut)
The largest stress a material can take in a simple pull test before it breaks. Precisely: maximum engineering stress in a tension test.
Factor of safety (n)
How many times stronger the part is than the load it sees. Precisely: strength divided by the stress that the theory compares with it. Safe when n is greater than 1.
Distortion energy
The part of the stored energy that changes the shape of a body, rather than its volume. Precisely: the energy per unit volume that causes angular distortion of a small element.
Poisson's ratio (nu)
The ratio of sideways shrinking to lengthwise stretching in a bar. Precisely: negative of lateral strain divided by axial strain, typically about 0.3 for steel.
Plane stress
A state where one principal stress is zero, as on the free surface of a thin plate. Precisely: sigma3 = 0 with sigma1 and sigma2 acting in the plane.
Stress-space diagram
A flat graph with sigma1 on one axis and sigma2 on the other. Precisely: a plot where the boundary of each theory separates safe stress pairs from unsafe ones.

Stress in two directions and principal stresses

Before any theory can be used, we need the principal stresses. Imagine a small square cut out of a plate. The square has normal stresses sigmax (pulling sideways) and sigmay (pulling up), and a shear stress tauxy that tries to skew the square. If you turn the square until the shear disappears, the two normal stresses left over are the principal stresses. Turning the square is like rotating a picture until it looks straight.

The formula for the two principal stresses in plane stress is the following.

sigma1,2 = (sigmax + sigmay)/2 ± sqrt( ((sigmax − sigmay)/2)2 + tauxy2 )

Here sigmax and sigmay are the normal stresses, tauxy is the shear stress, and the two answers are sigma1 (the larger) and sigma2 (the smaller). The first part, (sigmax + sigmay)/2, is the average. The square root is the radius of a circle of stress, which tells us how far the principal stresses spread from the average.

Worked example. Take sigmax = 90 MPa, sigmay = 30 MPa, and tauxy = 40 MPa.

  1. The average is (90 + 30)/2 = 60.
  2. Half the difference is (90 − 30)/2 = 30.
  3. The radius is the square root of (30 squared + 40 squared) = square root of (900 + 1600) = square root of 2500 = 50.
  4. So sigma1 = 60 + 50 = 110 MPa, and sigma2 = 60 − 50 = 10 MPa.

The third principal stress sigma3 is zero, because the plate's free surface carries no stress across its thickness. This is plane stress.

TipThe numbers 30, 40, 50 are a Pythagorean triple (3, 4, 5 scaled by 10). Such triples are a gift in exam questions, because the square root comes out as a whole number. Spot them quickly.

The Python check below repeats the same steps, so you can see that the numbers are right.

import math
sx, sy, txy = 90, 30, 40
center = (sx + sy) / 2
R = math.sqrt(((sx - sy) / 2) ** 2 + txy ** 2)
s1 = center + R
s2 = center - R
print("sigma1 =", s1, "MPa")
print("sigma2 =", s2, "MPa")
sigma1 = 110.0 MPa
sigma2 = 10.0 MPa
If the shear stress tauxy is zero, what are the principal stresses?

The radius becomes zero, so the principal stresses are just sigmax and sigmay. The shear is already gone, so the given stresses are already principal.

Why do GATE questions ask for principal stresses?

Every classic failure theory is written in terms of principal stresses. Once you have sigma1 and sigma2, the check is simple algebra.

Ductile and brittle materials: which failure is expected

Materials do not all fail the same way. A paper clip bent back and forth becomes permanently bent first (it yields) and then snaps. A cast iron pipe may crack with almost no warning. These two behaviours are the reason we have different theories.

A ductile material, such as mild steel, first deforms permanently at its yield strength. Engineers usually design it so that yielding never happens, so the check is "does the stress reach the yield strength?" For ductile materials the most popular rules are the maximum shear stress theory (Tresca) and the maximum distortion energy theory (von Mises).

A brittle material, such as cast iron or ceramic, breaks by fracture, and its strength in tension is much lower than its strength in compression. For brittle materials the usual rule is the maximum principal stress theory (Rankine), with the tensile strength Sut as the limit.

An everyday comparison helps. Think of chewing toffee (ductile, it bends a lot before it breaks) and biting a biscuit (brittle, it snaps). Both can be broken, but you judge the danger in different ways.

Key ideaDuctile material: check against yield (Tresca or von Mises). Brittle material: check against fracture (Rankine). Maximum strain is historically linked to brittle materials, but it is also taught as a general theory in GATE syllabi, so know its formula.

TrapDo not choose the theory by the name of the metal alone. Read the question. If it says "cast iron" or "brittle", use the maximum principal stress theory. If it says "steel" or "ductile", use Tresca or von Mises, unless the question tells you otherwise.

Which theory is most suitable for a ductile steel shaft under combined torque and bending?

Maximum shear stress (Tresca) or maximum distortion energy (von Mises). Von Mises is the closer match for most ductile metals, and Tresca is the more conservative choice.

What is the typical choice for a cast iron component?

Maximum principal stress theory (Rankine), compared with the tensile strength of the cast iron.

The four classic theories, one by one

Each theory below has a rule, a formula, and a small example. Read them in order, because the later ones are easier once you understand the first.

Theory 1: Maximum principal stress (Rankine)

Idea. The part fails when its biggest principal stress reaches the strength of the material. It is like saying "the strongest pull on the part is the one that matters". This is the natural theory for brittle materials, which fracture when pulled.

Failure when sigma1 = Sut (brittle), and n = Sut / sigma1

Here sigma1 is the largest principal stress, Sut is the tensile strength, and n is the factor of safety.

Worked example. A cast iron part has sigma1 = 110 MPa (from the previous section) and Sut = 150 MPa.

  1. Write the formula n = Sut/sigma1.
  2. Substitute: n = 150/110.
  3. Divide: 150/110 = 1.364.

The factor of safety is about 1.36. That is greater than 1, so the part is safe by this theory.

TrapRankine uses the largest principal stress only. If sigma2 is also large, Rankine does not care. This is why it is not used for ductile metals, which yield under shear even when no single stress is large.

Theory 2: Maximum shear stress (Tresca)

Idea. A ductile material yields when the largest shear stress reaches half of the yield strength. The largest shear in a stress state is half the difference between the largest and the smallest principal stresses. Think of a deck of cards pushed sideways: the sliding is what starts the permanent damage.

taumax = (sigma1 − sigma3)/2. Failure when taumax = Sy/2, so n = Sy/(sigma1 − sigma3)

Here sigma3 is the smallest principal stress, and Sy is the yield strength. Notice that sigma3 matters. In plane stress with a free surface, sigma3 = 0, so the formula becomes n = Sy/sigma1 when both principal stresses are positive.

Worked example. The same part is now made of ductile steel with Sy = 200 MPa. The principal stresses are sigma1 = 110 MPa, sigma2 = 10 MPa, and sigma3 = 0.

  1. Compute the difference: sigma1 − sigma3 = 110 − 0 = 110 MPa.
  2. Write n = Sy/110 = 200/110.
  3. Divide: 200/110 = 1.818.

The factor of safety is about 1.82.

Key ideaTresca depends on the difference between the largest and the smallest principal stress. Every time you use it, first find sigma1 and sigma3, with the correct order and sign.

Theory 3: Maximum distortion energy (von Mises-Hencky)

Idea. Any stress state stores energy in the material. Part of that energy changes the volume and part changes the shape. The von Mises theory says that yielding starts when the shape-changing (distortion) energy reaches the value it has in a simple tension test at yield. It is the most widely used rule for ductile metals.

The check uses an equivalent stress called the von Mises stress, sigma', which is a single number that stands for the whole stress state.

sigma' = square root of ( (sigma1 − sigma2)2 + (sigma2 − sigma3)2 + (sigma3 − sigma1)2 ) / 2 )
For plane stress: sigma' = square root of (sigma12 − sigma1 sigma2 + sigma22), and n = Sy/sigma'

Each term is a square of a difference between two principal stresses, and the square root gives one equivalent stress that you compare with Sy.

Worked example. Use the same stress state: sigma1 = 110 MPa, sigma2 = 10 MPa, and Sy = 200 MPa.

  1. Square sigma1: 110 squared = 12100.
  2. Multiply sigma1 by sigma2: 110 times 10 = 1100.
  3. Square sigma2: 10 squared = 100.
  4. Add and subtract: 12100 − 1100 + 100 = 11100.
  5. Take the square root: sqrt(11100) = 105.36 MPa.
  6. Divide: n = 200/105.36 = 1.898.

GATE loves thisFor plane stress, the von Mises stress has the short form sigma12 − sigma1 sigma2 + sigma22. Memorise that form. It saves time in the exam.

Theory 4: Maximum strain (Saint-Venant)

Idea. This theory says that failure happens when the largest strain (stretch per unit length) reaches the strain at yield in a simple test. Strain is not the same as stress, because a material also shrinks sideways when it is stretched. That sideways shrinking is controlled by Poisson's ratio nu.

epsilon1 = (sigma1 − nu (sigma2 + sigma3)) / E. Failure when epsilon1 = Sy/E. For plane stress: sigma1 − nu sigma2 = Sy, so n = Sy/(sigma1 − nu sigma2)

Here E is Young's modulus (the stiffness of the material), nu is Poisson's ratio, and epsilon1 is the largest strain.

Worked example. Take nu = 0.3, sigma1 = 110 MPa, sigma2 = 10 MPa, and Sy = 200 MPa.

  1. Compute nu times sigma2: 0.3 times 10 = 3.
  2. Subtract: 110 − 3 = 107 MPa.
  3. Divide: n = 200/107 = 1.869.

TrapBe careful with the sign of sigma2. If sigma2 is negative (a compression), then nu times sigma2 is negative, and the subtraction becomes an addition. Always substitute the signed value.

For pure shear in plane stress, which ductile theory predicts yielding at the smaller shear stress?

Tresca. For pure shear, sigma1 = tau and sigma2 = -tau. Tresca gives tau = Sy/2, while von Mises gives tau = Sy/square root of 3, which is about 0.577 Sy. Tresca is the more conservative theory.

Why is the von Mises stress written with squares?

Squares make each term positive whatever the sign of the difference, so the result measures the size of the stress differences in any direction. This matches the idea of energy, which is always positive.

Comparing theories on a stress-space diagram

Now we put the theories side by side. Draw a flat graph. The horizontal axis is sigma1 and the vertical axis is sigma2. Every point on the graph is a possible plane stress state. Each theory draws a boundary. Inside the boundary the part is safe. On the boundary, the part is just at failure. Outside, it fails. The size of each shape is measured in units of Sy squared, so one unit means Sy = 1.

  • Rankine (maximum principal stress) draws a square with corners at (Sy, Sy), (Sy, -Sy), (-Sy, Sy) and (-Sy, -Sy). Its area is 4 Sy squared.
  • Tresca (maximum shear) draws a six-sided figure, a hexagon, with corners at (Sy, 0), (Sy, Sy), (0, Sy), and their opposite points. Its area is 3 Sy squared.
  • von Mises (distortion energy) draws an ellipse, a stretched circle, that passes through the six corners of the Tresca hexagon. Its area is about 3.63 Sy squared.

The ellipse touches the hexagon at its six corners. Between those corners the ellipse bulges outward. So the Tresca safe region is always inside the von Mises safe region, which means Tresca never allows a stress state that von Mises rejects. Tresca is the more conservative of the two in every direction.

To see the Tresca and von Mises difference with numbers, look at the pure shear direction where sigma1 = s and sigma2 = -s. Tresca fails when 2s = Sy, so s = 0.5 Sy. Von Mises fails when 3 s squared = Sy squared, so s = 0.577 Sy. The Python check below prints both limits and the three areas.

import math
Sy = 200
print("Tresca limit on s:", Sy / 2, "MPa")
print("von Mises limit on s:", round(Sy / math.sqrt(3), 2), "MPa")
hexa = [(1, 0), (1, 1), (0, 1), (-1, 0), (-1, -1), (0, -1)]
area = 0
for i in range(6):
    x1, y1 = hexa[i]
    x2, y2 = hexa[(i + 1) % 6]
    area += x1 * y2 - x2 * y1
print("Tresca area (units of Sy^2):", abs(area) / 2)
print("von Mises area (units of Sy^2):", round(2 * math.pi / math.sqrt(3), 3))
print("Rankine area (units of Sy^2):", 4)
Tresca limit on s: 100.0 MPa
von Mises limit on s: 115.47 MPa
Tresca area (units of Sy^2): 3.0
von Mises area (units of Sy^2): 3.628
Rankine area (units of Sy^2): 4

Key ideaRead the picture this way: the Tresca hexagon is the strictest boundary of the three, the von Mises ellipse sits outside it, and the Rankine square is a different shape with its own strengths and weaknesses. For ductile metals, the design choice is almost always Tresca or von Mises.

Which theory's safe region is entirely inside the von Mises ellipse?

The Tresca hexagon. Its six corners touch the ellipse and its edges lie inside it, so any stress state that is safe by Tresca is also safe by von Mises.

Why does a stress-space diagram help?

It shows all theories on one picture. You can see at a glance which theory is strictest for a given direction of loading, without redoing the algebra for each case.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original, written in the GATE style. They are not copied from any real exam paper. For each one, read the question, then try the "How to think about it" steps on your own before you read the answer. The eight problems go from warm-up to hard.

Tresca on a plane stress plate

A ductile steel plate with yield strength Sy = 250 MPa is under plane stress with sigmax = 80 MPa, sigmay = 20 MPa and tauxy = 40 MPa. Using the maximum shear stress theory, find the factor of safety. (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A factor of safety n, using Tresca, for a ductile material.
  2. Which idea applies. Find the principal stresses first. Then use n = Sy/(sigma1 − sigma3).
  3. Work it out. The average of 80 and 20 is 50. The radius is the square root of (30 squared + 40 squared) = 50. So sigma1 = 100 and sigma2 = 0. In plane stress sigma3 = 0, so sigma1 − sigma3 = 100. Then n = 250/100 = 2.5.
  4. Check. The stress 100 MPa is less than the yield strength 250 MPa, so n must be greater than 1. The value 2.5 is consistent.

Answer: n = 2.5.

Rankine on a cast iron bracket

A cast iron bracket has tensile strength Sut = 200 MPa. At a critical point the largest principal stress is sigma1 = 100 MPa and the other principal stress is 0. Using the maximum principal stress theory, what is the factor of safety? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A factor of safety for a brittle material, so the Rankine theory.
  2. Which idea applies. For brittle material, n = Sut/sigma1.
  3. Work it out. Substitute: n = 200/100. Divide: n = 2.0.
  4. Check. Stress is half the strength, so a factor of 2 is what we expect.

Answer: n = 2.0.

Which statements about the theories are right?

Consider the following statements about failure theories for plane stress. Select all that apply.

  • (A) For any plane stress state, if the Tresca theory predicts safety then the von Mises theory also predicts safety.
  • (B) The maximum principal stress theory is the recommended theory for ductile steel.
  • (C) The maximum distortion energy theory uses all three principal stresses in its general form.
  • (D) The maximum strain theory depends on Poisson's ratio.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which of the four statements are correct. This is an MSQ, so more than one answer can be right.
  2. Which idea applies. The stress-space picture: the Tresca hexagon lies inside the von Mises ellipse. Rankine is for brittle metals. Von Mises uses all three principal stresses in its general form. Maximum strain has nu in its formula.
  3. Work it out. (A) Tresca's safe region lies inside von Mises's, so this is correct. (B) Rankine is for brittle materials, so this is wrong for ductile steel. (C) The general von Mises form has (sigma1 − sigma2)2, (sigma2 − sigma3)2 and (sigma3 − sigma1)2, so this is correct. (D) The strain formula has nu times sigma2, so this is correct.
  4. Check. Look back at the picture: the Tresca hexagon touches the ellipse at six corners, which supports (A).

Answer: (A), (C) and (D) are correct. (B) is wrong.

Von Mises on a state with equal normal stresses

A ductile part with Sy = 200 MPa is in plane stress with sigmax = 10 MPa, sigmay = 10 MPa and tauxy = 40 MPa. Using the maximum distortion energy theory, find the factor of safety to two decimal places. (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A factor of safety n, by von Mises, to two decimals.
  2. Which idea applies. First principal stresses, then sigma' = square root of (sigma12 − sigma1 sigma2 + sigma22), then n = Sy/sigma'.
  3. Work it out. The average is (10 + 10)/2 = 10. The radius is sqrt(0 + 40 squared) = 40. So sigma1 = 50 and sigma2 = -30. Then sigma'2 = 502 - 50(-30) + (-30)2 = 2500 + 1500 + 900 = 4900, so sigma' = 70. Then n = 200/70 = 2.857, which rounds to 2.86.
  4. Check. The sign of sigma2 is negative, so the middle term becomes an addition. That is why sigma' is larger than 50 but smaller than 80. The value 70 is in that range.

Answer: n = 2.86.

Maximum strain with Poisson's ratio

Use the same stress state as the previous problem: sigmax = 10 MPa, sigmay = 10 MPa, tauxy = 40 MPa, Sy = 200 MPa. Use the maximum strain theory with Poisson's ratio nu = 0.3. Find the factor of safety to two decimal places. (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A factor of safety by maximum strain, with nu given.
  2. Which idea applies. For plane stress, n = Sy/(sigma1 − nu sigma2).
  3. Work it out. From the previous problem, sigma1 = 50 and sigma2 = -30. Then nu sigma2 = 0.3 times (-30) = -9. So sigma1 − nu sigma2 = 50 - (-9) = 59. Then n = 200/59 = 3.39.
  4. Check. The number 3.39 is larger than the von Mises answer 2.86. That makes sense here, because the sign of sigma2 makes the strain theory less strict for this state.

Answer: n = 3.39.

Which ductile theory governs the design?

A ductile steel component with Sy = 200 MPa has sigmax = 90 MPa, sigmay = 30 MPa and tauxy = 40 MPa, in plane stress. Its design rule says that the factor of safety must be taken as the smallest of the three values given by the Tresca, von Mises and maximum strain theories (nu = 0.3). What is that smallest factor of safety, to two decimal places? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Three factors of safety, then the smallest one.
  2. Which idea applies. Principal stresses first. Then the three formulas for a ductile material in plane stress.
  3. Work it out. The average is 60 and the radius is 50, so sigma1 = 110 and sigma2 = 10. Tresca: n = 200/110 = 1.82. Von Mises: sigma' = sqrt(1102 - 110 times 10 + 102) = sqrt(11100) = 105.36, so n = 1.90. Maximum strain: n = 200/(110 - 0.3 times 10) = 200/107 = 1.87. The smallest is 1.82.
  4. Check. Tresca is the most conservative theory, so it should give the smallest value for ductile metals. The answer matches that pattern.

Answer: 1.82 (from the Tresca theory).

How much shear can the plate take?

A ductile plate with Sy = 200 MPa is in plane stress with sigmax = 60 MPa, sigmay = 0 and an unknown shear stress tauxy. The design requires a factor of safety of 2.0 using the maximum distortion energy theory. What is the largest allowed tauxy, in MPa, to two decimal places? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The largest shear stress that still gives n = 2.0 by von Mises.
  2. Which idea applies. With n = 2, the von Mises stress may be at most Sy/2 = 100 MPa. Then use the principal stresses in terms of tau.
  3. Work it out. The average is 30 and the radius is R = sqrt(302 + tau2). So sigma1 = 30 + R and sigma2 = 30 - R. Using the form sigma'2 = sigma12 - sigma1 sigma2 + sigma22, we get sigma'2 = 3600 + 3 tau2. Set this equal to 1002 = 10000. Then 3 tau2 = 6400, so tau2 = 2133.3 and tau = 46.19 MPa.
  4. Check. Put tau = 46.19 back in. The principal stresses are about 85.07 and -25.07, the von Mises stress is about 100, and n is about 2.00. The check works.

Answer: about 46.19 MPa.

Brittle material in tension and compression

A brittle casting has tensile strength Sut = 150 MPa and compressive strength Suc = 600 MPa. At a point the principal stresses are sigma1 = 50 MPa (tension) and sigma2 = -250 MPa (compression). Using the maximum principal stress theory, checking both the tension and the compression, what is the lowest factor of safety? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The smallest factor of safety when the material is brittle and has different strengths in tension and compression.
  2. Which idea applies. For brittle material, check the tensile principal stress against Sut, and the compressive principal stress against Suc. The lowest factor governs.
  3. Work it out. Tension: nt = 150/50 = 3.0. Compression: nc = 600/250 = 2.4. The lowest value is 2.4.
  4. Check. The compression is the more dangerous part here, because the stress is 250 MPa against a strength of 600 MPa, while the tension is 50 MPa against 150 MPa. So the answer should come from compression, and it does.

Answer: 2.4 (governed by the compression check).

import math
def principal(sx, sy, txy):
    c = (sx + sy) / 2
    R = math.sqrt(((sx - sy) / 2) ** 2 + txy ** 2)
    return c + R, c - R
s1, s2 = principal(80, 20, 40)
print("P1 sigma1, sigma2:", s1, s2)
print("P1 Tresca n:", 250 / (s1 - 0))
print("P2 Rankine n:", 200 / 100)
s1, s2 = principal(10, 10, 40)
print("P4 sigma1, sigma2:", s1, s2)
print("P4 Tresca n:", 200 / (s1 - s2))
svm = math.sqrt(s1 ** 2 - s1 * s2 + s2 ** 2)
print("P4 von Mises stress:", svm)
print("P4 von Mises n:", round(200 / svm, 2))
print("P5 max strain n:", round(200 / (s1 - 0.3 * s2), 2))
s1, s2 = principal(90, 30, 40)
n_t = 200 / (s1 - 0)
n_v = 200 / math.sqrt(s1 ** 2 - s1 * s2 + s2 ** 2)
n_s = 200 / (s1 - 0.3 * s2)
print("P6 Tresca, von Mises, strain:", round(n_t, 2), round(n_v, 2), round(n_s, 2))
tau = math.sqrt((100 ** 2 - 3600) / 3)
print("P7 tau max:", round(tau, 2))
s1, s2 = principal(60, 0, round(tau, 2))
print("P7 check n:", round(200 / math.sqrt(s1 ** 2 - s1 * s2 + s2 ** 2), 2))
print("P8 tension n, compression n:", 150 / 50, round(600 / 250, 2))

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Von Mises (NAT, 2 marks)

Plane stress σ1 = 120 MPa, σ2 = −60 MPa. Sy = 300 MPa. Find the von Mises factor of safety, correct to 2 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. n = Sy/σvm.
  2. Which idea applies. σvm = √(σ1² − σ1σ2 + σ2²).
  3. Work it out. √(14400 + 7200 + 3600) = 158.75. n = 1.89.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
import math
vm = math.sqrt(120 ** 2 - 120 * -60 + 60 ** 2)
print("%.2f, n = %.2f" % (vm, 300 / vm))

158.75, n = 1.89

Answer: about 1.89.

Exam-level 2: Tresca (NAT, 2 marks)

For the same state, find the Tresca factor of safety.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. n = Sy/(σ1 − σ3).
  2. Which idea applies. In plane stress with σ1 > 0 > σ2, the max shear uses σ1 − σ2.
  3. Work it out. 300/180 = 1.67.
  4. Check. Tresca is more conservative than von Mises.

Answer: about 1.67.

Exam-level 3: Theory choice (MSQ, 2 marks)

Which are TRUE? (A) Ductile materials are best predicted by von Mises. (B) Brittle materials suit the maximum normal stress theory. (C) Tresca is always more conservative than von Mises. (D) Under pure shear, von Mises predicts yield at τ = 0.577Sy.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Failure theory facts.
  2. Which idea applies. In pure shear, Tresca gives 0.5Sy and von Mises gives Sy/√3.
  3. Work it out. All four are true (Tresca never gives a higher allowable than von Mises).
  4. Check. The two agree for uniaxial stress.

Answer: (A), (B), (C) and (D).

Summary

Here are the one-line meanings of the main ideas on this page.

Failure theory
A rule that turns several stresses into one safe or unsafe answer.
Principal stress
A normal stress on a plane where the shear stress is zero.
Plane stress
A state where one principal stress is zero, as on a thin plate's surface.
Ductile material
Stretches a lot and yields before breaking, such as mild steel.
Brittle material
Breaks suddenly with little stretching, such as cast iron.
Yield strength Sy
The stress where a ductile material starts to deform permanently.
Tensile strength Sut
The largest stress in a simple pull test before fracture.
Rankine theory
Maximum principal stress theory, used for brittle materials.
Tresca theory
Maximum shear stress theory, failure when shear reaches half the yield strength.
von Mises theory
Maximum distortion energy theory, the most common ductile check.
Maximum strain theory
Failure when the largest strain reaches the yield strain, using Poisson's ratio.
von Mises stress
One equivalent stress that stands for the whole plane stress state.
Poisson's ratio nu
Sideways shrinkage divided by lengthwise stretch, about 0.3 for steel.
Factor of safety n
Strength divided by the stress the theory compares with it. Safe when greater than 1.
Stress-space diagram
A graph of sigma1 against sigma2 showing each theory's safe region.
Tresca hexagon
The strictest safe region for ductile material, inside the von Mises ellipse.
Asymmetric strength
A brittle material with different tensile and compressive strengths, checked in both directions.

Remember these

  1. Always find the principal stresses first, using sigma1,2 = average plus or minus the radius.
  2. Ductile metals: use Tresca (strictest) or von Mises (most common).
  3. Brittle materials: use Rankine, and check compression with the compressive strength.
  4. Tresca: n = Sy/(sigma1 − sigma3). Von Mises: n = Sy/sigma'.
  5. The Tresca hexagon lies inside the von Mises ellipse, so Tresca is never less conservative.
  6. Maximum strain uses sigma1 − nu sigma2, so always substitute the sign of sigma2.

Friction: rolling friction, belt-pulley

Friction is the force that resists sliding and rolling. It decides whether a block stays on a slope, how much force a wheel needs, and how tight a belt must be to drive a pulley without slipping. GATE asks for these ideas in numerical form.

  • Rolling friction
  • Belt and pulley friction

The idea in plain words

Push a heavy book across a table and you feel a resistance. That resistance is friction. It acts along the surface, and it always opposes the way the object moves or tries to move. Friction is not a nuisance only. Without it you could not walk, a car could not turn, and a belt could not drive a machine.

Everyday analogy. Think of a child's sledge on snow. Pulling gently, the sledge does not move. Pull harder and it suddenly jumps forward. Then it slides more easily than it started. The friction at the start was higher than the friction during sliding. This is exactly the difference between static and kinetic friction.

Why it matters. Engineers use friction on purpose in brakes, clutches, belt drives and grippers. They avoid it in bearings, where it wastes power. Knowing the size of friction lets you size the motor and the belt.

Where it appears in GATE. In the Robotics and Automation (RA) paper, expect numerical questions on the force needed to slide a block, the angle at which a block starts to slide, rolling resistance, and belt tensions. Conceptual questions ask you to compare static, kinetic and rolling friction.

Key terms

Friction force
A force that acts along a contact surface and opposes sliding or the tendency to slide.
Normal force (N)
The force a surface pushes on an object, perpendicular to the surface. It is measured in newtons.
Coefficient of friction (μ)
A number, with no unit, that compares friction with the normal force. Precisely: friction force = μ × N, for sliding.
Static friction
Friction on an object that is not moving. It adjusts itself, up to a maximum, to stop sliding.
Coefficient of static friction (μs)
The number that sets the largest static friction. Precisely: Fs,max = μs N.
Kinetic (sliding) friction
Friction on an object that is sliding. Precisely: Fk = μk N.
Coefficient of kinetic friction (μk)
The number that sets the sliding friction. It is usually smaller than μs.
Impending motion
The state just before an object starts to slide, when static friction has reached its maximum.
Weight (W)
The force of gravity on a mass, W = mg, with g = 9.81 m/s² on Earth.
Rolling friction (rolling resistance)
The resistance that opposes a wheel or ball rolling on a surface. It is usually much smaller than sliding friction.
Coefficient of rolling friction (δ)
A length, in metres, in many textbooks. It gives the moment that opposes rolling: M = N × δ.
Belt tension
The pull in a belt. The side that pulls the load is the tight side, and the other side is the slack side.
Angle of wrap (θ)
The angle, in radians, through which the belt is in contact with the pulley.
Slip
Sliding between a belt and a pulley when the friction is not enough to carry the load.
Power (P)
The rate of doing work. For a belt drive, P = (T1 - T2) × v.

Coulomb friction: static and kinetic

Step 1: friction depends on the normal force

Put a heavy box on a floor and push it. Friction grows as you push harder, up to a limit. The bigger the box's weight, the bigger the normal force, and the bigger the friction. This is Coulomb's model of friction, and it has three main points:

  • Friction is proportional to the normal force.
  • Friction does not depend much on the size of the contact area.
  • Static friction can be anything from zero up to a maximum, while sliding friction has a fixed value.

Step 2: the two formulas

For a block at rest, static friction Fs can have any value up to its maximum:

Fs ≤ μs N

Once the block slides, friction becomes the sliding value:

Fk = μk N

Here N is the normal force in newtons, and the coefficients are plain numbers. Usually μk is smaller than μs, which is why an object that is hard to start moving can slide easily afterwards.

Worked example: pushing a block

A 20 kg block sits on a flat floor, with μs = 0.4 and μk = 0.3. Take g = 9.81 m/s². Find the maximum static friction, and the friction when it slides.

  1. Weight: W = 20 × 9.81 = 196.2 N.
  2. On a flat floor the normal force equals the weight: N = 196.2 N.
  3. Maximum static friction: 0.4 × 196.2 = 78.48 N.
  4. Sliding friction: 0.3 × 196.2 = 58.86 N.
  5. So you must push with more than 78.48 N to start the block. Once it moves, a push of about 58.86 N keeps it moving at steady speed.
m = 20.0          # mass in kg
g = 9.81          # gravity in m/s^2
mu_s = 0.4
mu_k = 0.3

N = m * g                 # normal force on a flat floor, in newtons
F_s_max = mu_s * N
F_k = mu_k * N
print("Normal force = %.1f N" % N)
print("Maximum static friction = %.2f N" % F_s_max)
print("Sliding friction = %.2f N" % F_k)
Normal force = 196.2 N
Maximum static friction = 78.48 N
Sliding friction = 58.86 N

TrapDo not write friction = μN when the object is at rest. At rest, friction is whatever it needs to be, up to μsN. Only when sliding has started do you use μkN.

Question: a 5 kg block on a flat floor has μk = 0.3. What is its sliding friction? Use g = 9.81 m/s².

N = 5 × 9.81 = 49.05 N. Fk = 0.3 × 49.05 = 14.7 N.

Question: why does a box often need a bigger push to start than to keep moving?

Static friction can reach μsN, which is larger than the sliding value μkN when μk is smaller than μs.

Blocks on slopes

Resolving the weight

Place a block on a slope at angle θ. Its weight W = mg pulls straight down. Split the weight into two parts. One part is along the slope, trying to slide the block down. The other part is perpendicular to the slope, pressing it into the surface.

W along slope = mg sin θ and N = mg cos θ

Here θ is the slope angle, N is the normal force, and friction must hold back the component mg sin θ.

                   N (perpendicular to slope)
                 ^
                 |   /
                 |  /  block
                 | /
  ---------------+/---------------- slope at angle theta
                  /\
                 /  \
                    W = mg (straight down)

Does the block slide?

The block stays at rest if the needed friction mg sin θ is no more than the maximum static friction μs mg cos θ. Cancel mg, and the test becomes:

stays at rest if tan θ ≤ μs

The angle at which sliding just starts is called the angle of repose, and it satisfies tan θ = μs.

Worked example: a block on a 30 degree slope

A 10 kg block is on a slope at 30 degrees, with μs = 0.6. Does it stay at rest?

  1. Component along the slope: mg sin 30° = 98.1 × 0.5 = 49.05 N.
  2. Normal force: N = mg cos 30° = 98.1 × 0.8660 = 84.96 N.
  3. Maximum static friction: 0.6 × 84.96 = 50.97 N.
  4. Compare: 49.05 N is less than 50.97 N, so the block stays at rest.
  5. Check with tan: tan 30° = 0.577, which is less than 0.6. The same answer.
import math

m = 10.0
g = 9.81
theta = math.radians(30.0)
mu_s = 0.6

W = m * g
along = W * math.sin(theta)
N = W * math.cos(theta)
F_max = mu_s * N
print("Along slope = %.2f N" % along)
print("Max static friction = %.2f N" % F_max)
print("Stays at rest: %s" % (along <= F_max))
print("tan(30) = %.3f" % math.tan(theta))
Along slope = 49.05 N
Max static friction = 50.97 N
Stays at rest: True
tan(30) = 0.577

GATE loves thisThe angle of repose depends only on μs, not on the mass. If a question gives the angle and μs, compare tan θ with μs and skip the mass entirely.

Question: a block on a slope starts to slide at 26.57 degrees. What is μs approximately?

μs = tan 26.57° = 0.5, approximately.

Question: does the answer depend on the mass of the block?

No. Mass cancels when you compare mg sin θ with μs mg cos θ.

Rolling friction

Why rolling is easier than sliding

A wheel rolling on a road touches it at a small patch, and the surfaces deform a little under the load. The contact patch is always pushed forward and backward slightly, so energy is lost as heat in the tyre and the road. This loss is rolling resistance. It is far smaller than sliding friction for the same load, which is why wheels were invented.

The rolling resistance formula

In many textbooks, the coefficient of rolling friction δ is a length. It gives a resisting moment that acts on the wheel:

Mr = N × δ

Here Mr is the resisting moment in N·m, N is the normal force in newtons, and δ is the coefficient of rolling friction in metres. To find the equivalent horizontal force at the rim of a wheel of radius R, divide the moment by the radius:

Fr = N × δ / R

Worked example: wheel versus sliding block

A wheel of radius 0.3 m carries a normal force of 500 N. Its coefficient of rolling friction is δ = 0.002 m. A block with the same load slides with μ = 0.02. Compare the two resistances.

  1. Rolling moment: Mr = 500 × 0.002 = 1.00 N·m.
  2. Equivalent rim force: Fr = 1.00 / 0.3 = 3.33 N.
  3. Sliding friction: F = 0.02 × 500 = 10.00 N.
  4. The rolling force is about one third of the sliding force, and the difference is large.
N = 500.0          # normal force in newtons
R = 0.3            # wheel radius in metres
delta = 0.002      # coefficient of rolling friction in metres
mu_slide = 0.02

M_roll = N * delta
F_roll = M_roll / R
F_slide = mu_slide * N
print("Rolling resistance moment = %.2f N-m" % M_roll)
print("Rolling resistance force = %.2f N" % F_roll)
print("Sliding friction force = %.2f N" % F_slide)
Rolling resistance moment = 1.00 N-m
Rolling resistance force = 3.33 N
Sliding friction force = 10.00 N

TrapThe coefficient of rolling friction δ is not the same kind of number as μ. Its unit is metres, so you cannot compare δ = 0.002 with μ = 0.02 directly. Convert to a force first.

Question: a wheel of radius 0.5 m has δ = 0.001 m and N = 1000 N. What is the rolling resistance force?

Mr = 1000 × 0.001 = 1 N·m. Fr = 1 / 0.5 = 2 N.

Question: why does a bigger wheel roll more easily for the same δ?

The rolling force is Fr = N δ / R, so a larger radius R gives a smaller force.

Belt and pulley friction

How a belt grips a pulley

A belt drive transfers power from one pulley to another. The belt grips the pulley by friction. One side of the belt, the tight side, carries high tension T1 and pulls the load. The other side, the slack side, carries lower tension T2. The difference T1 - T2 is what turns the pulley.

        tight side T1 (pulls load)
   ______________________________
  /                              \
 |   driver pulley       wrap     |
  \______________________________/
        slack side T2

The belt equation

When the belt is on the point of slipping, the ratio of the two tensions depends on the friction and the wrap angle. This is the Euler-Eytelwein equation:

T1 / T2 = eμθ

Here μ is the coefficient of friction between belt and pulley, θ is the angle of wrap in radians, and e is about 2.718. The wrap angle must be in radians, so 180 degrees becomes π radians.

Worked example: tensions from the belt equation

A belt has μ = 0.3 and wraps 180 degrees around a pulley. The slack side tension is T2 = 100 N. The belt speed is 10 m/s. Find T1 and the power transmitted.

  1. Wrap angle: θ = 180° = π radians.
  2. Exponent: μθ = 0.3 × π = 0.9425.
  3. Ratio: T1 / T2 = e0.9425 = 2.566.
  4. Tight side: T1 = 2.566 × 100 = 256.6 N.
  5. Power: P = (T1 - T2) × v = (256.6 - 100) × 10 = 1566 W.
import math

mu = 0.3
theta = math.pi          # 180 degrees in radians
T2 = 100.0               # slack side tension in N
v = 10.0                 # belt speed in m/s

ratio = math.exp(mu * theta)
T1 = T2 * ratio
P = (T1 - T2) * v
print("T1/T2 = %.3f" % ratio)
print("T1 = %.1f N" % T1)
print("Power = %.0f W" % P)
T1/T2 = 2.566
T1 = 256.6 N
Power = 1566 W

V-belts

A V-belt sits in a groove, and the sides of the groove wedge the belt in. The wedge increases the normal force for the same tension, so the friction is larger. The effective coefficient becomes μ / sin β, where β is the half-angle of the groove. The same equation then holds with μ replaced by this larger value. V-belts therefore transmit more power than flat belts for the same tension.

Key ideaFriction in a belt drive grows exponentially with the wrap angle. A little more wrap gives a lot more grip, which is why drives use idler pulleys to increase the wrap.

Question: a flat belt has μ = 0.25 and wraps 90 degrees. What is T1 / T2?

θ = π/2 = 1.5708 rad. μθ = 0.25 × 1.5708 = 0.3927. e0.3927 = 1.481.

Question: why is the wrap angle in the belt equation measured in radians?

The equation comes from calculus on the belt, which works in radians. Using degrees gives a number that is far too large.

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original problems in the GATE style. They are not real past paper questions.

Sliding friction on a floor

A 5 kg block slides across a flat floor with μk = 0.3. Take g = 9.81 m/s². Find the friction force in newtons.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The sliding friction force in N.
  2. Which idea applies. N = mg on a flat floor, and Fk = μk N.
  3. Work it out. N = 5 × 9.81 = 49.05 N. Fk = 0.3 × 49.05 = 14.715 N, which is 14.7 N to one decimal place.
  4. Check. The friction is about 30 percent of the weight, which matches μk = 0.3. Good.
m = 5.0
g = 9.81
mu_k = 0.3
F_k = mu_k * m * g
print("Friction = %.1f N" % F_k)
Friction = 14.7 N

Answer: 14.7 N.

Will a pull start the block?

A 20 kg block rests on a flat floor with μs = 0.4. Take g = 9.81 m/s². A horizontal pull of 60 N is applied. Does the block move?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Whether the block starts to move.
  2. Which idea applies. Compare the pull with the maximum static friction, μs N.
  3. Work it out. N = 20 × 9.81 = 196.2 N. Maximum static friction = 0.4 × 196.2 = 78.48 N. The pull of 60 N is less than 78.48 N.
  4. Check. Friction adjusts to match the pull, so the friction is 60 N and the block stays at rest. Good.
m = 20.0
g = 9.81
mu_s = 0.4
pull = 60.0
F_max = mu_s * m * g
print("Maximum static friction = %.2f N" % F_max)
print("Block moves: %s" % (pull > F_max))
Maximum static friction = 78.48 N
Block moves: False

Answer: No. The block stays at rest, because 60 N is less than 78.48 N.

Which statements about friction are true?

Consider the following statements about friction. Select all that apply.

(A) Static friction can be smaller than μs N.
(B) Kinetic friction is usually lower than the maximum static friction for the same pair of surfaces.
(C) The coefficient of rolling friction is always larger than the sliding coefficient.
(D) In a belt drive, increasing the wrap angle increases the ratio T1 / T2.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. Static friction is at most μs N, kinetic friction is μk N, rolling resistance is small, and the belt equation.
  3. Work it out. (A) True, static friction adjusts to the force applied, so it can be anything from zero to μs N. (B) True, usually μk < μs. (C) False, rolling resistance is usually much smaller. (D) True, the exponent μθ grows with θ.
  4. Check. Statement (C) contradicts the big difference between rolling and sliding, so it is false.

Answer: (A), (B) and (D). Statement (C) is false.

Angle at which a block starts to slide

A block sits on a rough plane with μs = 0.5. The plane is slowly tilted. Find the angle, in degrees to two decimal places, at which the block begins to slide.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The sliding angle in degrees.
  2. Which idea applies. Sliding starts when tan θ = μs.
  3. Work it out. θ = arctan(0.5). Using a calculator, arctan(0.5) = 26.565 degrees, which is 26.57 degrees.
  4. Check. tan 26.57° is about 0.5, so the answer reproduces μs. Good.
import math

mu_s = 0.5
theta = math.degrees(math.atan(mu_s))
print("Sliding angle = %.2f degrees" % theta)
Sliding angle = 26.57 degrees

Answer: 26.57 degrees.

Belt tension on a 90 degree wrap

A flat belt wraps 90 degrees around a pulley with μ = 0.25. The slack side tension is 200 N. Find the tight side tension T1 in newtons, correct to one decimal place.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. T1 in N.
  2. Which idea applies. T1 / T2 = eμθ, with θ in radians.
  3. Work it out. θ = π / 2 = 1.5708 rad. μθ = 0.25 × 1.5708 = 0.3927. The ratio is e0.3927 = 1.481. T1 = 1.481 × 200 = 296.2 N.
  4. Check. A 90 degree wrap gives a smaller ratio than the 180 degree wrap of the worked example (2.566), so 1.481 is reasonable. Good.
import math

mu = 0.25
theta = math.pi / 2
T2 = 200.0
T1 = T2 * math.exp(mu * theta)
print("T1 = %.1f N" % T1)
T1 = 296.2 N

Answer: 296.2 N.

Tension ratio of a V-belt

A V-belt has a coefficient of friction μ = 0.3 and a groove half-angle β = 20 degrees. It wraps 180 degrees around the pulley. The slack side tension is 100 N. Using the effective coefficient μ / sin β, find the tight side tension T1 in newtons, correct to the nearest newton.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. T1 in N, to the nearest newton.
  2. Which idea applies. Use the belt equation with μ replaced by μ / sin β.
  3. Work it out. sin 20° = 0.34202. μ' = 0.3 / 0.34202 = 0.87714. θ = π rad. μ'θ = 0.87714 × 3.14159 = 2.7555. The ratio is e2.7555 = 15.73. T1 = 15.73 × 100 = 1573 N.
  4. Check. A flat belt with μ = 0.3 gives only 2.566 for the same wrap (see the worked example). The V-belt gives a much larger ratio, as expected from the wedge effect.
import math

mu = 0.3
beta = math.radians(20.0)
theta = math.pi
T2 = 100.0

mu_eff = mu / math.sin(beta)
T1 = T2 * math.exp(mu_eff * theta)
print("Effective mu = %.4f" % mu_eff)
print("T1 = %.0f N" % T1)
Effective mu = 0.8771
T1 = 1573 N

Answer: about 1573 N.

Choose the correct statements about slopes and rolling

Consider the following statements. Select all that apply.

(A) For the same normal force, rolling resistance is generally smaller than sliding friction.
(B) A block on a 40 degree slope with μs = 0.8 begins to slide.
(C) A block on a 30 degree slope with μs = 0.5 stays at rest.
(D) Increasing the wrap angle of a belt increases the grip.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. Rolling versus sliding, the slope test tan θ ≤ μs, and the belt equation.
  3. Work it out. (A) True, rolling resistance is usually much smaller. (B) True, tan 40° = 0.839, which is more than 0.8, so sliding starts. (C) False, tan 30° = 0.577, which is more than 0.5, so the block slides. (D) True, the exponent μθ grows with θ.
  4. Check. For (B) and (C), compare tan θ with μs directly. Both checks agree with the answers.

Answer: (A), (B) and (D). Statement (C) is false.

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Belt tension ratio (NAT, 2 marks)

A flat belt has μ = 0.3 and wraps 180° round a pulley. The slack side tension is 200 N. Find the maximum tight side tension, correct to 1 decimal place.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. T1 from the capstan equation.
  2. Which idea applies. T1/T2 = eμθ.
  3. Work it out. e0.3π = 2.566. T1 = 513.3 N.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
import math
print("%.1f N" % (200 * math.exp(0.3 * math.pi)))

513.3 N

Answer: about 513.3 N.

Exam-level 2: Block on an incline (NAT, 2 marks)

A 50 kg block rests on a 30° incline with μs = 0.4. Take g = 9.81. Find the smallest force parallel to the incline that starts it moving up.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The force to overcome gravity and friction.
  2. Which idea applies. P = mg(sin θ + μ cos θ).
  3. Work it out. 490.5(0.5 + 0.3464) = 415.2 N.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
import math
print("%.1f N" % (50 * 9.81 * (math.sin(math.pi/6) + 0.4 * math.cos(math.pi/6))))

415.2 N

Answer: about 415.2 N.

Exam-level 3: Rolling resistance (NAT, 2 marks)

A wheel of radius 0.4 m carries 2000 N. The coefficient of rolling resistance is 0.8 mm. Find the horizontal force to keep it rolling at constant speed.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. F = Wa/r.
  2. Which idea applies. The ground reaction acts a distance a ahead of the centre.
  3. Work it out. 2000 · 0.0008/0.4 = 4 N.
  4. Check. Rolling resistance is far smaller than sliding friction.

Answer: 4 N.

Remember these

  1. Static friction is at most μs N, and it adjusts to the load.
  2. Sliding friction is μk N, and μk is usually smaller than μs.

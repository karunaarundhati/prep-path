Confidence intervals

A confidence interval turns one sample estimate into a range of plausible values for an unknown population quantity. It is one of the most used tools in data analysis, and GATE DA asks you to build intervals for means and proportions and to explain what the confidence level really means.

The idea in plain words

Suppose you want to know the average monthly spending of all students in a university. You cannot ask everyone, so you ask 25 students and find their average is 50 units. That single number is a point estimate. It is your best single guess, but you know it will not be exactly right. Another sample of 25 would give a different average.

A confidence interval gives a range instead of one number, such as "between 46 and 54". The range is built so that, if you repeated the whole sampling process many times, most of the intervals would contain the true average. The phrase "95 per cent confidence" describes that long-run behaviour of the method, not the chance that one particular interval is right.

The interval has three parts: the estimate, the margin of error, and the confidence level. Every formula on this page is built from these three parts. Confidence intervals appear in GATE DA whenever a question asks for a range of plausible values or for a sample size needed to reach a precision.

Key terms

Each entry gives the plain meaning first and the precise definition second.

Population parameter
A fixed, unknown number describing the population, such as the mean mu. Parameters do not change.
Point estimate
A single number computed from the sample to estimate a parameter, such as x-bar for mu.
Interval estimate
A range of values, computed from the sample, that is likely to contain the parameter.
Standard error (SE)
The standard deviation of the estimate. For a mean, SE = s / square root of n.
Margin of error
Half the width of the interval. Precise definition: critical value times SE.
Critical value
The number of standard errors used for the chosen confidence level, such as z = 1.96 for 95 per cent.
t-distribution
A bell-shaped distribution with heavier tails than the normal. Used when sigma is unknown and n is small.
Degrees of freedom
For one sample mean, n - 1. It controls the shape of the t-distribution.
Sample proportion
The fraction of successes in a sample, written p-hat.
Confidence level
The long-run percentage of intervals that contain the true parameter, such as 95 per cent.

Point estimates and interval estimates

Why one number is not enough

A point estimate is simple, but it hides how uncertain we are. Two studies can both report an average of 50, yet one used 25 people and the other 2500. The second estimate deserves more trust. An interval shows that difference directly: a narrow interval means a precise estimate, and a wide one means the data leave a lot of doubt.

The general form

Most intervals in this topic have the same shape:

estimate plus or minus (critical value times standard error)

Symbols: the estimate is the point estimate, the critical value comes from a table or distribution and depends on the confidence level, and the standard error measures the spread of the estimate. The part after the plus or minus sign is the margin of error.

Key ideaEvery confidence interval is the estimate, plus or minus a margin of error. The margin grows with the confidence level and shrinks with a bigger sample.

TrapThe population parameter is fixed. It is the interval that is random, because it depends on the sample. Do not say that the true mean has a 95 per cent chance of lying in one particular interval.

Self-check: is the interval or the parameter random?

The interval is random, because a different sample gives a different interval. The parameter is a fixed, unknown number.

Self-check: if the estimate is 50 and the margin of error is 4, what is the interval?

From 50 - 4 = 46 to 50 + 4 = 54, written (46, 54).

Confidence interval for a mean: known and unknown variance

Case 1: sigma known

If the population standard deviation sigma is known, the sample mean follows a normal distribution, approximately, by the Central Limit Theorem. The 95 per cent interval is:

x-bar plus or minus 1.96 times sigma / square root of n

Symbols: x-bar is the sample mean, sigma is the known population SD, n is the sample size, and 1.96 is the z critical value that leaves 2.5 per cent in each tail.

Example: x-bar = 50, sigma = 10, n = 25. Then SE = 10 / 5 = 2, and the margin is 1.96 times 2 = 3.92. The interval is 50 - 3.92 = 46.08 to 50 + 3.92 = 53.92.

Case 2: sigma unknown

In practice sigma is almost never known. We replace it with the sample standard deviation s. This adds extra uncertainty, because s itself varies from sample to sample. The correct distribution is the t-distribution with n - 1 degrees of freedom. The t-distribution has heavier tails than the normal, so its critical values are a little larger, especially for small samples.

x-bar plus or minus t times s / square root of n, with df = n - 1

For n = 25, df = 24, and the 95 per cent two-sided t critical value is 2.064 (read from a t-table). Suppose s = 10. Then SE = 10 / 5 = 2, the margin is 2.064 times 2 = 4.128, and the interval is 45.872 to 54.128.

Notice that the t interval is a little wider than the z interval, 2.064 against 1.96. The extra width pays for not knowing sigma.

Choosing between z and t

  • If sigma is known, use z.
  • If sigma is unknown and the sample is small, use t with n - 1 degrees of freedom.
  • If sigma is unknown and n is large, z and t give nearly the same answer, so either is acceptable in practice.

TrapUsing z when sigma is unknown and n is small makes the interval too narrow. The interval will then cover the true mean less often than 95 per cent of the time.

TipRead the degrees of freedom from n, not from the number of groups. For one sample mean, df = n - 1.

Self-check: n = 16, x-bar = 20 and sigma = 4 is known. Find the 95 per cent interval.

SE = 4 / 4 = 1. Margin = 1.96 times 1 = 1.96. Interval is 18.04 to 21.96.

Self-check: why is the t critical value larger than 1.96 for small samples?

Because the sample SD s is itself uncertain, and the t-distribution has heavier tails to reflect that. As n grows, the t critical value approaches 1.96.

Confidence interval for a proportion

The setup

Sometimes the variable is yes or no, such as whether a student passed. The estimate is the sample proportion p-hat, the number of successes divided by n. Its standard error is:

SE = square root of (p-hat times (1 - p-hat) / n)

The 95 per cent interval is p-hat plus or minus 1.96 times that SE, provided n is large enough that both n times p-hat and n times (1 - p-hat) are at least about 10.

Worked example

In a sample of n = 400, 120 people say yes. Then p-hat = 120 / 400 = 0.3.

Step 1: SE = square root of (0.3 times 0.7 / 400) = square root of (0.21 / 400) = square root of 0.000525 = 0.02291.

Step 2: margin = 1.96 times 0.02291 = 0.04491.

Step 3: interval = 0.3 - 0.0449 = 0.2551 to 0.3 + 0.0449 = 0.3449.

So we estimate the true proportion to lie between about 25.5 and 34.5 per cent.

Key ideaThe standard error for a proportion uses p-hat itself. When p-hat is near 0.5, the SE is largest, so the interval is widest.

TrapDo not use the sample SD of the yes or no values as the SE. Use the formula with p-hat times (1 - p-hat) shown above.

Self-check: p-hat = 0.5 and n = 100. What is the SE?

SE = square root of (0.5 times 0.5 / 100) = square root of 0.0025 = 0.05.

Self-check: which p-hat gives the widest interval for a fixed n?

p-hat = 0.5, because p-hat times (1 - p-hat) is largest there.

Confidence level, margin of error and sample size

What the confidence level means

A 95 per cent confidence level means that the method, used on many samples, produces intervals that contain the true value in about 95 out of 100 cases. Higher confidence needs a bigger critical value, so the interval is wider. A 99 per cent interval uses z = 2.576, which is wider than 1.96.

How sample size shrinks the interval

The margin of error is proportional to 1 divided by the square root of n. Using sigma = 10 and z = 1.96:

n = 25: width = 2 times 1.96 times 10 / 5 = 7.84
n = 100: width = 2 times 1.96 times 10 / 10 = 3.92

Quadrupling n halves the width. To halve the width again, you need four times as many observations.

Sample size for a target margin

If you want a margin of error E at 95 per cent confidence, with known sigma, solve E = 1.96 times sigma / square root of n:

n = (1.96 times sigma / E) squared

Round up to a whole number, because you cannot sample a fraction of a person. For sigma = 10 and E = 2, n = (1.96 times 10 / 2) squared = (9.8) squared = 96.04, so take n = 97.

GATE loves thisQuestions often ask for the sample size needed for a given margin. Always round up, never down, because rounding down makes the margin too large.

Self-check: halving the margin of error requires how much more data?

Four times as many observations, because the margin depends on the square root of n.

Self-check: does a 99 per cent interval have a larger or smaller margin than a 95 per cent interval for the same data?

Larger, because the critical value 2.576 is bigger than 1.96.

A Python check

This program computes the three kinds of intervals from this page, and shows how the width of the mean interval changes with n.

import math

xbar, sigma, n = 50, 10, 25
se = sigma / math.sqrt(n)
z = 1.96
m = z * se
print("SE =", se)
print("known sigma 95% CI =", round(xbar - m, 2), "to", round(xbar + m, 2))

s = 10
t = 2.064
m2 = t * s / math.sqrt(n)
print("unknown sigma 95% CI =", round(xbar - m2, 3), "to", round(xbar + m2, 3))

p = 120 / 400
se_p = math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / 400)
mp = 1.96 * se_p
print("proportion p-hat =", p)
print("proportion 95% CI =", round(p - mp, 4), "to", round(p + mp, 4))

for nn in (25, 100):
    print("n =", nn, "width =", round(2 * 1.96 * sigma / math.sqrt(nn), 3))

SE = 2.0
known sigma 95% CI = 46.08 to 53.92
unknown sigma 95% CI = 45.872 to 54.128
proportion p-hat = 0.3
proportion 95% CI = 0.2551 to 0.3449
n = 25 width = 7.84
n = 100 width = 3.92

The first lines confirm the worked examples, and the last two lines confirm that quadrupling n halves the width.

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original, GATE-style exercises written for practice. They are not past exam questions. Try each one on paper before reading the answer.

Margin of error

sigma = 8 is known and n = 64. Find the margin of error for a 95 per cent interval.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The half-width of the interval.
  2. Which idea applies. Margin = z times sigma / square root of n.
  3. Work it out. SE = 8 / 8 = 1. Margin = 1.96 times 1 = 1.96.
  4. Check. The margin is close to two standard errors, as expected for 95 per cent.

Answer: 1.96

What does 95 per cent confidence mean?

Which statement correctly describes a 95 per cent confidence interval?

  • (A) There is a 95 per cent chance that the true mean lies in this interval.
  • (B) If the procedure is repeated many times, about 95 per cent of the intervals contain the true mean.
  • (C) 95 per cent of the data lie in the interval.
  • (D) The sample mean is 95 per cent accurate.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The correct meaning of the confidence level.
  2. Which idea applies. The confidence level describes the long-run behaviour of the method.
  3. Work it out. (A) is the common mistake, since the parameter is fixed. (B) is the correct long-run statement. (C) confuses the interval with the data. (D) is meaningless.
  4. Check. Only (B) describes repeated sampling.

Answer: (B)

Interval with unknown sigma

A sample of n = 16 has mean 30 and sample standard deviation 8. The 95 per cent t critical value for 15 degrees of freedom is 2.131. Find the lower limit of the 95 per cent confidence interval for the mean.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The lower end of a t interval.
  2. Which idea applies. x-bar minus t times s / square root of n.
  3. Work it out. SE = 8 / 4 = 2. Margin = 2.131 times 2 = 4.262. Lower limit = 30 - 4.262 = 25.738.
  4. Check. The limit is below the mean of 30, as a lower limit should be.

Answer: 25.738

Sample size for a proportion

A survey wants the 95 per cent margin of error for a proportion to be at most 0.04. Use p-hat = 0.5 for planning. What is the smallest sample size? (Give a whole number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The smallest n that keeps the margin at most 0.04.
  2. Which idea applies. Margin = 1.96 times square root of (0.5 times 0.5 / n) at most 0.04.
  3. Work it out. Square root of (0.25 / n) at most 0.04 / 1.96 = 0.020408. So 0.25 / n at most 0.00041649, so n at least 600.25. Round up to 601.
  4. Check. With n = 601, margin = 1.96 times square root of (0.25 / 601) = 1.96 times 0.020400 = 0.03998, which is at most 0.04.

Answer: 601

Which statements are true?

Select all that apply.

  • (A) For a fixed sample, a 99 per cent interval is wider than a 95 per cent interval.
  • (B) Quadrupling n halves the width of a z-interval.
  • (C) When sigma is unknown and n is small, the z critical value gives a more reliable interval than the t value.
  • (D) The standard error of a proportion is largest when p-hat is 0.5.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Judge each statement.
  2. Which idea applies. Critical values, the square root of n, and the t-distribution.
  3. Work it out. (A) True: 2.576 is larger than 1.96. (B) True: width is proportional to 1 over the square root of n. (C) False: t is the appropriate, wider choice. (D) True: p-hat times (1 - p-hat) peaks at 0.5.
  4. Check. Statement (C) reverses the correct advice.

Answer: (A), (B) and (D). Select all that apply.

Two-sided interval with unknown proportion

In a random sample of n = 900, 360 people prefer product X. Find the 95 per cent confidence interval for the true proportion. Give the lower limit to 4 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The lower limit of a proportion interval.
  2. Which idea applies. p-hat plus or minus 1.96 times square root of (p-hat (1 - p-hat) / n).
  3. Work it out. p-hat = 360 / 900 = 0.4. Then 0.4 times 0.6 = 0.24, divided by 900 gives 0.000266667, and its square root is 0.016330. Margin = 1.96 times 0.016330 = 0.032007. Lower limit = 0.4 - 0.032007 = 0.3680.
  4. Check. The interval (0.3680, 0.4320) contains p-hat, as it should.

Answer: 0.3680

Changing the sample size and confidence level

Sigma is known to be 12. Compare two intervals for the mean: one with n = 36 at 95 per cent, and one with n = 144 at 99 per cent. Find the ratio of the width of the second interval to the width of the first. Give the answer to 3 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A ratio of two interval widths.
  2. Which idea applies. Width = 2 times z times sigma / square root of n.
  3. Work it out. First width = 2 times 1.96 times 12 / 6 = 7.84. Second width = 2 times 2.576 times 12 / 12 = 5.152. Ratio = 5.152 / 7.84 = 0.657.
  4. Check. The second interval is narrower, since quadrupling n halves the width and the higher confidence widens it by 2.576 / 1.96 = 1.314, giving about 0.657.

Answer: 0.657

Exam-level 1: Width of a t interval (NAT, 2 marks)

A random sample of 16 bottles of a drink has mean volume 52 ml and sample standard deviation 8 ml. Assume the volumes are roughly normal. The t critical value for 95 percent confidence with 15 degrees of freedom is 2.131. Find the width of the 95 percent confidence interval for the population mean volume, to 3 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The width of an interval, which is twice the margin of error.
  2. Which idea applies. The t-interval: sample mean plus or minus t times s / square root of n.
  3. Work it out. The standard error is 8 / 4 = 2. The margin of error is 2.131 times 2 = 4.262. The interval is 52 - 4.262 to 52 + 4.262, which is (47.738, 56.262). Its width is 2 times 4.262 = 8.524.
  4. Check. The width should be a little more than 2 times 1.96 times 2 = 7.84, the z-based width, because the t critical value is bigger for a small sample. 8.524 is indeed larger.

Answer: 8.524

import math
n, xbar, s, t = 16, 52, 8, 2.131
se = s / math.sqrt(n)
moe = t * se
print(f"standard error = {se}")
print(f"margin of error = {round(moe, 3)}")
print(f"CI = {round(xbar - moe, 3)} to {round(xbar + moe, 3)}")
print(f"width = {round(2 * moe, 3)}")
standard error = 2.0
margin of error = 4.262
CI = 47.738 to 56.262
width = 8.524

Exam-level 2: Upper limit for a proportion (NAT, 2 marks)

A poll asks 400 randomly chosen voters whether they support a proposal, and 116 say yes. Use the large-sample (Wald) method for a proportion with a 95 percent confidence level, using the critical value 1.96. The estimated proportion is p-hat = 116 / 400, and the standard error uses p-hat times (1 - p-hat) divided by n. Find the upper limit of the confidence interval for the true proportion of supporters, to 4 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The upper end of the interval for a population proportion.
  2. Which idea applies. The interval for a proportion: p-hat plus or minus 1.96 times the standard error, where the standard error is square root of (p-hat (1 - p-hat) / n).
  3. Work it out. p-hat = 116 / 400 = 0.29. The standard error is square root of (0.29 times 0.71 / 400) = 0.02269. The margin of error is 1.96 times 0.02269 = 0.0445. The upper limit is 0.29 + 0.0445 = 0.3345.
  4. Check. The lower limit is 0.2455. Both limits lie between 0 and 1 and are centred on the estimate 0.29, as they should be.

Answer: 0.3345

import math
n, yes, z = 400, 116, 1.96
p = yes / n
se = math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n)
moe = z * se
print(f"p-hat = {p}")
print(f"margin of error = {round(moe, 4)}")
print(f"lower limit = {round(p - moe, 4)}")
print(f"upper limit = {round(p + moe, 4)}")
p-hat = 0.29
margin of error = 0.0445
lower limit = 0.2455
upper limit = 0.3345

Exam-level 3: Which interval statements hold (MSQ, 2 marks)

Select all that apply. A researcher calculates confidence intervals from a random sample.

  • (A) A 95 percent confidence interval means there is a 95 percent probability that the true mean lies inside this particular interval.
  • (B) For the same data, a 99 percent interval is wider than a 95 percent interval.
  • (C) If the sample size is multiplied by 4 and the standard deviation stays the same, the width of a z-interval is halved.
  • (D) For a small sample, the t critical value is larger than the z critical value at the same confidence level.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements about confidence intervals are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. The meaning of confidence level, and how the width depends on the critical value and on n.
  3. Work it out. (A) False: the true mean is fixed, so the interval either contains it or does not. The 95 percent describes the long-run success rate of the method. (B) True: a higher confidence level needs a larger critical value. (C) True: the width is proportional to 1 / square root of n, and the square root of 4 is 2. (D) True: t critical values are larger than z values for small degrees of freedom.
  4. Check. The Python block shows the z-interval width halving when n goes from 25 to 100.

Answer: (B), (C) and (D). Select all that apply.

import math
s = 10
for n in (25, 100):
    print(f"n = {n}, width = {round(2 * 1.96 * s / math.sqrt(n), 3)}")
print(f"width ratio = {round((2 * 1.96 * s / math.sqrt(100)) / (2 * 1.96 * s / math.sqrt(25)), 3)}")
n = 25, width = 7.84
n = 100, width = 3.92
width ratio = 0.5

Exam-level 4: Sample size for a mean (NAT, 2 marks)

A school wants to estimate the mean test score of its students with a 95 percent confidence interval whose margin of error is at most 2 points. A pilot study gives a standard deviation of 9 points. Use the normal critical value 1.96. Find the smallest whole number n of students needed to achieve this margin of error. Remember that the margin of error depends on the standard error, which is sigma divided by the square root of n.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The smallest sample size n for a required margin of error.
  2. Which idea applies. Margin of error = 1.96 times sigma / square root of n. Solve for n, then round up to a whole number.
  3. Work it out. We need 1.96 times 9 / square root of n at most 2, so n is at least (1.96 times 9 / 2) squared = 8.82 squared = 77.79. The smallest whole number is n = 78.
  4. Check. With n = 78 the margin is 17.64 / square root of 78 = 1.997, which is just under 2.

Answer: n = 78

import math
z, sigma, E = 1.96, 9, 2
n_exact = (z * sigma / E) ** 2
n = math.ceil(n_exact)
print(f"exact n = {round(n_exact, 2)}")
print(f"smallest whole n = {n}")
print(f"margin with n = {n} is {round(z * sigma / math.sqrt(n), 3)}")
exact n = 77.79
smallest whole n = 78
margin with n = 78 is 1.997

Remember these

  1. An interval is the estimate plus or minus a margin of error.
  2. The interval moves from sample to sample. The parameter does not.
  3. Use t with n - 1 degrees of freedom when sigma is unknown.
  4. The proportion SE is largest when p-hat is 0.5.
  5. Higher confidence means a wider interval.
  6. Round sample sizes up.

Normal & Standard Normal distribution

The normal distribution is the bell-shaped curve that describes many measurements, such as heights, errors and test scores. Converting any normal variable to the standard normal Z lets you use one table for all of them, which is the core skill GATE tests.

The idea in plain words

Measure the heights of a thousand adults and plot how many fall into each height band. The plot usually looks like a bell. Most people are near the middle, and fewer and fewer people are very short or very tall. The curve is symmetric, so the left side mirrors the right side. This bell shape is the normal distribution.

Why is the bell so common? Many small, independent effects add together, and their sum tends to form a bell. Think of a measurement that is the sum of many tiny errors. Each error pushes the result a little up or down, and most of the time the pushes roughly cancel. Extreme results need many pushes in the same direction, so they are rare. This idea, called the central limit theorem, is one reason the normal distribution appears everywhere.

The normal distribution has two numbers that control it. The mean μ sets where the centre of the bell sits. The standard deviation σ sets how wide the bell is. A small σ gives a tall, narrow bell. A large σ gives a short, wide bell.

The big practical trick is standardisation. Any normal variable can be turned into the standard normal variable Z, which has mean 0 and standard deviation 1. Then one table of values works for every normal problem. This is why GATE questions often ask you to convert to Z before you look anything up.

Why does this matter for GATE? The Data Science and AI (DA) paper uses the normal distribution in probability, in statistical inference and in many machine learning models. The Robotics and Automation (RA) paper uses it for sensor noise, measurement error and tolerance analysis. Expect numerical questions that ask for a probability or for a value that gives a probability.

Key terms

Each term first gets its plain meaning, then its precise definition.

Normal distribution N(μ, σ²)
Plain meaning: the bell-shaped distribution of many natural measurements. Precise definition: a continuous distribution with mean μ and variance σ², whose density is symmetric about μ.
Mean μ
Plain meaning: the centre of the bell. Precise definition: the expectation E[X] of the normal variable.
Standard deviation σ
Plain meaning: the typical distance of a value from the centre. Precise definition: the positive square root of the variance, σ = √Var(X).
Variance σ²
Plain meaning: the squared spread. Precise definition: Var(X) = E[(X - μ)²].
Standard normal Z
Plain meaning: a normal variable with centre 0 and spread 1. Precise definition: Z ~ N(0, 1), with mean 0 and variance 1.
Z-score
Plain meaning: how many standard deviations a value is from the mean. Precise definition: z = (x - μ)/σ.
Standardising
Plain meaning: converting a value to a Z-score. Precise definition: the transformation Z = (X - μ)/σ, which turns N(μ, σ²) into N(0, 1).
Standard normal CDF Φ(z)
Plain meaning: the chance that Z is at most z. Precise definition: Φ(z) = P(Z ≤ z).
Symmetry
Plain meaning: the left and right sides are mirror images. Precise definition: f(μ - d) = f(μ + d) for every d, so P(X ≤ μ - d) = P(X ≥ μ + d).
Z-table
Plain meaning: a printed list of Φ(z) values. Precise definition: a table giving P(Z ≤ z) for z on a grid, used when a calculator is not available.

Concept 1: The normal distribution and its bell curve

The density

f(x) = (1 / (σ √(2π))) × e-(x - μ)² / (2σ²)

Read the formula in pieces:

  • (x - μ)² is the squared distance from the centre. It is zero at the centre and grows away from it.
  • e-(x - μ)² / (2σ²) is a bell. It is largest at x = μ, and it falls off on both sides.
  • 1 / (σ √(2π)) is a scaling factor. It makes the total area under the curve equal to 1.
  • π is the familiar constant 3.14159 and √(2π) is a fixed number, about 2.5066.

You will rarely integrate this formula by hand. What matters is the shape: a symmetric bell centred at μ, with width set by σ.

Key properties

  1. Symmetry. The density is the same at μ - d and μ + d. So the mean, median and mode are all equal to μ.
  2. Peak. The highest point is at x = μ.
  3. Total area. The area under the whole curve is 1.
  4. Infinite reach. The tails never touch the axis, but they become very small.

Worked example: the density at two points

Take the standard normal, μ = 0 and σ = 1. Find f(0) and f(1).

  1. At x = 0: (x - μ)² = 0, so the exponent is 0 and e0 = 1. The density is 1/√(2π), about 0.3989.
  2. At x = 1: (x - μ)² = 1, so the exponent is -1/2 and e-1/2 = 0.6065. The density is 0.6065 × 0.3989 = 0.2420.
  3. Check. The density is higher at the centre (0.3989) than one standard deviation away (0.2420), as the bell shape requires.
import math

def normal_pdf(x, mu=0.0, sigma=1.0):
    return math.exp(-(x - mu) ** 2 / (2 * sigma ** 2)) / (sigma * math.sqrt(2 * math.pi))

print(f"f(0) = {normal_pdf(0):.4f}")
print(f"f(1) = {normal_pdf(1):.4f}")
f(0) = 0.3989
f(1) = 0.2420

Key ideaFor a normal distribution, the mean, median and mode are equal. This means the centre of the bell is also the middle value and the most likely region.

TrapThe density value is not a probability. As with all continuous variables, probabilities are areas. The height at the peak is only a measure of how concentrated the distribution is.

Self-check: what is the mean of N(20, 9)?

The mean is 20. The second number in N(μ, σ²) is the variance, so σ = 3 here, but the mean is still μ = 20.

Self-check: for a symmetric bell, what is P(X ≤ μ)?

It is 0.5. Half the area lies to the left of the centre, by symmetry.

Concept 2: The empirical rule (68-95-99.7)

The idea

The normal distribution has a memorable structure. Measure how much of the area lies within one, two and three standard deviations of the mean:

  • About 68 percent lies within 1 standard deviation: μ - σ to μ + σ.
  • About 95 percent lies within 2 standard deviations: μ - 2σ to μ + 2σ.
  • About 99.7 percent lies within 3 standard deviations: μ - 3σ to μ + 3σ.

This is called the empirical rule, or the 68-95-99.7 rule. It gives quick estimates without a table. The numbers are approximate, and an exam question that asks for an exact value needs the table or a calculator.

Worked example: test scores

Scores on a test are normal with mean 60 and standard deviation 10. Estimate the chance that a score lies between 40 and 80.

  1. Find the distance in standard deviations. 40 is 20 below the mean, which is 20/10 = 2 standard deviations below. 80 is 20 above the mean, which is 2 standard deviations above.
  2. Apply the rule. The interval from 40 to 80 is μ - 2σ to μ + 2σ.
  3. Read off the answer. About 95 percent of scores lie in this range.
  4. Check. The exact value, from the table, is 0.9545, which matches the rule to four decimal places.

The Python block below checks the empirical rule exactly, using the error function to compute the standard normal CDF.

import math

def Phi(z):
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2)))

for k in (1, 2, 3):
    print(f"P(|Z| <= {k}) = {Phi(k) - Phi(-k):.4f}")
P(|Z| <= 1) = 0.6827
P(|Z| <= 2) = 0.9545
P(|Z| <= 3) = 0.9973

TipUse the rule to sanity-check any normal answer. If your computed chance for "within 2 standard deviations" is far from 95 percent, you have made a mistake.

TrapThe rule covers the interval around the mean, not the tail. The chance of being more than 2 standard deviations above the mean is only about 2.5 percent, not 5 percent, because the remaining 5 percent is split between two tails.

Self-check: about what percentage of values lie above μ + σ?

Half of the 32 percent outside μ ± σ lies above, so about 16 percent lies above μ + σ.

Self-check: for N(100, 15²), what interval holds about 95 percent of values?

From 100 - 2 × 15 = 70 to 100 + 2 × 15 = 130.

Concept 3: Standardising with the Z-score

Why we standardise

Suppose one exam has scores with mean 60 and standard deviation 10, and another has mean 500 and standard deviation 100. A score of 70 on the first and 600 on the second look different, but they are at the same relative height, one standard deviation above the mean. The Z-score puts every normal problem on one common scale.

The formula

Z = (X - μ) / σ

Each symbol means:

  • X is the value we are given, from a normal distribution with mean μ and standard deviation σ.
  • X - μ is the distance from the mean, with sign.
  • Dividing by σ measures that distance in standard deviations.

The result Z is standard normal: its mean is 0 and its variance is 1. So P(X ≤ x) = P(Z ≤ z), where z = (x - μ)/σ. Converting the question changes the variable, not the probability.

Worked example: converting a question

Let X be normal with mean 50 and standard deviation 4. Find P(X ≤ 56).

  1. Compute the Z-score. z = (56 - 50)/4 = 6/4 = 1.5.
  2. Write the probability in Z. P(X ≤ 56) = P(Z ≤ 1.5) = Φ(1.5).
  3. Look up the value. Φ(1.5) = 0.9332 from a standard normal table.
  4. Check. The value 56 is 1.5 standard deviations above the mean, so most of the area lies below it. A value near 0.93 is sensible.

Key ideaAlways standardise first. Once the question is in terms of Z, every normal problem uses the same table.

TrapDivide by the standard deviation σ, not the variance σ². Students who divide by the variance get a wrong Z-score, often by a factor of σ.

Self-check: X is normal with mean 80 and standard deviation 5. What is the Z-score of 90?

z = (90 - 80)/5 = 10/5 = 2.

Self-check: X ~ N(10, 16). What is σ?

The variance is 16, so σ = √16 = 4.

Concept 4: Reading the standard normal table

What the table gives

A standard normal table lists Φ(z) = P(Z ≤ z) for z values such as 0.00, 0.01, 0.02 and so on. The first column gives z to one decimal place, and the top row gives the second decimal. The number where the row and column meet is Φ(z).

For example, to find Φ(1.23), go to the row for 1.2 and the column for 0.03. The entry is 0.8907. Read carefully, because a small slip in the row or column gives a wrong value.

Three rules that save time

  1. Negative z. Use symmetry: Φ(-z) = 1 - Φ(z). For example, Φ(-1) = 1 - Φ(1) = 1 - 0.8413 = 0.1587.
  2. Upper tail. P(Z > z) = 1 - Φ(z).
  3. Between two values. P(a < Z ≤ b) = Φ(b) - Φ(a).

Worked example: a two-sided probability

Find P(-1 < Z ≤ 1) using the table.

  1. Use the interval rule. P(-1 < Z ≤ 1) = Φ(1) - Φ(-1).
  2. Read Φ(1) = 0.8413 from the table.
  3. Find Φ(-1) = 1 - Φ(1) = 1 - 0.8413 = 0.1587.
  4. Subtract. 0.8413 - 0.1587 = 0.6826, which is 0.6827 with more precise values.

The Python block below reproduces the table values exactly, and it is useful to check your table reading.

import math

def Phi(z):
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2)))

print(f"Phi(1) = {Phi(1):.4f}")
print(f"Phi(-1) = {Phi(-1):.4f}")
print(f"P(-1 < Z <= 1) = {Phi(1) - Phi(-1):.4f}")
Phi(1) = 0.8413
Phi(-1) = 0.1587
P(-1 < Z <= 1) = 0.6827

TrapTable rounding causes small differences. Our hand sum gave 0.6826 and the precise value is 0.6827. In a GATE question, use the table value given in the question, and do not round too early.

GATE loves thisQuestions often give a short table extract, such as Φ(1.5) = 0.9332 and Φ(2) = 0.9772, and ask you to combine them. Write each value on your sheet before you combine.

Self-check: if Φ(0.5) = 0.6915, what is Φ(-0.5)?

Φ(-0.5) = 1 - Φ(0.5) = 1 - 0.6915 = 0.3085.

Self-check: what is P(Z > 2) if Φ(2) = 0.9772?

P(Z > 2) = 1 - Φ(2) = 1 - 0.9772 = 0.0228.

Concept 5: Z-scores in GATE numericals

The general method

Every normal numerical follows the same steps. Learn these and most questions become routine.

  1. Identify μ and σ. If the question gives a variance, take the square root to get σ.
  2. Write the probability in terms of X. Decide whether you need P(X ≤ x), P(X > x), or an interval.
  3. Standardise each endpoint. Compute z = (x - μ)/σ for each boundary.
  4. Use the table or the complement rules. Look up Φ values and combine them.
  5. Check the size. Probabilities lie between 0 and 1, and the answer should agree with the empirical rule.

Worked example: a machine tolerance

A part length X is normal with mean 10 mm and standard deviation 0.2 mm. A part is accepted if its length lies between 9.6 mm and 10.4 mm. Find the probability that a part is accepted.

  1. Standardise the lower limit. z = (9.6 - 10)/0.2 = -2.
  2. Standardise the upper limit. z = (10.4 - 10)/0.2 = 2.
  3. Write the interval. P(9.6 ≤ X ≤ 10.4) = P(-2 ≤ Z ≤ 2) = Φ(2) - Φ(-2).
  4. Compute. Φ(2) = 0.9772 and Φ(-2) = 1 - 0.9772 = 0.0228. So the answer is 0.9772 - 0.0228 = 0.9544, about 0.9545 with precise values.
  5. Check. The interval is μ ± 2σ, so the empirical rule gives about 95 percent, which agrees.
import math

def Phi(z):
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2)))

mu, sigma = 10.0, 0.2
lo = (9.6 - mu) / sigma
hi = (10.4 - mu) / sigma
print(f"z-values: {lo:.1f} and {hi:.1f}")
print(f"P(accepted) = {Phi(hi) - Phi(lo):.4f}")
z-values: -2.0 and 2.0
P(accepted) = 0.9545

Key ideaEvery normal interval probability is a difference of two Φ values. Standardise both endpoints, then subtract.

TrapWhen the lower z is negative, Φ of a negative number is small. Writing Φ(lower) - Φ(upper) gives a negative result, which signals a sign error. Always write Φ(upper) - Φ(lower).

Self-check: for a normal with σ = 4, what is z for x = 58 if μ = 50?

z = (58 - 50)/4 = 2.

Self-check: why is P(a ≤ X ≤ b) the same as P(a < X < b) for a normal variable?

Because the normal is continuous, a single point has zero probability, so the endpoints add nothing to the area.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original GATE-style questions written for practice. They are not past paper questions. Work each one on paper first, then compare with the solution.

The median of a normal variable

X is normal with mean 50 and standard deviation 4. Find P(X ≤ 50).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The chance that X is at or below its mean.
  2. Which idea applies. Symmetry of the normal bell about its mean.
  3. Work it out. Standardise: z = (50 - 50)/4 = 0. So P(X ≤ 50) = Φ(0) = 0.5.
  4. Check. Half of a symmetric bell lies on each side of its centre, so 0.5 is the right answer.

Answer: 0.5

Select all true statements about a normal variable

X is normal with mean μ and variance σ². Select all that apply.

(A) The mean, median and mode are all equal to μ.
(B) P(X ≤ μ) = 0.5.
(C) The standard deviation is σ.
(D) P(X > μ + σ) = 0.5.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Tick each true statement.
  2. Which idea applies. Symmetry, the parameters μ and σ, and the one-standard-deviation probability.
  3. Work it out. Option A: the normal is symmetric about μ, so the three measures agree, and A is true. Option B: by symmetry, half the area lies below μ, so B is true. Option C: the variance is σ², so the standard deviation is σ, and C is true. Option D: P(X > μ + σ) = 1 - Φ(1) = 0.1587, not 0.5, so D is false.
  4. Check. Option D would say that the chance of exceeding one standard deviation above the mean is the same as the chance of exceeding the mean. That cannot be true, because the upper region is much smaller.

Answer: (A), (B) and (C) are true. Option (D) is false.

A high exam score

Scores on an exam are normal with mean 60 and standard deviation 10. Find the probability that a student scores more than 75. Give the answer correct to four decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. P(X > 75).
  2. Which idea applies. Standardise, then use the upper tail 1 - Φ(z).
  3. Work it out. z = (75 - 60)/10 = 1.5. P(X > 75) = 1 - Φ(1.5) = 1 - 0.9332 = 0.0668.
  4. Check. A score of 75 is 1.5 standard deviations above the mean. Only a small share of students should be beyond that, and 0.0668 is small, which agrees.

Answer: 0.0668

A relative standing comparison

Exam A scores are normal with mean 60 and standard deviation 10. Exam B scores are normal with mean 50 and standard deviation 5. Ravi scores 75 on Exam A and 62 on Exam B. In which exam did Ravi do better relative to the other students? Give the exam letter.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Compare the two scores on a common scale.
  2. Which idea applies. Compute the Z-score in each exam, because a larger Z means a better relative standing.
  3. Work it out. Exam A: z = (75 - 60)/10 = 1.5. Exam B: z = (62 - 50)/5 = 2.4. Since 2.4 is larger than 1.5, Ravi is further above the mean in Exam B.
  4. Check. A raw difference of 13 marks is larger in Exam A, but that does not mean much because Exam A spreads scores more widely. The Z-scores account for the spread, which is the fair comparison.

Answer: Exam B

The upper score cut-off

NAT question. Scores are normal with mean 50 and standard deviation 4. A student is in the top 10 percent if the score is at least k. Use the table value z = 1.28 for the top 10 percent, and find k. Give the answer correct to two decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The cut-off k such that P(X ≥ k) = 0.10.
  2. Which idea applies. P(Z ≥ z) = 0.10 means Φ(z) = 0.90, which gives z = 1.28 from the table. Then invert the Z-score.
  3. Work it out. k = μ + zσ = 50 + 1.28 × 4 = 50 + 5.12 = 55.12.
  4. Check. The cut-off must be above the mean, since the top 10 percent lies above the centre, and 55.12 is above 50. Standardising 55.12 gives z = 5.12/4 = 1.28, which matches.

Answer: 55.12

The sum of two independent normals

NAT question. X is normal with mean 10 and variance 4. Y is normal with mean 5 and variance 9. X and Y are independent. Let S = X + Y. Find P(S > 20). Give the answer correct to four decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The upper tail of the sum.
  2. Which idea applies. For independent normals, the sum is normal with mean μX + μY and variance σX² + σY², then standardise.
  3. Work it out. Mean of S = 10 + 5 = 15. Variance of S = 4 + 9 = 13, so σ = √13 ≈ 3.6056. z = (20 - 15)/3.6056 ≈ 1.3868. P(S > 20) = 1 - Φ(1.3868) ≈ 1 - 0.9172 = 0.0828.
  4. Check. The threshold 20 is about 1.39 standard deviations above the mean, so a small tail probability near 0.08 is expected.

Answer: 0.0828

Finding the mean from a tail

NAT question. X is normal with standard deviation 3 and unknown mean μ. It is given that P(X < 40) = 0.0228. Using Φ(-2) = 0.0228, find μ.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The unknown mean μ.
  2. Which idea applies. Standardise 40 with the unknown μ, and match the given probability to a Z value.
  3. Work it out. P(X < 40) = Φ((40 - μ)/3) = 0.0228. Since Φ(-2) = 0.0228, we need (40 - μ)/3 = -2. So 40 - μ = -6, and μ = 46.
  4. Check. With μ = 46, the value 40 is 6 below the mean, which is 2 standard deviations below. The chance below 2 standard deviations is 0.0228, which agrees with the given information.

Answer: 46

Exam-level 1: Standardising a normal interval (NAT, 2 marks)

NAT question. The weight X, in grams, of a chocolate bar is normally distributed with mean 50 grams and variance 16 grams squared. A bar is accepted if its weight is between 46 and 58 grams. Find the probability that a randomly chosen bar is accepted. Use the standard normal distribution function Φ, with Φ(1) = 0.8413 and Φ(2) = 0.9772, and give the answer correct to two decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. P(46 < X < 58), to two decimal places.
  2. Which idea applies. Standardise with Z = (X - μ) / σ. Remember that σ is the square root of the variance, so σ = 4 here, not 16. Then use P(a < X < b) = Φ(z_b) - Φ(z_a) and Φ(-z) = 1 - Φ(z).
  3. Work it out. z_low = (46 - 50) / 4 = -1 and z_high = (58 - 50) / 4 = 2. So P = Φ(2) - Φ(-1) = 0.9772 - (1 - 0.8413) = 0.9772 - 0.1587 = 0.8185, which is 0.82 to two decimal places.
  4. Check. The interval covers 1 standard deviation below the mean and 2 above. The tails outside it hold 0.1587 below and 0.0228 above, a total of 0.1815, so the accepted share is 1 - 0.1815 = 0.8185. The answer agrees.

Answer: 0.82

from statistics import NormalDist
X = NormalDist(mu=50, sigma=4)
p = X.cdf(58) - X.cdf(46)
print("P(46 to 58) =", round(p, 4))
print("z-values:", (46 - 50) / 4, (58 - 50) / 4)
print("to 2 dp:", round(p, 2))
P(46 to 58) = 0.8186
z-values: -1.0 2.0
to 2 dp: 0.82

Exam-level 2: Standard deviation of a difference (MCQ, 2 marks)

The daily output X of machine 1 is normal with mean 10 and variance 4. The daily output Y of machine 2 is normal with mean 5 and variance 9. The two outputs are independent. A manager tracks the quantity W = 2X - Y. Which of the following is the standard deviation of W? (A) 5 (B) 7 (C) √13 (D) 11. Select one option.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The standard deviation of W = 2X - Y.
  2. Which idea applies. For independent variables, Var(aX + bY) = a² Var(X) + b² Var(Y). The minus sign disappears when the variance is taken, because it is squared. Then the standard deviation is the square root of the variance.
  3. Work it out. Var(2X) = 2² × 4 = 16 and Var(-Y) = (-1)² × 9 = 9. So Var(W) = 16 + 9 = 25, and the standard deviation is √25 = 5. The answer is option A.
  4. Check. The answer 5 lies between the standard deviation of Y (3) and the sum 2 × 2 + 3 = 7, which is the largest possible spread. Why the other options fail: (B) adds standard deviations instead of variances, (C) is the standard deviation of X + Y and forgets the factor 2, and (D) is not produced by any correct step.

Answer: (A) 5

from math import sqrt
varX = 4
varY = 9
a = 2
b = -1
varW = a * a * varX + b * b * varY
print("Var(W) =", varW)
print("sd(W) =", sqrt(varW))
print("mean(W) =", a * 10 + b * 5)
print("sqrt(X + Y variance) =", round(sqrt(varX + varY), 4))
print("2 sd(X) + sd(Y) =", 2 * sqrt(varX) + sqrt(varY))
Var(W) = 25
sd(W) = 5.0
mean(W) = 15
sqrt(X + Y variance) = 3.6056
2 sd(X) + sd(Y) = 7.0

Exam-level 3: Facts about the standard normal (MSQ, 2 marks)

Z is a standard normal random variable, with mean 0 and variance 1. Use a standard normal table or a calculator to decide which statements are true. Select all the statements that are true.

  • (A) P(Z < 0) = 0.5
  • (B) P(-1 < Z < 1) is about 0.6827
  • (C) P(Z > 1.96) is about 0.025
  • (D) P(Z > 2) = 0.5

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which of the four statements are true. Select all that apply.
  2. Which idea applies. The symmetry of the standard normal about 0, the area within one standard deviation, and tail areas of the form 1 - Φ(z).
  3. Work it out. By symmetry P(Z < 0) = 0.5, so (A) is true. P(-1 < Z < 1) = Φ(1) - Φ(-1) = 2Φ(1) - 1 = 2(0.8413) - 1 = 0.6826 from the table, or about 0.6827 exactly, so (B) is true. P(Z > 1.96) = 1 - Φ(1.96) = 1 - 0.9750 = 0.025, so (C) is true. P(Z > 2) = 1 - Φ(2) = 1 - 0.9772 = 0.0228, not 0.5, so (D) is false.
  4. Check. Only the centre point 0 has the half-way area of 0.5. Option (D) confuses a tail area far out with the centre. The answer is A, B and C.

Answer: A, B and C

from statistics import NormalDist
Z = NormalDist()
print("P(Z < 0) =", round(Z.cdf(0), 4))
print("P(-1 to 1) =", round(Z.cdf(1) - Z.cdf(-1), 4))
print("P(Z exceeds 1.96) =", round(1 - Z.cdf(1.96), 4))
print("P(Z exceeds 2) =", round(1 - Z.cdf(2), 4))
P(Z < 0) = 0.5
P(-1 to 1) = 0.6827
P(Z exceeds 1.96) = 0.025
P(Z exceeds 2) = 0.0228

Exam-level 4: An upper percentile (NAT, 2 marks)

NAT question. The tensile strength X of a steel rod, in megapascals, is normally distributed with mean 80 and variance 25. A rod is classed as strong when its strength exceeds a limit c. Only 10 percent of rods are classed as strong, so P(X > c) = 0.10. Find the limit c. Give the answer correct to two decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The limit c with P(X > c) = 0.10, to two decimal places.
  2. Which idea applies. Find the z-value with 10 percent of the area above it, then convert back with c = μ + zσ.
  3. Work it out. The standard deviation is σ = √25 = 5. The 90th percentile of the standard normal is z ≈ 1.2816, because Φ(1.2816) ≈ 0.90. So c = 80 + 1.2816 × 5 = 80 + 6.408 = 86.408, which is 86.41 to two decimal places.
  4. Check. The limit must sit above the mean, since only 10 percent of rods are above it, and 86.41 is above 80. It is also about 1.28 standard deviations above the mean, which matches the z-value.

Answer: 86.41

from statistics import NormalDist
X = NormalDist(mu=80, sigma=5)
c = X.inv_cdf(0.90)
print("c =", round(c, 4))
print("check P(X exceeds c) =", round(1 - X.cdf(c), 4))
print("to 2 dp:", round(c, 2))

Summary

Normal distribution
A symmetric bell-shaped distribution with mean μ and variance σ².
Mean
The centre μ of the bell, equal to the median and the mode.
Standard deviation
σ, the square root of the variance, which sets the width of the bell.
Standard normal
Z ~ N(0, 1), with mean 0 and variance 1.
Z-score
z = (x - μ)/σ, the distance from the mean in standard deviations.
Φ(z)
P(Z ≤ z), read from a table or computed by software.
Symmetry rule
Φ(-z) = 1 - Φ(z).
Interval rule
P(a < X ≤ b) = Φ((b - μ)/σ) - Φ((a - μ)/σ).
Empirical rule
About 68, 95 and 99.7 percent lie within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations.
Median and mode
Both equal μ for a normal distribution.
Sum of independent normals
Normal, with means added and variances added.
Inverse Z-score
x = μ + zσ, used to find a cut-off from a probability.
Relative standing
Compare Z-scores, not raw scores, when the spreads differ.
Two-sided probability
The area between two symmetric points, often 2Φ(z) - 1.

Remember these

  1. Standardise every normal problem first: z = (x - μ)/σ.
  2. Divide by σ, the standard deviation, never by the variance.
  3. The normal bell is symmetric, so P(X ≤ μ) = 0.5.
  4. Use Φ(-z) = 1 - Φ(z) for negative values.
  5. Write interval probabilities as Φ(upper) minus Φ(lower).
  6. Within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations lie about 68, 95 and 99.7 percent of values.
  7. For a sum of independent normals, add the means and add the variances.

Transient response of DC & AC networks

When a switch is thrown, a circuit does not jump to its new state. It takes time to settle, and that settling is called the transient response. Capacitors and inductors store energy, which explains why the response is not instant. GATE RA and DA ask for these responses in both DC and AC circuits.

The idea in plain words

Think of filling a bathtub. When you open the tap, the water level does not jump to its final height. It rises over time, and the rise slows down as the level gets closer to the final height. A circuit with a capacitor or an inductor behaves in the same way when a switch is thrown. The voltage or current moves smoothly from its old value to its new value.

The reason is energy. A capacitor stores energy in its electric field, and it holds a voltage that cannot change in zero time. An inductor stores energy in its magnetic field, and it holds a current that cannot change in zero time. Resistors store nothing, so they do not delay anything. The delay comes from the storage elements.

The part of the response that dies away is the transient. The part that remains is the steady state. Almost every GATE question on transients asks for the value at a given time, the time constant, or the type of damping. Learn the three-step method on this page and most of those questions become routine.

Key ideaA capacitor's voltage and an inductor's current cannot jump at the moment of switching. Use their values just before the switch as the starting point.

Key terms

Each term has a plain meaning first, then a precise definition.

Transient
The temporary part of a response that dies away. Precisely: the solution part that decays to zero as time goes on.
Steady state
The settled behaviour after a long time. Precisely: the part of the response that remains after all transients have decayed.
Step response
The response to a sudden change, such as a switch closing. Precisely: the output when the input jumps from one constant value to another.
Initial condition
The value just after switching. Precisely: the capacitor voltage v(0+) or the inductor current i(0+).
Final value
The steady value as time goes to infinity. Precisely: the value at t → ∞.
Time constant τ
The time that sets the speed of the response. Precisely: τ = RC for an RC circuit and τ = L/R for an RL circuit, in seconds.
Natural response
The response when no source drives the circuit. Precisely: the decaying part set by the stored energy.
Forced response
The response driven by the source. Precisely: the steady part of the solution.
Damping
The loss of energy that makes oscillations die away. Precisely: the resistance that dissipates energy in an RLC circuit.
Damping ratio ζ
A number that decides the type of response. Precisely: ζ = α/ω0, with α = R/(2L) and ω0 = 1/√(LC).
Overdamped, critically damped, underdamped
The three types of second-order response, for ζ above 1, equal to 1, and below 1.
Second-order circuit
A circuit with two independent storage elements, such as an RLC circuit. Its equation contains a second derivative.

Storage elements and initial conditions

Why the capacitor voltage cannot jump

A capacitor's voltage is related to its charge, q = Cv. Charge cannot appear or disappear in a finite circuit, and a finite current cannot move charge instantly. So the voltage across a capacitor is continuous. If the capacitor had 6 V just before the switch, it still has 6 V just after.

vC(0+) = vC(0−)

Why the inductor current cannot jump

An inductor's current is related to its flux. A voltage across an inductor cannot change its current instantly, because the change needs a finite voltage for a finite time. So the current through an inductor is continuous. If the inductor carried 2 A just before the switch, it still carries 2 A just after.

iL(0+) = iL(0−)

The notation 0− means just before switching, and 0+ means just after. Both are usually the same value.

What the elements look like at the instant of switching

At 0+, a capacitor with a known voltage can be replaced by a voltage source of that value. An inductor with a known current can be replaced by a current source of that value. Once you have done this, the circuit is a simple resistive circuit, and you can solve it.

TrapThe capacitor voltage and inductor current are continuous. Other quantities, such as the capacitor current and the inductor voltage, may jump. Do not assume that every quantity is continuous.

Self-check

A capacitor holds 4 V just before a switch closes. What is its voltage just after?

It is still 4 V, because the capacitor voltage is continuous.

An inductor carries 1.5 A just before switching. Can its current be 0 A just after?

No. The inductor current is continuous, so it must still be 1.5 A just after switching.

First-order RC and RL step responses

The RC circuit

A circuit with one resistor and one capacitor, driven by a step voltage, is a first-order circuit. Its time constant is τ = RC. Charging a capacitor from zero toward a source Vs gives:

vC(t) = Vs (1 − e−t/τ) for t ≥ 0, with τ = RC

At t = 0 the voltage is zero, as required. As t grows, the exponential e−t/τ shrinks toward zero, and the voltage approaches Vs. The current through the resistor is i(t) = (Vs/R) e−t/τ, which starts at Vs/R and dies away.

Worked example 1: charging a capacitor

A 12 V source charges a capacitor through a 2 kilo-ohm resistor. The capacitor is 5 µF and starts uncharged. Find τ, the voltage at 10 ms and 20 ms, and the starting current.

  1. Time constant. τ = RC = 2000 × 5 × 10−6 = 0.01 s, which is 10 ms.
  2. Voltage at 10 ms. t/τ = 1, so v = 12(1 − e−1) = 12 × 0.632 = 7.585 V.
  3. Voltage at 20 ms. t/τ = 2, so v = 12(1 − e−2) = 12 × 0.865 = 10.376 V.
  4. Starting current. i(0) = 12/2000 = 0.006 A, which is 6 mA.
import math
V_s, R, C = 12.0, 2000.0, 5e-6
tau = R * C
print("tau =", round(tau * 1000, 3), "ms")
for t in [0.010, 0.020]:
    v = V_s * (1 - math.exp(-t / tau))
    print("t =", round(t * 1000), "ms, v =", round(v, 3), "V")
print("initial current =", round(V_s / R * 1000, 3), "mA")
tau = 10.0 ms
t = 10 ms, v = 7.585 V
t = 20 ms, v = 10.376 V
initial current = 6.0 mA

The RL circuit

A resistor and an inductor, driven by a step voltage, form another first-order circuit. Its time constant is τ = L/R. The current rises from zero toward the final value Vs/R:

iL(t) = (Vs/R)(1 − e−t/τ), with τ = L/R

Notice the difference from RC. Here the inductance sits in the top of τ = L/R, so a larger inductance slows the response. A larger resistance makes τ smaller, which speeds the response up.

Worked example 2: a current rising in an inductor

A 10 V step is applied to a 5 ohm resistor in series with a 2 H inductor. The initial current is zero. Find τ, the current at 0.4 s and 0.8 s, and the final current.

  1. Time constant. τ = L/R = 2/5 = 0.4 s.
  2. Final current. V/R = 10/5 = 2 A.
  3. Current at 0.4 s. t/τ = 1, so i = 2(1 − e−1) = 2 × 0.632 = 1.264 A.
  4. Current at 0.8 s. t/τ = 2, so i = 2(1 − e−2) = 2 × 0.865 = 1.729 A.
import math
V, R, L = 10.0, 5.0, 2.0
tau = L / R
Ifinal = V / R
print("tau =", tau, "s, final current =", Ifinal, "A")
for t in [0.4, 0.8]:
    i = Ifinal * (1 - math.exp(-t / tau))
    print("t =", t, "s, i =", round(i, 3), "A")
tau = 0.4 s, final current = 2.0 A
t = 0.4 s, i = 1.264 A
t = 0.8 s, i = 1.729 A

What the time constant means

After one time constant, the response has covered 1 − e−1, about 63.2 percent of its total change. After five time constants, it has covered 1 − e−5, about 99.3 percent, and we call the response settled. These numbers are worth remembering.

TipUse the time constant as a ruler. Count how many τ have passed, then use the table of percentages instead of working out the exponential each time.

Self-check

An RC circuit has R = 1 kilo-ohm and C = 10 µF. What is τ?

τ = RC = 1000 × 10 × 10−6 = 0.01 s, which is 10 ms.

In an RL circuit, what happens to τ if the resistance doubles?

τ = L/R halves, so the response becomes twice as fast.

The three-step method and time constant

The general form

Every first-order response, whatever the circuit, has the same shape:

x(t) = xfinal + (x(0+) − xfinal) e−t/τ

Here x stands for the capacitor voltage or the inductor current. This form covers both charging and discharging.

The three steps

  1. Initial value. Find x(0+) using the continuity rule. Use the value just before switching for the capacitor voltage or the inductor current.
  2. Final value. Find xfinal by replacing capacitors with open circuits and inductors with short circuits, then solving the resistive circuit.
  3. Time constant. Find the resistance Rth seen by the storage element, with independent sources off. Then τ = RthC or τ = L/Rth.

Worked example 3: discharging a capacitor

A capacitor of 1 µF is charged to 6 V. At t = 0 it is connected through a 3 kilo-ohm resistor to nothing else. Find the voltage after 3 ms.

  1. Initial value. v(0+) = 6 V.
  2. Final value. With no source, the capacitor discharges fully, so vfinal = 0.
  3. Time constant. τ = RC = 3000 × 1 × 10−6 = 0.003 s, which is 3 ms.
  4. Result. v(t) = 0 + (6 − 0)e−t/τ = 6e−t/τ. At t = 3 ms, t/τ = 1, so v = 6/e = 2.207 V.
import math
V0, R, C = 6.0, 3000.0, 1e-6
tau = R * C
print("tau =", round(tau * 1000, 3), "ms")
v = V0 * math.exp(-0.003 / tau)
print("v after 3 ms =", round(v, 3), "V")
tau = 3.0 ms
v after 3 ms = 2.207 V

TrapWhen finding Rth for the time constant, switch off independent sources only. Use the resistance the storage element sees, not the resistance of a single resistor in the picture.

GATE loves thisMost first-order questions follow the three steps exactly. If you write the three values on paper first, the answer is usually a single substitution.

Self-check

A capacitor starts at 10 V and is driven toward a final value of 4 V with τ = 2 s. What is v at t = 2 s?

v = 4 + (10 − 4)e−1 = 4 + 6 × 0.368 = 6.21 V, which is 6.21 V to two decimals.

Why do we replace a capacitor by an open circuit to find the final value?

After a long time, the capacitor is fully charged or fully discharged, so no current flows through it. A zero-current element is an open circuit.

Second-order series RLC and damping

The second-order equation

A series RLC circuit, with a step voltage and no initial energy, has two storage elements. Its response obeys a second-order equation. The key numbers are:

α = R/(2L), ω0 = 1/√(LC), ζ = α/ω0 = (R/2)√(C/L)

Here α is the damping rate in per second, ω0 is the natural frequency in rad/s, and ζ is the damping ratio, which has no unit. The value of ζ decides the type of response.

The three cases

  • Overdamped, ζ > 1. The response rises smoothly, with no oscillation, and is slow. It is the sum of two decaying exponentials.
  • Critically damped, ζ = 1. The response is the fastest one that still avoids oscillation. It has a repeated root.
  • Underdamped, ζ < 1. The response oscillates while it decays. Its oscillation frequency is ωd = √(ω02 − α2), which is slightly less than ω0.

For a step input to the capacitor voltage, the underdamped response overshoots the final value before it settles. The overshoot is largest when ζ is small.

Key ideaLarger resistance means more damping, so the response is smoother. Smaller resistance means less damping, so the response oscillates more.

Worked example 4: an underdamped step

A series RLC circuit has R = 4 ohm, L = 0.5 H and C = 0.02 F. A 10 V step is applied with zero initial energy. Find ω0, α, ζ and ωd, and the capacitor voltage at t = 0.1 s.

  1. Natural frequency. LC = 0.5 × 0.02 = 0.01, so ω0 = 1/0.1 = 10 rad/s.
  2. Damping rate. α = R/(2L) = 4/1 = 4 per second.
  3. Damping ratio. ζ = α/ω0 = 4/10 = 0.4. Since 0.4 is less than 1, the response is underdamped.
  4. Oscillation frequency. ωd = √(100 − 16) = √84, about 9.165 rad/s.
  5. Capacitor voltage. vC(t) = V(1 − e−αt(cos ωdt + (α/ωd) sin ωdt)). This starts at zero and ends at 10 V, as required. At t = 0.1 s, the value is about 3.599 V.
import math
R, L, C, V = 4.0, 0.5, 0.02, 10.0
w0 = 1 / math.sqrt(L * C)
alpha = R / (2 * L)
zeta = alpha / w0
wd = math.sqrt(w0 ** 2 - alpha ** 2)
print("w0 =", round(w0, 3), "rad/s, alpha =", alpha, "/s")
print("zeta =", zeta)
print("wd =", round(wd, 3), "rad/s")
t = 0.1
vc = V * (1 - math.exp(-alpha * t) * (math.cos(wd * t) + (alpha / wd) * math.sin(wd * t)))
print("vc at 0.1 s =", round(vc, 3), "V")
w0 = 10.0 rad/s, alpha = 4.0 /s
zeta = 0.4
wd = 9.165 rad/s
vc at 0.1 s = 3.599 V

TrapDo not confuse ω0 and ωd. The natural frequency ω0 sets the undamped rhythm, while ωd is the actual oscillation frequency with damping present. ωd is always smaller.

Self-check

For a series RLC, what value of R gives critical damping, in terms of L and C?

ζ = 1 means (R/2)√(C/L) = 1, so R = 2√(L/C).

If ζ = 0.25 and ω0 = 2 rad/s, what is α?

α = ζω0 = 0.25 × 2 = 0.5 per second.

AC transients after switching

Steady state plus transient

When a sinusoidal source is switched on, the response has two parts. The steady-state part is a sinusoid at the source frequency. The transient part decays with the time constant of the circuit. The total response is:

i(t) = iss(t) + K e−t/τ

The constant K is chosen so that the initial current matches the continuity rule. For a series RL circuit fed by v = Vm sin(ωt) and switched on at t = 0, the result is:

i(t) = (Vm/|Z|)[sin(ωt − φ) + sin φ · e−Rt/L], with φ = arctan(ωL/R)

Here |Z| = √(R2 + ω2L2). At t = 0, the bracket is sin(−φ) + sin φ = 0, which matches the rule that the inductor current starts at zero.

When is the transient largest?

The size of the transient depends on the instant the switch closes. If the switch closes when the steady-state current would be at its peak, the transient is as large as the peak itself. The total current can then reach nearly twice its steady peak early on. If the switch closes when the steady current is zero, the transient is zero. GATE questions often ask about this worst case.

TipFor an AC switching question, first find the steady-state current. Then use the continuity rule at the switching instant to find the size of the transient. The transient is whatever value is needed to make the current continuous.

Self-check

In the RL result above, what does the term sin φ e−Rt/L represent?

It is the transient. It decays with the time constant L/R, and it is needed to make the current zero at t = 0.

When is the transient current largest?

When the switch closes at an instant where the steady-state current would be at its peak. The transient then equals the peak value and must decay from it.

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original problems in the GATE style, not past exam questions. Try each one on paper first.

Time constant of an RC circuit

A 1 kilo-ohm resistor is in series with a 10 µF capacitor. Find τ in millisecond.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The time constant τ.
  2. Which idea applies. τ = RC for an RC circuit.
  3. Work it out. τ = 1000 × 10 × 10−6 = 0.01 s = 10 ms.
  4. Check. Units: ohm times farad gives seconds. 10 ms is a sensible speed for this pair.

Answer: 10 ms.

Time constant of an RL circuit

A 4 H inductor is in series with a 2 ohm resistor. Find τ in second.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. τ for an RL circuit.
  2. Which idea applies. τ = L/R.
  3. Work it out. τ = 4/2 = 2 s.
  4. Check. Ohm and henry combine as L/R, which is in seconds.

Answer: 2 s.

Statements about continuity and time constants (MSQ)

Select all that apply.

(A) The capacitor voltage cannot change instantly.
(B) The inductor voltage can change instantly while the current stays the same.
(C) After five time constants, an RC step response is about 99 percent complete.
(D) In an RL circuit, τ = RL.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. Continuity of vC and iL, and the time constant rule.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) True: the voltage across an inductor is L di/dt, and it can jump while the current is continuous. (C) True: 1 − e−5 is about 0.993. (D) False: τ = L/R.
  4. Check. Statement (C) matches the 99.3 percent value worked out in the text.

Answer: (A), (B) and (C).

Capacitor voltage at one time constant (NAT)

A 5 V source charges a capacitor through a 10 kilo-ohm resistor. The capacitor is 100 µF and starts uncharged. Find the capacitor voltage at t = 1 s, in volt.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. vC(1 s).
  2. Which idea applies. The RC charging formula with τ = RC.
  3. Work it out. τ = 10000 × 100 × 10−6 = 1 s. So v = 5(1 − e−1) = 5 × 0.632 = 3.161 V.
  4. Check. One time constant gives about 63 percent of the final value, and 63 percent of 5 V is about 3.16 V.

Answer: about 3.161 V.

import math
print("v =", round(5 * (1 - math.exp(-1)), 3), "V")
v = 3.161 V

Discharge after one time constant (NAT)

A capacitor is charged to 8 V and then discharges through a resistor with τ = 2 s. Find its voltage after 2 s, in volt.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. v(2 s).
  2. Which idea applies. A pure discharge: v(t) = v(0+)e−t/τ.
  3. Work it out. t/τ = 1, so v = 8e−1 = 2.943 V.
  4. Check. The voltage must be below 8 V and above zero, and 2.943 V fits.

Answer: about 2.943 V.

import math
print("v =", round(8 * math.exp(-1), 3), "V")
v = 2.943 V

Time for an RL current to reach half of its maximum (NAT)

A 12 V step is applied to a series circuit with R = 6 ohm and L = 3 H, starting from zero current. Find the time, in second, at which the current reaches 1 A.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The time t when i = 1 A.
  2. Which idea applies. i(t) = (V/R)(1 − e−t/τ) with τ = L/R.
  3. Work it out. τ = 3/6 = 0.5 s and V/R = 2 A. Set 1 = 2(1 − e−t/τ), so e−t/τ = 1/2, and t = τ ln 2 = 0.5 × 0.693 = 0.347 s.
  4. Check. Half the final current is reached at about 0.7 time constants, a little less than one time constant, which fits the 63 percent rule.

Answer: about 0.347 s.

import math
print("t =", round(0.5 * math.log(2), 3), "s")
t = 0.347 s

Damped frequency of a series RLC (NAT)

A series RLC has R = 1 ohm, L = 1 H and C = 0.25 F. It is driven by a step voltage with zero initial energy. Find the oscillation frequency ωd in rad/s, to two decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. ωd.
  2. Which idea applies. First find ω0 and α, then ωd = √(ω02 − α2), if the response is underdamped.
  3. Work it out. ω0 = 1/√(0.25) = 2 rad/s. α = 1/(2 × 1) = 0.5 per second. ζ = 0.5/2 = 0.25, which is below 1, so the response oscillates. ωd = √(4 − 0.25) = √3.75, about 1.94 rad/s.
  4. Check. ωd is slightly less than ω0 = 2, as it must be.

Answer: about 1.94 rad/s.

import math
w0 = 1 / math.sqrt(1 * 0.25)
alpha = 1 / (2 * 1)
print("wd =", round(math.sqrt(w0 ** 2 - alpha ** 2), 2), "rad/s")
wd = 1.94 rad/s

Statements about second-order responses (MSQ)

Select all that apply, for a series RLC step response.

(A) If R is greater than 2√(L/C), the response is overdamped.
(B) An underdamped capacitor voltage overshoots its final value.
(C) An overdamped response oscillates before it settles.
(D) Critical damping occurs when R = √(L/C).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. The damping ratio ζ = (R/2)√(C/L), and the behaviour of each case.
  3. Work it out. (A) True: R > 2√(L/C) gives ζ > 1. (B) True: an underdamped response overshoots. (C) False: overdamped responses do not oscillate. (D) False: critical damping needs R = 2√(L/C).
  4. Check. For the example of Worked example 4, R = 4, L = 0.5 and C = 0.02 give 2√(L/C) = 2√25 = 10 ohm. Since R = 4 is less than 10, the response is underdamped, which agrees with ζ = 0.4.

Answer: (A) and (B).

A 10 V step is applied to R = 2 kΩ in series with C = 50 µF, initially uncharged. Find the capacitor voltage at t = 0.2 s, correct to 2 decimal places.

  2. Which idea applies. i = I0e−Rt/L, τ = L/R = 0.1 s.

Exam-level 3: Damping of RLC (MCQ, 2 marks)

A series RLC with L = 1 H and C = 0.25 F. For what R is the response critically damped? (A) 1 Ω (B) 2 Ω (C) 4 Ω (D) 8 Ω

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The critical resistance.
  2. Which idea applies. Critical damping when R = 2√(L/C).
  3. Work it out. √(1/0.25) = 2, so R = 4 Ω.
  4. Check. Smaller R gives an oscillating (underdamped) response.

Sinusoidal steady-state analysis & resonance

A sinusoidal source drives a circuit that settles into a steady rhythm. Phasors and impedance turn that rhythm into simple algebra. Resonance is the special frequency where an RLC circuit responds most strongly, and GATE asks about it constantly.

The idea in plain words

Think of a child on a swing. If you push the swing once, it moves and then slowly settles. If you push it regularly, at the right rhythm, it swings higher and higher. A sinusoidal source is a source whose voltage goes up and down in a smooth wave, like a swing. After the first few moments, the circuit settles into the same wave shape, at the same frequency. This settled state is called the steady state.

In the steady state, every current and voltage in a linear circuit is also a sinusoid at the source frequency. Only two things can change: the size (amplitude) and the timing (phase). A phasor is a single complex number that holds both. Its length gives the amplitude and its angle gives the phase. Because of this, we can replace all the calculus with simple algebra.

Each element then gets an impedance, which is a complex number that plays the role of resistance. The big idea of this topic is resonance. At one special frequency, the reactance of the inductor and the capacitor cancel. The circuit then behaves like a pure resistor, and the response is at its largest. GATE RA and DA ask about the resonant frequency, the quality factor, and the response almost every year.

Key ideaIn steady state, treat the circuit as if it were resistive, with each element having an impedance. Then use the same rules you already know, such as series and parallel combinations and Ohm's law.

Key terms

Each term has a plain meaning first, then a precise definition.

Sinusoid
A smooth wave shaped like cos or sin. Precisely: v(t) = Vm cos(ωt + φ).
Amplitude Vm
The peak size of the wave. Precisely: the largest value the voltage reaches.
Angular frequency ω
How fast the wave repeats, in radians per second. Precisely: ω = 2πf, where f is the frequency in hertz.
Phase φ
The timing shift of the wave. Precisely: the angle at t = 0.
Phasor
A complex number that holds amplitude and phase. Precisely: V = Vm ∠φ, which is Vm ejφ.
j
The imaginary unit, used by engineers instead of i for current. Precisely: j2 = −1.
Impedance Z
The AC version of resistance. Precisely: Z = V/I for phasors, measured in ohm.
Reactance X
The imaginary part of impedance. Precisely: Z = R + jX.
Inductive reactance XL
The reactance of an inductor. Precisely: XL = ωL.
Capacitive reactance XC
The reactance of a capacitor. Precisely: XC = 1/(ωC), entering the impedance as −jXC.
Resonance
The condition where the reactances cancel. Precisely: XL = XC, so the imaginary part of Z is zero.
Resonant frequency ω0
The frequency where resonance occurs. Precisely: ω0 = 1/√(LC).
Quality factor Q
A measure of how sharp the resonance is. Precisely: Q = ω0L/R for a series RLC circuit.
Bandwidth B
The width of the band of frequencies with a strong response. Precisely: B = R/L for a series RLC circuit, in rad/s.
Half-power points
The two frequencies where the power falls to half its peak. Precisely: the edges of the bandwidth.

Phasors and impedance

From waves to phasors

A sinusoidal voltage is v(t) = Vm cos(ωt + φ). Take the time part, ωt, out of the picture. What is left is the amplitude Vm and the phase φ. These two numbers are packed into a phasor, written in polar form as V = Vm ∠φ. Phasors make the algebra easy, because multiplying by a constant is just scaling, and adding waves is just adding complex numbers.

Complex numbers have a real part and an imaginary part, a + jb. In a phasor diagram, the real part is the horizontal distance and the imaginary part is the vertical distance. For example, 3 + j4 has length √(9 + 16) = 5 and angle atan(4/3), about 53.13 degrees.

Impedance of the three basic elements

In a steady sinusoidal state, each element relates its voltage and current in a simple way. The impedance is the ratio V/I.

ElementImpedance ZReactancePhase of V relative to I
Resistor RR0same phase
Inductor LjωLXL = ωLV leads I by 90 degrees
Capacitor C1/(jωC) = −j/(ωC)XC = 1/(ωC)V lags I by 90 degrees

Two points help you remember. The inductor's reactance grows with frequency, while the capacitor's reactance shrinks with frequency. A simple way to remember the phase is that the inductor's voltage "leads" the current, and the capacitor's voltage "lags" it.

Worked example 1: the impedance of a series RLC at one frequency

Take R = 4 ohm, L = 0.02 H and C = 0.005 F, at ω = 100 rad/s. Find the impedance.

  1. Find XL. XL = ωL = 100 × 0.02 = 2 ohm. The inductor impedance is j2.
  2. Find XC. XC = 1/(ωC) = 1/(100 × 0.005) = 1/0.5 = 2 ohm. The capacitor impedance is −j2.
  3. Add the three. Series impedances add. Z = 4 + j2 − j2 = 4 ohm.
  4. Read the result. The imaginary part is zero, so the circuit looks purely resistive at this frequency. This is resonance, as we will see.

Self-check

What is the impedance of a 0.1 H inductor at ω = 200 rad/s?

XL = 200 × 0.1 = 20 ohm, so Z = j20 ohm.

Does a capacitor's voltage lead or lag its current?

It lags the current by 90 degrees. Its impedance is −j/(ωC), which has a negative imaginary part.

Series and parallel RLC circuits

The series RLC

In a series RLC circuit, the resistor, inductor and capacitor are connected end to end, so the same current flows through all three. Their impedances add:

Z = R + j(ωL − 1/(ωC))

Here R is the resistance, ωL is the inductive reactance, and 1/(ωC) is the capacitive reactance. The total reactance is X = ωL − 1/(ωC). When X is positive, the circuit is inductive, and the current lags the voltage. When X is negative, the circuit is capacitive, and the current leads the voltage.

The current is I = V/Z. Its size is |I| = |V|/|Z|, where |Z| = √(R2 + X2). Its phase is the phase of V minus the phase of Z.

Worked example 2: off resonance

Use the same circuit as Worked example 1, but at ω = 50 rad/s. The source is V = 12 V (peak), with zero phase. Find Z and I.

  1. Find the reactances. XL = 50 × 0.02 = 1 ohm. XC = 1/(50 × 0.005) = 1/0.25 = 4 ohm.
  2. Find Z. Z = 4 + j1 − j4 = 4 − j3 ohm. The reactance is −3, so the circuit is capacitive here.
  3. Find |Z|. |Z| = √(42 + 32) = √25 = 5 ohm.
  4. Find I. I = 12/(4 − j3). Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate 4 + j3: I = 12(4 + j3)/25 = (48 + j36)/25 = 1.92 + j1.44 A.
  5. Find |I| and the phase. |I| = 12/5 = 2.4 A, and the angle is atan(1.44/1.92), which is about 36.87 degrees. The current leads the voltage by this angle, as expected for a capacitive circuit.
import cmath, math
R, L, C, V = 4.0, 0.02, 0.005, 12.0
w = 50.0
Z = R + 1j * w * L + 1 / (1j * w * C)
I = V / Z
print(f"Z = {Z.real:.2f} {Z.imag:+.2f} j ohm")
print(f"|Z| = {abs(Z):.2f} ohm")
print(f"I = {I.real:.2f} {I.imag:+.2f} j A")
print(f"|I| = {abs(I):.2f} A")
print(f"phase of I = {math.degrees(cmath.phase(I)):.2f} degrees")
Z = 4.00 -3.00 j ohm
|Z| = 5.00 ohm
I = 1.92 +1.44 j A
|I| = 2.40 A
phase of I = 36.87 degrees

The parallel RLC

In a parallel RLC circuit, the three elements share the same voltage. Their admittances add, where admittance Y = 1/Z. The parallel form is used when the source feeds the L, C and R branches together. Its resonance behaves differently, as explained below.

TrapDo not add the reciprocals of impedances in series, and do not add impedances in parallel. Series impedances add directly. Parallel impedances combine as 1/Z = 1/Z1 + 1/Z2. Mixing these up is the most common error in AC circuit questions.

Self-check

Two impedances, j5 ohm and −j5 ohm, are connected in series. What is the total?

Z = j5 + (−j5) = 0 ohm. This is an ideal short at this frequency. It is the reason the series circuit has a large current at resonance when R is small.

In Worked example 2, why is the circuit capacitive?

Because XC = 4 ohm is larger than XL = 1 ohm. The net reactance is negative, which means the capacitor dominates.

Resonance

What resonance means

As the frequency rises, XL grows and XC shrinks. Somewhere between, they become equal. At that frequency, the net reactance is zero, so the circuit's impedance is purely resistive. This condition is resonance. The frequency is the resonant frequency.

ωL = 1/(ωC), so ω0 = 1/√(LC)

Here ω0 is the resonant angular frequency in rad/s, L is the inductance in henry, and C is the capacitance in farad. The frequency in hertz is f0 = ω0/(2π).

Worked example 3: finding ω0 and the current at resonance

In the same circuit (L = 0.02 H, C = 0.005 F, R = 4 ohm), find the resonant frequency. Then find the current if V = 12 V (peak).

  1. Find ω0. LC = 0.02 × 0.005 = 0.0001. The square root is 0.01. So ω0 = 1/0.01 = 100 rad/s.
  2. Find f0. f0 = 100/(2π), about 15.92 Hz.
  3. Find Z at resonance. XL = XC = 2 ohm, so Z = R = 4 ohm.
  4. Find I. I = 12/4 = 3 A (peak), in phase with the voltage.
import math
L, C, R, V = 0.02, 0.005, 4.0, 12.0
w0 = 1 / math.sqrt(L * C)
print("w0 =", round(w0, 2), "rad/s")
print("f0 =", round(w0 / (2 * math.pi), 2), "Hz")
print("current at resonance =", V / R, "A")
w0 = 100.0 rad/s
f0 = 15.92 Hz
current at resonance = 3.0 A

Series and parallel resonance

In a series RLC, the impedance is smallest at resonance, equal to R. So the current is largest. In a parallel RLC, where the source feeds the L, C and R branches together, the impedance is largest at resonance. The source current is then smallest. This reversal is a favourite GATE trap, so learn both behaviours.

GATE loves thisAsk two questions in every resonance problem. Is the circuit series or parallel? Then the answer about the current or the impedance follows directly.

Self-check

A series RLC has L = 1 H and C = 0.25 F. What is ω0?

LC = 0.25, √0.25 = 0.5, so ω0 = 1/0.5 = 2 rad/s.

At resonance, what is the impedance of a series RLC with R = 10 ohm?

The reactances cancel, so Z = R = 10 ohm. The current is then at its maximum for the given source voltage.

Quality factor and bandwidth

The quality factor

Imagine two swings. One is heavy and damped, so it barely responds to the push. Another is light and springy, so it swings strongly only at one rhythm. The quality factor Q measures this sharpness. A large Q means a sharp, narrow peak. A small Q means a broad, flat response.

Q = ω0L/R = (1/R)√(L/C) for a series RLC

A small resistance gives a large Q, because the resistor wastes less energy. A large resistance damps the response and gives a small Q.

The bandwidth

The bandwidth is the range of frequencies over which the power is at least half its peak value. For a series RLC circuit, it is simply:

B = R/L (rad/s), and B = ω0/Q

The two half-power points multiply to ω02, and their values are:

ω1,2 = √(α2 + ω02) ∓ α, where α = R/(2L)

Their difference is ω2 − ω1 = 2α = R/L, which is the bandwidth.

Worked example 4: Q and bandwidth

Take L = 0.02 H, C = 0.005 F and R = 0.4 ohm. (A smaller R than before.) Find ω0, Q, the bandwidth, and the half-power points.

  1. Resonant frequency. ω0 = 100 rad/s, as before, since L and C are unchanged.
  2. Quality factor. Q = ω0L/R = 100 × 0.02/0.4 = 2/0.4 = 5.
  3. Bandwidth. B = R/L = 0.4/0.02 = 20 rad/s. Check: ω0/Q = 100/5 = 20.
  4. Half-power points. α = R/(2L) = 0.4/0.04 = 10. Then √(α2 + ω02) = √(100 + 10000) = √10100, about 100.499. So ω2 = 100.499 + 10 = 110.499 rad/s, and ω1 = 100.499 − 10 = 90.499 rad/s. Their difference is 20 rad/s.
import math
L, C, R = 0.02, 0.005, 0.4
w0 = 1 / math.sqrt(L * C)
Q = w0 * L / R
B = R / L
alpha = R / (2 * L)
w_high = math.sqrt(alpha ** 2 + w0 ** 2) + alpha
w_low = math.sqrt(alpha ** 2 + w0 ** 2) - alpha
print("Q =", round(Q, 3))
print("B =", round(B, 3), "rad/s")
print("w_low =", round(w_low, 3), "rad/s")
print("w_high =", round(w_high, 3), "rad/s")
print("w_high - w_low =", round(w_high - w_low, 3), "rad/s")
Q = 5.0
B = 20.0 rad/s
w_low = 90.499 rad/s
w_high = 110.499 rad/s
w_high - w_low = 20.0 rad/s

Self-check

A series RLC has R = 2 ohm, L = 0.5 H and ω0 = 20 rad/s. What is Q?

Q = ω0L/R = 20 × 0.5/2 = 5.

What is the bandwidth of the circuit in the previous question?

B = R/L = 2/0.5 = 4 rad/s. The check ω0/Q = 20/5 = 4 agrees.

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original problems in the GATE style, not past exam questions. Try each one on paper first.

Reactance of an inductor

An inductor of 0.1 H is connected to a source with angular frequency 200 rad/s. Find its reactance in ohm.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The inductive reactance XL.
  2. Which idea applies. XL = ωL.
  3. Work it out. XL = 200 × 0.1 = 20 ohm.
  4. Check. Doubling the frequency would double the reactance, which fits the rule that inductors resist fast changes more at high frequency.

Answer: 20 ohm (the impedance is j20 ohm).

from fractions import Fraction as F
print("XL =", F(200) * F(1, 10), "ohm")
XL = 20 ohm

Resonant frequency of a simple LC pair

A series LC circuit has L = 1 H and C = 0.25 F. Find the resonant angular frequency in rad/s.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. ω0 in rad/s.
  2. Which idea applies. ω0 = 1/√(LC).
  3. Work it out. LC = 0.25. √0.25 = 0.5. ω0 = 1/0.5 = 2 rad/s.
  4. Check. Reactances at 2 rad/s: XL = 2 × 1 = 2 ohm, and XC = 1/(2 × 0.25) = 2 ohm. They are equal, as required.

Answer: 2 rad/s.

import math
print("w0 =", 1 / math.sqrt(1 * 0.25), "rad/s")
w0 = 2.0 rad/s

Statements about resonance (MSQ)

Select all that apply.

(A) At resonance, XL equals XC.
(B) The current from a series RLC source is largest at resonance.
(C) The quality factor of a series RLC is Q = R/(ω0L).
(D) The impedance of a parallel RLC is smallest at resonance.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. The resonance condition, the series current behaviour, and the Q formula.
  3. Work it out. (A) True, the definition. (B) True: Z is smallest, equal to R. (C) False: Q = ω0L/R, the reciprocal. (D) False: the parallel impedance is largest at resonance.
  4. Check. Test (C) with numbers: ω0 = 100, L = 0.02, R = 0.4 gives Q = 5, not 1/5.

Answer: (A) and (B).

Peak current at resonance (NAT)

A series RLC has R = 5 ohm, L = 0.1 H and C = 0.1 F. It is driven by a 10 V (peak) source at its resonant frequency. Find the peak current in ampere.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The peak current at resonance.
  2. Which idea applies. ω0 = 1/√(LC), then at resonance Z = R.
  3. Work it out. LC = 0.01, so ω0 = 10 rad/s. At that frequency XL = 10 × 0.1 = 1 ohm and XC = 1/(10 × 0.1) = 1 ohm, so Z = 5 ohm. Then I = 10/5 = 2 A.
  4. Check. The reactances cancel, so only the 5 ohm remains, as expected.

Answer: 2 A.

import math
w0 = 1 / math.sqrt(0.1 * 0.1)
print("w0 =", round(w0, 2), "rad/s")
print("peak current =", 10 / 5, "A")
w0 = 10.0 rad/s
peak current = 2.0 A

Quality factor of a series circuit (NAT)

A series RLC has R = 2 ohm, L = 0.5 H and ω0 = 20 rad/s. Find Q.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The quality factor Q.
  2. Which idea applies. Q = ω0L/R.
  3. Work it out. Q = 20 × 0.5/2 = 10/2 = 5.
  4. Check. Using Q = ω0/B with B = R/L = 4 rad/s gives 20/4 = 5. The same answer.

Answer: 5.

from fractions import Fraction as F
print("Q =", F(20) * F(1, 2) / 2)
Q = 5

Bandwidth of a series circuit (NAT)

A series RLC has R = 2 ohm and L = 0.5 H. Find the bandwidth in rad/s.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The bandwidth B.
  2. Which idea applies. B = R/L for a series RLC.
  3. Work it out. B = 2/0.5 = 4 rad/s.
  4. Check. Bandwidth is a frequency width, so its unit is rad/s, and it must be smaller than ω0 for a circuit with Q above 1. Here Q = 5 and ω0 = 20, so 20 is bigger than 4.

Answer: 4 rad/s.

from fractions import Fraction as F
print("B =", F(2) / F(1, 2), "rad/s")
B = 4 rad/s

Current magnitude off resonance (NAT)

A series circuit has R = 3 ohm, L = 0.01 H and C = 1/1200 F. It is driven by a 50 V (peak) source at ω = 200 rad/s. Find the magnitude of the current in ampere.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. |I| at the given frequency.
  2. Which idea applies. Find XL, XC, then Z, then I = V/Z.
  3. Work it out. XL = 200 × 0.01 = 2 ohm. XC = 1/(200 × 1/1200) = 1200/200 = 6 ohm. Z = 3 + j(2 − 6) = 3 − j4 ohm. |Z| = √(9 + 16) = 5 ohm. |I| = 50/5 = 10 A.
  4. Check. The circuit is capacitive, so the current leads the voltage. The magnitude does not depend on the phase, and the Python check confirms it.

Answer: 10 A.

import cmath
R, L, C, V, w = 3.0, 0.01, 1 / 1200, 50.0, 200.0
Z = R + 1j * w * L + 1 / (1j * w * C)
I = V / Z
print(f"|Z| = {abs(Z):.2f} ohm")
print(f"|I| = {abs(I):.2f} A")
|Z| = 5.00 ohm
|I| = 10.00 A

Statements about frequency response (MSQ)

Select all that apply.

(A) Below resonance, a series RLC is net capacitive.
(B) Increasing R in a series RLC increases Q.
(C) The bandwidth of a series RLC is R/L.
(D) The source current of a parallel RLC is smallest at resonance.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. The reactance comparison below resonance, the Q formula, the bandwidth formula, and parallel resonance.
  3. Work it out. (A) True: below ω0, XC is larger than XL. (B) False: Q = ω0L/R falls as R rises. (C) True. (D) True: the parallel impedance is largest, so the source current is smallest.
  4. Check. Test (A) with the Worked example 2 numbers at ω = 50: XC = 4 ohm is larger than XL = 1 ohm.

Answer: (A), (C) and (D).

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Series RLC impedance (NAT, 2 marks)

R = 30 Ω, L = 0.1 H, C = 100 µF in series at ω = 200 rad/s. Find |Z|.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The impedance magnitude.
  2. Which idea applies. XL = ωL, XC = 1/(ωC), |Z| = √(R² + (XL − XC)²).
  3. Work it out. XL = 20, XC = 50. |Z| = √(900 + 900) = 42.43 Ω.
  4. Check. The circuit is capacitive since XC > XL.
import math
R, L, C, w = 30, 0.1, 100e-6, 200
print("%.2f" % abs(complex(R, w * L - 1 / (w * C))))

42.43

Answer: about 42.43 Ω.

Exam-level 2: Resonance and Q (NAT, 2 marks)

For a series RLC with R = 10 Ω, L = 0.1 H and C = 10 µF, find the resonant frequency in rad/s and the quality factor.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. ω0 and Q.
  2. Which idea applies. ω0 = 1/√(LC). Q = ω0L/R.
  3. Work it out. LC = 10−6, ω0 = 1000 rad/s. Q = 100/10 = 10.
  4. Check. Bandwidth = ω0/Q = 100 rad/s = R/L.

Answer: 1000 rad/s and Q = 10.

Exam-level 3: Phasor sum (NAT, 2 marks)

Find the amplitude of v(t) = 3 cos(ωt) + 4 sin(ωt).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The amplitude of a single sinusoid.
  2. Which idea applies. As phasors: 3∠0° and 4∠−90°.
  3. Work it out. |3 − 4j| = 5.
  4. Check. The code samples the waveform.
import math
print("%.4f" % max(3 * math.cos(t / 1000) + 4 * math.sin(t / 1000) for t in range(7000)))

5.0000

Answer: 5.

Remember these

  1. In steady state, every element has an impedance, and circuits are solved with algebra.
  2. An inductor's voltage leads its current. A capacitor's voltage lags its current.
  3. At resonance, XL = XC, so the net reactance is zero.

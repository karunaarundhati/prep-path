Additive manufacturing

Additive manufacturing (AM) makes a part by adding material one thin layer at a time, directly from a 3D model. This page explains the basic principle, compares it with the removal of material in machining, describes the common processes (FDM, SLA, SLS and DMLS) in plain words, and sets out their advantages and limits for GATE conceptual questions.

The idea in plain words

Welcome. Most machined parts start as a big block and lose material until the final shape is left. A sculptor carving a statue works this way. Additive manufacturing works the other way round. It starts with nothing and builds the part up, one thin layer at a time, the way a wall is built from bricks. Each layer is a slice of the 3D model, and the machine lays down the next slice on top of the last one.

Think of a stack of paper sheets. Each sheet has a shape cut from it, and when you stack the sheets, you get a three-dimensional object with steps on the edges. Make the sheets thinner and the steps become smaller, and the object looks smoother. This is the central idea of additive manufacturing: a 3D shape is a stack of thin 2D shapes, and the machine makes each one in turn.

The term additive manufacturing covers many processes. People also use words such as 3D printing, rapid prototyping and direct digital manufacturing. In GATE questions the term AM is standard, and the key question is almost always "how is the layer formed?" The answer tells you the process.

Why does this matter for GATE? In the Machine Design and CIM part of the Robotics and Automation paper, AM questions are conceptual. They ask you to match a process to its principle, to say which process gives a metal part or a smooth surface, or to compare advantages and limits. There are few formulas, but the ideas must be clear. This page builds them from the ground up.

Key terms

Read this list once, and come back to it whenever a term feels new. Each entry gives the plain meaning first, then the precise one.

Additive manufacturing (AM)
Making a part by joining material layer by layer. Precisely: a process that builds a solid object from a 3D model by depositing, curing or fusing material in successive layers.
Subtractive manufacturing
Making a part by removing material from a solid block. Precisely: machining processes such as milling, turning and drilling, which cut away the excess.
Layer
One thin slice of the part. Precisely: a cross section of the 3D model, with a thickness set by the machine, usually 0.02 mm to 0.3 mm.
Layer thickness
The height of one layer. Precisely: the vertical distance the machine moves between one layer and the next.
STL file
A file that describes a surface as a mesh of triangles. Precisely: the common input format for AM, listing the triangles that enclose the part.
Slicing
Cutting the 3D model into layers. Precisely: the software step that converts the STL mesh into a stack of cross sections with machine paths.
Support structure
Temporary material that holds up overhangs. Precisely: extra material printed under sloping or hanging parts, removed after the build.
FDM (fused deposition modelling)
Material extrusion of a melted thermoplastic filament. Precisely: a nozzle deposits a bead of molten plastic that fuses to the layer below.
SLA (stereolithography)
Curing a liquid photopolymer with a light source. Precisely: a UV laser or projector hardens resin layer by layer in a vat.
Photopolymer
A liquid resin that hardens when light hits it. Precisely: a monomer mixture that polymerises under ultraviolet light.
SLS (selective laser sintering)
Fusing powder with a laser, without melting it fully. Precisely: a laser sinters powdered polymer particles together, one layer at a time, in a powder bed.
DMLS (direct metal laser sintering)
Laser fusion of metal powder into solid metal. Precisely: a powder bed process in which a laser fuses metal particles, layer by layer, into a dense part.
Powder bed
A bed of loose powder in which each layer is formed. Precisely: a layer of powder spread over the build platform, with the unfused powder supporting the part.
Anisotropy
Properties that depend on direction. Precisely: a part built layer by layer is often weaker across the layers than along them.

Additive versus subtractive manufacturing

The two families are opposites. Subtractive manufacturing starts with a solid blank larger than the part. A milling machine rotates a cutter and removes chips until the shape is left. Turning spins the workpiece against a tool. Drilling makes holes by removing a cylinder of material. In each case the machine takes material away, and the waste becomes chips.

Additive manufacturing starts with almost nothing and adds material only where the part needs it. A part with an internal channel, for example, is built with the channel already empty, because no material is placed there. In machining, the same channel would need a long drill or a special tool that might not reach it at all.

FeatureSubtractiveAdditive
Starting pointA solid blank, larger than the partNothing, or a powder bed
How the shape is madeMaterial is cut awayMaterial is added layer by layer
WasteChips and offcutsMostly unused powder, or support material
Complex internal shapesHard or impossibleNatural
Large numbers of identical partsEfficientUsually slower per part
Surface finishSmooth, preciseOften rougher, with layer lines

Key ideaSubtractive is best for accurate parts in large numbers. Additive is best for complex shapes, internal features and small batches.

Which kind of manufacturing is most natural for an internal cooling channel?

Additive manufacturing. The channel is simply left empty while the layers are built around it.

What happens to the waste in each family?

In subtractive manufacturing, the removed material becomes chips or offcuts. In additive manufacturing, the waste is mostly unused powder or the support material.

The layer principle and the STL file

Every AM process follows the same chain. First the part is designed in CAD as a solid model. The model is exported as an STL file, which describes the outside surface as a mesh of small triangles. The software then slices the mesh into horizontal layers. For each layer it produces the paths that the machine will follow, such as the path of a nozzle or of a laser spot. Finally the machine builds the layers, one on top of another.

  3D model (CAD) --> STL mesh --> slicing into layers --> machine paths
                                        |
            layer 1  ----  bottom
            layer 2  ----  next
              ...
            layer N  ----  top

The layer principle explains the main features of AM parts. Each layer is bonded to the one below, so the part is strongest within the layer and often weaker between layers. This is anisotropy. Layer lines are also visible on the surface, and they become more visible as the layer thickness grows.

The STL file has an important limit. It stores only triangles. It has no design intent, so the computer does not know that a hole was 10 mm, or that two faces were parallel. An STL file is therefore good for printing, but it is poor for editing the design.

TrapDo not describe the STL as the CAD model. It is a mesh of triangles that approximates the surface. A coarser mesh gives a rougher approximation of a curved part.

Why are AM parts often weaker across their layers?

Because the bond between layers is usually weaker than the material within a layer. Load that tries to pull the layers apart can cause failure at the joint between them.

What does slicing do?

It cuts the 3D model into horizontal cross sections and generates the machine paths for each one.

Material extrusion: fused deposition modelling (FDM)

FDM is the most familiar process, and many desktop printers use it. A spool of thermoplastic filament feeds into a heated nozzle. The plastic melts, and the nozzle moves in the plane of the layer, depositing a thin bead of molten plastic. The bead cools and bonds to the layer beneath. When one layer is complete, the platform drops by one layer thickness, and the next layer is deposited.

      filament spool
           |
           v
     [ heated nozzle ]  <-- moves in X and Y
           |
           v   bead of molten plastic
     ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~   current layer
     ==================   layer below
     ------------------   build platform (moves down each layer)

FDM is simple, cheap and clean. Its weak points are the visible layer lines, a relatively rough surface, and anisotropic strength. Overhangs need support material, which is usually a second filament that is removed after the build.

TipFor GATE, the key phrase for FDM is "melted filament extruded through a nozzle, layer by layer". If a question describes a heated nozzle and a plastic spool, the answer is FDM.

What is the feedstock for FDM?

A thermoplastic filament, fed from a spool into a heated nozzle.

Vat photopolymerisation: stereolithography (SLA)

SLA was one of the first AM processes. The build happens in a vat of liquid photopolymer resin. A UV laser or a projector shines on the surface of the resin and traces the cross section of the layer. Where the light hits, the resin hardens. The build platform then lifts or lowers by one layer thickness, a fresh film of liquid flows over the surface, and the next layer is cured.

   UV laser / projector
          |  (traces one layer)
          v
   ________________________
  | liquid resin (vat)     |
  |   [ part growing ]     |  platform moves in steps
  |________________________|

SLA gives a very smooth surface and fine detail, which is why it is used for jewellery patterns, dental models and detailed prototypes. The main limits are that the resin is brittle in many grades, the parts need post-curing, and the liquid must be handled carefully. Support structures are needed for overhangs.

GATE loves thisSLA is the answer whenever a question mentions a UV laser, a liquid photopolymer and a vat. The keyword is "cured", meaning hardened by light.

What does the laser do in SLA?

It traces each layer on the surface of the liquid resin, and the light hardens (cures) the resin where it lands.

Powder bed fusion: SLS, DMLS and SLM

Powder bed processes build each layer in a bed of loose powder. A roller or blade spreads a thin layer of powder over the platform. A laser then fuses the powder where the part is needed, and the unfused powder stays loose. The platform lowers by one layer, more powder is spread, and the next layer is fused. Because the loose powder surrounds the part, it supports overhangs, and the part is often built without separate support structures.

   powder roller  ->  spreads a thin layer of powder
   ______________________________________
  |  loose powder  (supports the part)   |
  |        [ fused layer ]               |  platform lowers each layer
  |______________________________________|
   laser fuses the powder where the part is

SLS, selective laser sintering, fuses polymer powder. The powder particles are heated until they stick together, and they are not fully melted. SLS parts are strong, and they need no separate support structures.

DMLS, direct metal laser sintering, applies the same powder bed idea to metal powder. The laser fuses the metal particles, layer by layer, into a dense solid. It is used for metal parts with complex internal geometry, such as aerospace brackets and heat exchangers. A similar process, selective laser melting (SLM), fully melts the metal powder rather than only sintering it.

TrapSLS sinters polymer, and DMLS sinters metal. If a GATE question asks for a metal part made by a laser on a powder bed, the answer is DMLS (or SLM), not SLS.

Why are overhangs easy in SLS and DMLS?

The surrounding loose powder holds up the overhang while the layers are built, so separate support structures are usually not needed.

What is the main difference between sintering and melting?

Sintering heats powder until particles bond without fully liquefying. Melting heats it past the melting point, so the material becomes a liquid pool before solidifying.

Advantages of additive manufacturing

Why would an engineer choose AM over machining? Four advantages come up again and again.

  • Complex geometry. Internal channels, lattices and organic shapes can be built directly, with no tools that must reach inside.
  • Rapid prototyping. A new design can be printed in hours, which shortens the design loop. This is the origin of the phrase rapid prototyping.
  • No tooling. There are no moulds or cutting fixtures for each design. A change in the model is a change in the file, so one-off or customised parts cost little extra.
  • Material efficiency. Little material is wasted. The part uses only what it needs, plus the support and unused powder.

Key ideaAM trades speed in volume production for freedom of shape, no tooling, and fast change.

Why does AM suit one-off custom parts?

Because it needs no tooling or moulds, so the cost of a single part does not depend on making a mould for it.

Limitations of additive manufacturing

AM is not a replacement for every process. Its limits are just as important for GATE questions.

  • Material properties. The choice of materials is narrower than in casting or forging. Layer bonding can make parts anisotropic, weaker across the layers.
  • Surface finish. Layer lines leave a rougher surface than a finished machined part. Post-processing such as sanding, machining or polishing is often needed.
  • Speed. Each layer takes time, so a large part or a large batch can be slow. Machining or moulding is often faster for high volume.
  • Size. Each machine has a limited build volume, so very large parts must be split or made another way.
  • Cost per part for volume. For large numbers of identical parts, the cost per part is usually higher than in mass production.

TrapDo not assume AM is always faster or always cheaper. It is faster for a single complex prototype, and it is usually slower and dearer than casting or moulding for a million identical parts.

Name two limitations of AM.

Any two of: rough surface finish, anisotropic strength, slow build for large parts, limited build volume, higher cost per part in high volume, and a narrower range of materials.

Build time and layer thickness

The time to build a part depends mainly on the number of layers and the time per layer. The number of layers is the height divided by the layer thickness.

Number of layers N = h/t
Build time T = N times tlayer

Here h is the height of the part in mm, t is the layer thickness in mm, and tlayer is the time to build one layer in seconds. The formula is a simplification, because the time per layer changes with the area of the layer and with the travel between features. For GATE, the simple form is usually enough.

Worked example. A part is 40 mm tall and is built with layers of 0.1 mm, taking 90 s per layer.

  1. Number of layers: N = 40/0.1 = 400.
  2. Build time: T = 400 times 90 = 36000 s.
  3. In hours: 36000/3600 = 10 hours.

Now halve the layer thickness to 0.05 mm, and suppose the time per layer rises to 100 s, because the machine moves more slowly for finer detail.

  1. Number of layers: N = 40/0.05 = 800.
  2. Build time: T = 800 times 100 = 80000 s, which is 22.2 hours.

Key ideaThinner layers give a smoother surface, but they multiply the number of layers. Build time rises, often more than in proportion.

TrapDo not forget that halving the thickness doubles the layers. Many students assume the build time stays the same if the layer time is unchanged, and they forget the extra layers.

A part is 30 mm tall with layers of 0.15 mm. How many layers?

N = 30/0.15 = 200 layers.

If the layer thickness is doubled and the time per layer is unchanged, what happens to the build time?

It is halved, because there are half as many layers.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original, written in the GATE style, and not copied from any real exam paper. Try the "How to think about it" steps yourself before reading the answer.

Identify the process

A machine spreads liquid resin in a vat and traces each layer with an ultraviolet light, so that the resin hardens where the light lands. Which process is this? (MCQ: (A) FDM, (B) SLA, (C) SLS, (D) DMLS.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The name of the process.
  2. Which idea applies. Liquid resin hardened by light is vat photopolymerisation, which is stereolithography.
  3. Work it out. FDM extrudes filament. SLS and DMLS fuse powder. Only SLA uses a liquid resin cured by light.
  4. Check. The keywords liquid, vat and ultraviolet light all match SLA.

Answer: (B), SLA.

Number of layers

A part is 30 mm tall and is built with layers of 0.15 mm. How many layers are needed? (NAT: enter a whole number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The number of layers N.
  2. Which idea applies. N = h/t.
  3. Work it out. N = 30/0.15 = 200.
  4. Check. 200 layers of 0.15 mm stack to 200 times 0.15 = 30 mm, the height of the part.

Answer: 200 layers.

Statements about AM processes

Which of the following statements about additive manufacturing processes are correct? Select all that apply. (MSQ)

  • (A) FDM extrudes melted thermoplastic filament through a heated nozzle.
  • (B) SLS fully melts the polymer powder before each layer is formed.
  • (C) DMLS can build dense metal parts from metal powder.
  • (D) An STL file stores the design intent of each feature in the part.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true.
  2. Which idea applies. The principle of FDM, the difference between sintering and melting, the purpose of DMLS, and the content of an STL file.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) False: SLS sinters, it does not fully melt. (C) True. (D) False: an STL stores triangles only.
  4. Check. Statements (B) and (D) each describe the wrong process or file content, so they are false.

Answer: (A) and (C) are correct.

Total build time for a part

A part 30 mm tall is built with 0.15 mm layers. Each layer takes 60 s. How long does the build take, in hours, to two decimal places? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The build time T in hours.
  2. Which idea applies. N = h/t, then T = N times tlayer.
  3. Work it out. N = 200. T = 200 times 60 = 12000 s. In hours, 12000/3600 = 3.33 hours.
  4. Check. The answer is a little over three hours, which is reasonable for 200 layers at one minute each.

Answer: 3.33 hours.

Mass of a printed block

A solid block of size 20 mm by 20 mm by 10 mm is printed in a plastic of density 1.2 g/cm3. Assume the block is solid throughout. What is its mass, in grams, to one decimal place? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The mass in grams.
  2. Which idea applies. Mass = volume times density, with units converted to cm.
  3. Work it out. Volume = 20 times 20 times 10 = 4000 mm3 = 4 cm3. Mass = 4 times 1.2 = 4.8 g.
  4. Check. A 4 cm3 block of material with density about 1 g/cm3 weighs about 4 g, so 4.8 g is consistent.

Answer: 4.8 g.

Build time when thickness is halved

A part 40 mm tall is built with 0.1 mm layers, taking 90 s per layer. The layer thickness is then halved to 0.05 mm, and the time per layer rises to 100 s. How many hours longer or shorter is the new build than the old? Give the difference in hours to one decimal place, with a positive sign if the new build is longer. (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The difference between the new and old build times.
  2. Which idea applies. T = (h/t) times tlayer for each case.
  3. Work it out. Old: 400 times 90 = 36000 s = 10.0 h. New: 800 times 100 = 80000 s = 22.2 h. Difference: 22.2 - 10.0 = 12.2 h.
  4. Check. The new build has twice the layers and a longer time per layer, so it must be more than twice as long. 22.2 is more than 20, which fits.

Answer: +12.2 hours (the new build is longer).

Choosing a process for a metal part

A designer needs a dense metal bracket with an internal cooling passage that cannot be machined. Statements are given below. Select all that apply. (MSQ)

  • (A) Use DMLS, because a laser fuses metal powder layer by layer and the passage is simply left unfused.
  • (B) Use FDM with a plastic filament, because it gives the best metal strength.
  • (C) Use SLA, because the photopolymer resin hardens into a metal.
  • (D) Use a powder bed process, because the loose powder can be removed from the passage after the build.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which process choices are sound for a dense metal part with an internal passage.
  2. Which idea applies. Powder bed metal processes build dense metal and allow internal passages. FDM and SLA make plastic, not metal.
  3. Work it out. (A) True: DMLS builds dense metal and the unfused powder is removed from the passage. (B) False: FDM is a plastic process. (C) False: SLA resin is a polymer. (D) True: the loose powder in a powder bed is removed from internal passages after the build.
  4. Check. The two correct statements both rely on the powder bed idea, which is the only one that gives dense metal with an internal passage.

Answer: (A) and (D) are correct.

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Build time (NAT, 2 marks)

A part 60 mm tall is printed with layer thickness 0.2 mm. Each layer takes 45 s. Find the build time in hours.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Layers times time per layer.
  2. Which idea applies. Number of layers = height/thickness.
  3. Work it out. 300 layers · 45 s = 13500 s = 3.75 h.
  4. Check. Halving the layer thickness doubles the time.

Answer: 3.75 h.

Exam-level 2: Stair stepping (NAT, 2 marks)

A flat surface inclined at 60° to the build plate is printed with layer thickness t = 0.1 mm. The cusp height is h = t cos θ, where θ is the angle between the surface normal and the build direction. Find h in mm.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The cusp height.
  2. Which idea applies. A surface tilted 60° from the plate has its normal tilted 60° from the vertical build direction, so θ = 60°.
  3. Work it out. h = 0.1 cos 60° = 0.05 mm.
  4. Check. Vertical walls (θ = 90°) have no cusp.

Answer: 0.05 mm.

Exam-level 3: AM processes (MSQ, 2 marks)

Which are TRUE? (A) SLA cures liquid resin with light. (B) FDM extrudes thermoplastic filament. (C) SLS fuses powder with a laser. (D) Binder jetting parts are always fully dense without post-processing.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. AM process facts.
  2. Which idea applies. Binder jetting glues powder, so parts need sintering or infiltration.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) True. (C) True. (D) False.
  4. Check. SLS needs no support structures, since the powder bed supports the part.

Answer: (A), (B) and (C).

Remember these

  1. Additive builds the part up layer by layer, subtractive cuts it down from a block.
  2. Match the process by its starting material and energy source: filament (FDM), resin with UV light (SLA), powder with laser (SLS or DMLS).
  3. SLS sinters polymer, and DMLS sinters or melts metal powder.
  4. Number of layers is the height divided by the layer thickness.
  5. Halving the layer thickness doubles the number of layers and so increases the build time.
  6. AM gives freedom of shape and no tooling, but it has limits in surface finish, speed and volume production cost.

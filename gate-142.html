Free-body diagrams and equilibrium

A free-body diagram shows every force on one body, and equilibrium equations say that the forces and moments balance. Together they let you find unknown supports and loads, which is the starting point for almost every mechanics question in GATE.

The idea in plain words

Suppose a shelf is fixed to a wall and holds a book. The shelf does not fall and does not spin. Something must be pushing and pulling on it to balance the weight of the book. The wall supplies those pushes and pulls. Engineers call the balance state equilibrium, and they call the wall's pushes reactions.

A free-body diagram (FBD) is a sketch of one body with everything that touches it replaced by arrows. The body is "freed" from its supports, and the supports are shown as forces. Once the sketch is drawn, you use the rules of equilibrium to find the unknown arrows.

Everyday analogy. Imagine a seesaw with a child on one end. To keep it level, a friend must push up on the other end, and the seesaw's pin pushes up on the middle. Draw each push as an arrow and you have a free-body diagram of the seesaw.

Why it matters. Every bridge, crane, robot arm and wall bracket must stay in equilibrium. Before you size a bolt or choose a motor, you must know the forces that act on each part. The FBD is how you find them.

Where it appears in GATE. In the Robotics and Automation (RA) paper, expect numerical questions that ask for unknown support reactions, cable tensions or member forces in a loaded body. Drawing the FBD first is the most reliable way to earn the marks.

Key terms

Force
A push or pull, measured in newtons (N). It has a size and a direction.
Moment (or torque) about a point
The turning effect of a force about a point. Precisely: M = F × d, where d is the perpendicular distance from the point to the line of action of the force.
Line of action
The straight line along which a force acts. Moving a force along its line of action does not change its turning effect about a point.
Rigid body
An object whose shape does not change when forces act on it. Its parts stay the same distance apart.
Free-body diagram (FBD)
A sketch of one body, isolated from its surroundings, showing all external forces and moments acting on it.
External force
A force from outside the body, such as weight, an applied load, or a support reaction.
Reaction
A force or moment that a support applies to the body to keep it in place.
Equilibrium
The state where the body does not accelerate in any direction and does not rotate. Precisely: the sum of forces is zero and the sum of moments is zero.
Statically determinate
A body whose unknown reactions can all be found from the equilibrium equations alone.
Pin support
A support that stops movement in two directions but lets the body rotate. It gives two reaction components.
Roller support
A support that stops movement in one direction only, perpendicular to its surface. It gives one reaction.
Fixed support
A support that stops movement and rotation. It gives two force components and one moment.
Distributed load
A load spread along a length, such as the weight of a beam or snow on a roof. It is measured in newtons per metre (N/m) or kN/m.
Resultant (of a distributed load)
A single equivalent force. For a uniform load it equals the intensity times the length, and it acts at the middle.
Component
The part of a force along the x or y direction. For a force F at angle θ to the x axis, Fx = F cos θ and Fy = F sin θ.
Sign convention
The rule for which direction is positive. In this page, rightwards and upwards forces are positive, and anticlockwise moments are positive.

Forces, moments and equilibrium

Step 1: forces add as vectors, so split them into parts

A force that points at an angle is hard to add to others. The trick is to split it into a horizontal part and a vertical part. Those two parts are the components, and they add like ordinary numbers.

Fx = F cos θ and Fy = F sin θ

Here θ is measured from the positive x axis (usually the horizontal, to the right). Use degrees on your calculator only if it is set to degrees.

import math

F = 10.0          # force size in newtons
theta = 30.0      # angle from the horizontal, in degrees

Fx = F * math.cos(math.radians(theta))
Fy = F * math.sin(math.radians(theta))
print("Fx = %.2f N" % Fx)
print("Fy = %.2f N" % Fy)
Fx = 8.66 N
Fy = 5.00 N

TrapPython's math functions use radians, not degrees. Always convert with math.radians first. Forgetting this gives an answer that looks plausible but is wrong.

Step 2: moments turn things

A force makes a body turn about a point when it does not pass through that point. The moment is the force times the perpendicular distance to the point. A long spanner gives a big moment for the same push, because the distance is large.

M = F × d

Here M is the moment in N·m, F is the force in newtons, and d is the perpendicular distance in metres from the point to the line of action of the force.

Step 3: the equilibrium conditions

A body in equilibrium obeys three rules in two dimensions:

ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0, ΣM = 0

The symbol Σ (sigma) means "the sum of". The first two say the body does not move sideways or up and down. The third says it does not spin about any point. You may take moments about any point you like, and the body will still be in balance.

Key ideaThree equations, three unknowns. For a single rigid body in 2D, you can find up to three unknown reactions. If there are more, the problem needs extra methods.

Question: a 20 N force acts at 60 degrees to the horizontal. What is its horizontal component?

Fx = 20 × cos 60° = 20 × 0.5 = 10 N.

Question: a 50 N force acts at a perpendicular distance of 0.4 m from a point. What is the moment?

M = 50 × 0.4 = 20 N·m.

Drawing a free-body diagram

The five steps

  1. Choose the body. Decide which part you want to study, such as a beam, a bracket or a whole frame.
  2. Isolate it. Imagine cutting it free from all its supports and neighbours. Draw only the body.
  3. Mark the loads. Draw each applied force, and the weight of the body at its centre of gravity. For a uniform beam, the weight is at the middle.
  4. Mark the reactions. At each support, draw the reactions allowed by the support type. Use the table in the next section.
  5. Label and set axes. Name every unknown with a symbol, choose x and y axes, and pick a sign convention.

What the sketch looks like

Here is a simple beam on two supports, with a point load of 12 kN. The FBD shows the beam alone, with the supports replaced by reaction arrows.

        12 kN (down)
            |
            v
   A  ____________________  B
   ^ R_A      6 m          ^ R_B
   |                       |
  (pin)                  (roller)

   The beam is isolated. R_A and R_B are the unknown reactions.
   The point load sits 2 m from A.

Notice that the beam's own weight is left out in this simple case. If it matters, add it as a distributed load.

TipDraw the FBD first, on paper, before you write any equation. Most marks are lost by missing a force, not by bad algebra.

Question: do you show the internal forces inside a member on the free-body diagram of the whole body?

No. Internal forces act between parts of the same body, so they cancel out when you look at the whole body. Only external forces appear on the whole-body FBD.

Question: where is the weight of a uniform beam drawn?

At its centre of gravity, which is at the middle of a uniform beam.

Support reactions

How each support pushes back

A support can only push back in the directions it blocks. Knowing this tells you how many unknowns to draw. Use this table in every problem.

SupportWhat it blocksReactions to drawCount
Roller (smooth surface)Movement perpendicular to the surfaceOne force, perpendicular to the surface1
Pin (hinge)Movement in x and y, but not rotationTwo forces, Rx and Ry, no moment2
Fixed (built-in, wall)Movement in x and y, and rotationTwo forces, Rx and Ry, and one moment M3
Cable or link (two-force member)Movement along the cableOne force along the cable (tension)1

Why the fixed support has a moment

A wall does more than push up. It also stops the beam from rotating. A beam fixed into a wall is held at both the force level and the moment level. If you forget the moment at a fixed end, the equations will be wrong.

TrapA pin has a sideways reaction Rx as an unknown, which may turn out to be zero. A roller has no sideways component at all. Students often draw a sideways force at a roller, which is wrong.

Question: how many reaction unknowns does a fixed support give?

Three: two force components (Rx and Ry) and one moment M.

Question: a beam is on one pin and one roller. How many unknowns are there?

Three: two at the pin (Rx and Ry) and one at the roller. This matches the three equilibrium equations.

Using the three equilibrium equations

The method

  1. Draw the FBD, with every load and reaction.
  2. Take moments about a support that has an unknown force through it. That unknown gives zero moment and drops out.
  3. Solve for the first unknown.
  4. Use ΣFy = 0 or ΣFx = 0 to find the rest.
  5. Check with a moment equation about a different point.

Worked example: a simply supported beam

A beam of span L = 6 m has a pin at A (left end) and a roller at B (right end). A point load P = 12 kN acts 2 m from A. Find the reactions RA and RB.

  1. Moments about A (anticlockwise positive). RA gives no moment because it acts at A. The load P gives a clockwise moment of 12 × 2 = 24 kN·m. RB gives an anticlockwise moment of RB × 6. So RB × 6 - 12 × 2 = 0.
  2. Solve: RB = 24 / 6 = 4 kN.
  3. Vertical forces: RA + RB - 12 = 0, so RA = 12 - 4 = 8 kN.
  4. Horizontal forces: there is no horizontal load, so Rx at A is zero.
L = 6.0        # span in metres
P = 12.0       # point load in kN
a = 2.0        # distance of the load from support A, in metres

# Moments about A, anticlockwise positive: R_B * L - P * a = 0
R_B = P * a / L
R_A = P - R_B

print("R_B = %.1f kN" % R_B)
print("R_A = %.1f kN" % R_A)
print("Check, sum of vertical forces = %.1f kN" % (R_A + R_B - P))
R_B = 4.0 kN
R_A = 8.0 kN
Check, sum of vertical forces = 0.0 kN

The check works because the vertical forces balance. Always do this check, since it catches sign mistakes.

Worked example: a uniform distributed load

The same span of 6 m now carries a uniform load of w = 2 kN/m along its full length. Find the reactions.

  1. Resultant load: W = w × L = 2 × 6 = 12 kN.
  2. The resultant acts at the middle of the span, 3 m from A.
  3. The beam is symmetric, so each support takes half: RA = RB = 12 / 2 = 6 kN.
w = 2.0        # load intensity in kN per metre
L = 6.0        # span in metres

W = w * L      # resultant load in kN, acting at mid span
R_A = W / 2
R_B = W / 2
print("Resultant load = %.1f kN at %.1f m from A" % (W, L / 2))
print("R_A = %.1f kN, R_B = %.1f kN" % (R_A, R_B))
Resultant load = 12.0 kN at 3.0 m from A
R_A = 6.0 kN, R_B = 6.0 kN
Question: a beam on pin and roller supports carries a point load at its midpoint. What is the moment equation about A?

The load P is at 3 m from A (for a 6 m beam), so RB × 6 - P × 3 = 0. Here RB = P / 2.

Question: why is it useful to take moments about a support?

The unknown reaction at that support has zero moment arm and drops out, so one equation can be solved for the other reaction directly.

Loads and sign conventions

A cantilever: a fixed support with a tip load

A cantilever is a beam fixed at one end and free at the other. It is common in balconies, robot arms and diving boards. Its fixed end must give a force and a moment, so there are three unknowns.

        P = 5 kN (down)
            |
            v
  ===========|----------------------------
  |  wall   |        3 m (free tip)
  ===========
   fixed end: R_y (up), M (moment)

Take the wall as the point for moments. The load P has a moment arm equal to the length L, so the moment at the wall is P × L. The vertical reaction is simply P, to balance the vertical load.

P = 5.0          # tip load in kN, downward
L = 3.0          # length of the cantilever in metres

R_y = P          # vertical reaction at the wall, upward
M_wall = P * L   # moment reaction at the wall in kN m

print("Vertical reaction = %.1f kN" % R_y)
print("Moment at the wall = %.1f kN m" % M_wall)
Vertical reaction = 5.0 kN
Moment at the wall = 15.0 kN m

TrapStudents often forget the moment at a fixed end. Without it, the moment equation does not balance. Remember: a fixed support always gives a moment unknown.

Sign conventions

A sign convention is a rule you agree to use. This page uses the following rules, and you must state yours in an exam:

  • Rightwards forces and upwards forces are positive.
  • Anticlockwise moments are positive.
  • A negative answer does not mean an error. It means the force points the other way from your arrow.

Key ideaDraw each unknown in the direction you guess. If the answer is negative, the force really points the opposite way. Never change the arrow after you have started the algebra.

Question: a reaction comes out as -3 kN. What does it mean?

The reaction acts opposite to the arrow you drew. A 3 kN force acts in the other direction.

Question: which way does a clockwise moment count under the convention above?

Negative, because only anticlockwise moments are positive.

Solved GATE-style problems

These are original problems in the GATE style. They are not real past paper questions.

Reactions of a symmetric beam

A simply supported beam of span 4 m carries a 10 kN point load at its midpoint. Find the reaction at each support.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. RA and RB in kN.
  2. Which idea applies. Symmetry, or the moment equation about A.
  3. Work it out. Moments about A: RB × 4 - 10 × 2 = 0, so RB = 5 kN. Vertical balance: RA = 10 - 5 = 5 kN.
  4. Check. The load is central and the beam is symmetric, so both reactions must be half the load, which is 5 kN. Good.
P = 10.0
L = 4.0
R_B = P * (L / 2) / L
R_A = P - R_B
print("R_A = %.1f kN" % R_A)
print("R_B = %.1f kN" % R_B)
R_A = 5.0 kN
R_B = 5.0 kN

Answer: 5 kN at each support.

Moment of a force

A 200 N force acts perpendicular to a lever arm at a distance of 0.3 m from a pivot. Find the moment about the pivot, in N·m.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The moment in N·m.
  2. Which idea applies. M = F × d, where d is the perpendicular distance.
  3. Work it out. M = 200 × 0.3 = 60 N·m.
  4. Check. The force is perpendicular, so the full distance counts. If the force were at 30 degrees, the moment would be smaller. Good.
F = 200.0
d = 0.3
print("Moment = %.1f N-m" % (F * d))
Moment = 60.0 N-m

Answer: 60 N·m.

Which statements about supports are true?

Consider the following statements about supports and free-body diagrams. Select all that apply.

(A) A pin support gives two reaction components.
(B) A roller support gives one reaction, perpendicular to its surface.
(C) A fixed support gives a moment reaction as well as force reactions.
(D) Internal forces between two parts of one body must be shown on the FBD of the whole body.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. The support table, and the rule that only external forces appear on a whole-body FBD.
  3. Work it out. (A) True, Rx and Ry. (B) True, perpendicular to the surface. (C) True, a fixed support blocks rotation, so it has a moment. (D) False, internal forces cancel and are not shown on the whole-body FBD.
  4. Check. Statement (D) would double count the forces, which is not how equilibrium works.

Answer: (A), (B) and (C). Statement (D) is false.

Reaction with an off-centre load

A simply supported beam has span 8 m. A 20 kN point load acts 3 m from support A. Find the reaction RB at the roller, in kN.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. RB in kN.
  2. Which idea applies. Moments about A: RB × L = P × a.
  3. Work it out. RB × 8 = 20 × 3 = 60, so RB = 60 / 8 = 7.5 kN.
  4. Check. The load is closer to A, so A should take more load, and RA = 20 - 7.5 = 12.5 kN is bigger than RB. Good.
L = 8.0
P = 20.0
a = 3.0
R_B = P * a / L
print("R_B = %.1f kN" % R_B)
R_B = 7.5 kN

Answer: 7.5 kN.

Beam with a uniform load and a point load

A simply supported beam of span 6 m carries a uniform load of 1.5 kN/m over its full length and a point load of 4 kN at its midpoint. Find the reaction at A, in kN.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. RA in kN.
  2. Which idea applies. Replace the uniform load by its resultant, then the beam is symmetric.
  3. Work it out. Resultant of the uniform load = 1.5 × 6 = 9 kN at mid span. Total load = 9 + 4 = 13 kN at mid span. By symmetry, RA = 13 / 2 = 6.5 kN.
  4. Check. RA + RB = 6.5 + 6.5 = 13 kN, equal to the total load. Good.
w = 1.5
L = 6.0
P = 4.0
total = w * L + P
R_A = total / 2
print("R_A = %.1f kN" % R_A)
R_A = 6.5 kN

Answer: 6.5 kN.

Cable tension in a hinged rod

A uniform horizontal rod AB is 4 m long. It is hinged at A (a pin support) and carries a 50 N load at its midpoint. The rod is held by a cable attached at B. The cable makes an angle of 30 degrees above the horizontal, and it pulls B up and to the left. Find the tension T in the cable, in newtons.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The cable tension T in N.
  2. Which idea applies. Split T into components. Take moments about the pin A, which removes the pin reactions from the equation.
  3. Work it out. The vertical part of the cable force is T sin 30° = 0.5T, acting upward at B. Its moment about A is 0.5T × 4. The horizontal part of the cable lies along the rod line, so it gives no moment about A. The 50 N load at 2 m from A gives a clockwise moment of 50 × 2 = 100 N·m. So 0.5T × 4 = 100, which gives T = 50 N.
  4. Check. The vertical component is 0.5 × 50 = 25 N, and the rod load is 50 N, so the pin must supply 25 N upward. This is positive and reasonable.
import math

L = 4.0
load = 50.0
a = 2.0
theta = math.radians(30.0)

# Moments about A: T * sin(theta) * L = load * a
T = load * a / (math.sin(theta) * L)
print("Tension = %.2f N" % T)
print("Vertical part of tension = %.2f N" % (T * math.sin(theta)))
Tension = 50.00 N
Vertical part of tension = 25.00 N

Answer: T = 50 N.

Moment at the wall of a loaded cantilever

A cantilever of length 2 m is fixed at one end. It carries a uniform load of 3 kN/m over its full length, and a point load of 4 kN at its free tip. Find the moment at the fixed end, in kN·m.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The fixed-end moment in kN·m.
  2. Which idea applies. Replace the uniform load with its resultant at its middle, then take moments about the wall.
  3. Work it out. Resultant of the uniform load: W = 3 × 2 = 6 kN, acting at 1 m from the wall. Its moment about the wall is 6 × 1 = 6 kN·m. The tip load gives 4 × 2 = 8 kN·m. Total moment = 6 + 8 = 14 kN·m.
  4. Check. Both loads act downward on the same side of the wall, so their moments add. Good.
w = 3.0
L = 2.0
P = 4.0
W = w * L
M_wall = W * (L / 2) + P * L
print("Moment at the wall = %.1f kN m" % M_wall)
Moment at the wall = 14.0 kN m

Answer: 14 kN·m.

Choose the correct statements about equilibrium

A rigid body is in equilibrium under coplanar forces. Select all that apply.

(A) ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0 and ΣM = 0 are all needed for a body in 2D equilibrium.
(B) The moment equation can be taken about any point, and the result is the same.
(C) A rigid body in 2D with four unknown reactions can be solved using only the three equilibrium equations.
(D) Changing the sign convention changes the physical reaction forces.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. The three equilibrium equations, the freedom to choose the point for moments, and the meaning of sign conventions.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) True, if the body is in equilibrium, all moment sums are zero about any point. (C) False. Four unknowns and three equations leave one unknown undetermined, so the body is statically indeterminate. (D) False, a sign convention changes only how the signs are written, not the physical force.
  4. Check. A sign convention is only a bookkeeping choice, so statement (D) cannot be right.

Answer: (A) and (B). Statements (C) and (D) are false.

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Two cable tension (NAT, 2 marks)

A 100 N weight hangs from two cables making 30° and 60° with the horizontal. Find the tension in the 60° cable, correct to 2 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The tension T2.
  2. Which idea applies. Horizontal: T1cos 30° = T2cos 60°. Vertical: T1sin 30° + T2sin 60° = 100.
  3. Work it out. The two cables are 90° apart, so Lami's theorem gives T2/sin 120° = 100/sin 90°. So T2 = 86.60 N, and in the same way T1 = 100 sin 150° = 50 N.
  4. Check. The code solves both equations.
import math
c30, s30, c60, s60 = math.cos(math.pi/6), math.sin(math.pi/6), math.cos(math.pi/3), math.sin(math.pi/3)
T2 = 100 / (s30 * c60 / c30 + s60); T1 = T2 * c60 / c30
print("T1 = %.2f, T2 = %.2f" % (T1, T2))

T1 = 50.00, T2 = 86.60

Answer: about 86.60 N.

Exam-level 2: Beam reactions (NAT, 2 marks)

A simply supported beam of span 6 m carries 12 kN at 2 m from the left support and 6 kN at 5 m. Find the left reaction.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. RA.
  2. Which idea applies. Moments about the right support.
  3. Work it out. 6RA = 12 · 4 + 6 · 1 = 54, so RA = 9 kN.
  4. Check. RB = 18 − 9 = 9 kN.

Answer: 9 kN.

Exam-level 3: Equilibrium facts (MSQ, 2 marks)

Which are TRUE? (A) A two force member in equilibrium carries equal, opposite, collinear forces. (B) Three non-parallel forces in equilibrium are concurrent. (C) A planar rigid body has three independent equilibrium equations. (D) A free body diagram includes internal forces between parts of the body.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Rules of statics.
  2. Which idea applies. Internal forces cancel in pairs and do not appear on the FBD of the whole body.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) True. (C) True. (D) False.
  4. Check. Cut the body to expose internal forces.

Answer: (A), (B) and (C).

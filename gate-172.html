Castigliano's theorems

Castigliano's theorems let you find how far a loaded structure moves, and how a statically indeterminate structure shares its loads, using one idea: the energy stored inside a deformed member. They are a frequent GATE tool, and this page builds them from the energy idea one small step at a time.

The idea in plain words

Welcome. Imagine you are pulling a rubber band. The harder you pull, the more it stretches, and the more energy you store in it. When you let go, that stored energy returns as motion. Almost every elastic material works this way. A steel beam that bends under a load stores energy in its bent shape, just as a bent bow stores energy in its curve.

Castigliano's theorem uses that stored energy as a shortcut. It says: if you know how the stored energy depends on a load, then the rate at which the energy changes with that load tells you how far the structure moves at that load. You do not need to solve a differential equation for the whole beam. You need one energy integral and one derivative.

Here is an everyday picture. Suppose you know the total cost of a shopping trip as a function of how many apples you buy. The change in cost for one more apple is the rate of change. Castigliano works the same way. The stored energy is the "cost", the load is the "number of apples", and the displacement is the "rate".

Why does this matter for GATE? Both Robotics and Automation (RA) and the mechanics part of the syllabus ask you to find deflections and rotations of beams, frames and arms. Castigliano's theorem is one of the fastest ways to do this, and it also handles statically indeterminate structures, where equilibrium alone is not enough. GATE questions often give a beam with a load and ask for the deflection under the load or at a point with no load. The method on this page answers both kinds of question.

The plan: first we learn how to write strain energy. Then we state Castigliano's first theorem and use it on a cantilever. Then we handle points with no load using a dummy load. Finally we apply the theorem to an indeterminate beam.

Key terms

Read this list now, and come back whenever a word feels unfamiliar. Each entry gives the plain meaning first, then the precise definition.

Deflection (delta)
How far a point moves from its original position. Plain meaning: how far the beam sags or moves sideways. Precise definition: the displacement of a point on the structure, measured in mm.
Rotation (theta)
How much a cross-section turns. Plain meaning: how much the beam tilts at a point. Precise definition: the angle through which the cross-section rotates, in radians.
Strain energy (U)
The energy stored in an elastic body because it is deformed. Plain meaning: the energy you could get back if you let go. Precise definition: the work done by the internal stresses during deformation, U, in N mm (or N m).
Elastic material
A material that returns to its original shape when the load is removed. Plain meaning: it springs back. Precise definition: a material whose stress depends only on strain, with no permanent set.
Linear elastic
Elastic and obeying Hooke's law, so stress is proportional to strain. Plain meaning: the stretch is proportional to the pull. Precise definition: the stress is a linear function of strain, sigma = E × epsilon.
Bending moment (M)
The turning effect of the loads on a cross-section of a beam. Plain meaning: the force that tries to bend the beam at that point. Precise definition: the sum of the moments of all forces on one side of the section about the section.
Second moment of area (I)
A measure of how the cross-section resists bending. Plain meaning: the stiffness of the shape in bending. Precise definition: I = integral of y² dA, in mm⁴.
EI (flexural rigidity)
The bending stiffness of the beam. Plain meaning: the product of material stiffness and shape stiffness. Precise definition: E times I, in N mm².
Partial derivative (∂)
A derivative of a function with respect to one variable while the others are held constant. Plain meaning: how the result changes when you change just one input. Precise definition: ∂U / ∂P is the rate of change of U with respect to P, with all other variables fixed.
Castigliano's first theorem
For a linear elastic structure, the displacement at a load's point of application, in the load's direction, equals the partial derivative of strain energy with respect to that load. Plain meaning: energy tells you how far things move. Precise definition: deltai = ∂U / ∂Pi.
Dummy load (Q)
An imaginary load added at a point where there is no real load, so that its derivative gives the deflection there. Plain meaning: a pretend load that you set to zero at the end. Precise definition: an auxiliary force added to the structure, differentiated, and then set to zero.
Statically determinate structure
A structure whose reactions can be found from equilibrium alone. Plain meaning: the forces are known just from balance. Precise definition: a structure with exactly as many unknown reactions as independent equilibrium equations.
Statically indeterminate structure
A structure with more unknown reactions than equilibrium equations. Plain meaning: you need an extra rule about how the structure moves. Precise definition: a structure with redundant constraints, which require compatibility to solve.
Redundant
A reaction that could be removed without making the structure unstable. Plain meaning: an extra support that is not strictly needed for balance. Precise definition: an unknown reaction in an indeterminate structure, chosen as the unknown in Castigliano's method.
Compatibility
The rule that the deformed structure must fit together, with supports where they are. Plain meaning: nothing moves where a support holds it. Precise definition: displacements at supports are equal to their prescribed values, usually zero.
Cantilever
A beam fixed at one end and free at the other. Plain meaning: a diving board. Precise definition: a beam with a fixed support at one end and no support at the other.
Simply supported beam
A beam resting on two supports, free to rotate at both. Plain meaning: a plank on two bricks. Precise definition: a beam with pinned or roller supports at both ends.
Propped cantilever
A beam fixed at one end and supported (not fixed) at the other. Plain meaning: a cantilever with a prop under the free end. Precise definition: an indeterminate beam with one fixed support and one simple support.
Uniformly distributed load (w)
A load spread evenly along the length. Plain meaning: a load per unit length, like the weight of the beam itself. Precise definition: a load of intensity w, in N/mm, acting along the whole length.

Concept 1: Strain energy, the energy stored in a bent beam

Start with a simple spring. A spring with stiffness k stores energy U = ½ k x² when stretched by x. The energy is half the force times the stretch, because the force grows steadily from zero to its final value. A beam is a continuous spring: every small piece stores a little energy. To find the total energy, we add up the small pieces, which is an integral along the beam.

The energy stored in a beam in bending, for a linear elastic material, is:

U = integral from 0 to L of M² / (2 E I) dx

Let us say what each symbol means. U is the strain energy, in N mm. M is the bending moment at a position x along the beam, in N mm. E is Young's modulus, in MPa. I is the second moment of area, in mm⁴. x is the distance along the beam, in mm. L is the length of the beam. The integral adds the energy of every small slice dx of the beam.

Notice that M is squared, so the sign of the moment does not matter. Bending in one direction or the other stores the same energy.

For the other kinds of member, the same idea gives:

  • Axial load: U = integral of N² / (2 A E) dx, where N is the axial force and A the area.
  • Torsion: U = integral of T² / (2 G J) dx, where T is the torque, G the shear modulus and J the polar second moment of area.

For GATE, the bending formula is by far the most common. Here is a quick example of an energy integral done by hand, so you can see every step.

Worked example. A beam has a moment M = 2 N mm along its whole length L = 3 mm. Its EI = 4 N mm². Find U.

  1. Write the formula: U = ∫ M² / (2EI) dx.
  2. Since M is constant, M² = 4, and it comes out of the integral.
  3. The integral of dx from 0 to 3 is just 3.
  4. So U = (4 × 3) / (2 × 4) = 12 / 8 = 1.5 N mm.
from fractions import Fraction as F
M = 2
EI = 4
L = 3
U = F(M**2 * L, 2 * EI)
print("U =", U, "N mm")

U = 3/2 N mm

TrapForgetting the factor ½ in the energy formula. It is always U = integral of M² / (2EI) dx. Missing the half doubles every deflection you compute, and this error is very easy to make.

Question 1: Why is the sign of M irrelevant to the strain energy?

Because the energy formula uses M squared. A moment that bends the beam up stores the same energy as one that bends it down.

Question 2: What does the integral add up?

The energy stored in every small slice of the beam, from one end to the other.

Concept 2: Castigliano's first theorem

Now the main idea. Castigliano's first theorem says that for a linear elastic structure, the deflection at a point, in the direction of a load P at that point, is:

delta = ∂U / ∂P

The symbol delta is the deflection at the point where P acts, in mm, in the same direction as P. The symbol U is the total strain energy written in terms of P. The symbol ∂ means a partial derivative: we differentiate U with respect to P, treating all other loads as constant.

For a rotation at a point where a moment M acts, the same rule gives the rotation: theta = ∂U / ∂M. This is useful when a question asks for the slope of a beam at a support.

Worked example: tip deflection of a cantilever. A cantilever of length L has a tip load P. Its flexural rigidity is EI, constant along the beam. Find the tip deflection.

First, the layout. Use x measured from the free tip, so x = 0 at the load and x = L at the fixed support. The text picture is below.

  x = 0 at the tip                         x = L at the fixed end

     P (downward)
     |
     v
     ============================================  ###
     <---------------- L ---------------------->  ###  fixed wall

  Bending moment at a section x from the tip: M(x) = P × x
  1. Bending moment: at a section a distance x from the tip, only the load P is on that side, at arm length x. So M = P x.
  2. Energy: U = ∫ from 0 to L of (P x)² / (2 E I) dx = (P² / 2EI) ∫ from 0 to L of x² dx.
  3. The integral of x² from 0 to L is L³ / 3.
  4. So U = (P² / 2EI) × L³ / 3 = P² L³ / (6 E I).
  5. Differentiate with respect to P: ∂U / ∂P = 2 P L³ / (6 E I) = P L³ / (3 E I).
  6. So the tip deflection is delta = P L³ / (3 E I). This is the standard result.
from fractions import Fraction as F
# U = P^2 L^3 / (6 E I). Derivative wrt P is P L^3 / (3 E I).
# Check with P = 2, L = 3, E = 1, I = 1 (all in consistent units):
P, L, E, I = 2, 3, 1, 1
dU_dP = F(2 * P * L**3, 6 * E * I)
print("dU/dP =", dU_dP)
print("P L^3 / (3 E I) =", F(P * L**3, 3 * E * I))

dU/dP = 18
P L^3 / (3 E I) = 18

Key ideaThe energy is written in terms of the load first, then differentiated. The derivative is taken before any numbers are put in for the load. In practice, that means keeping P as a symbol until the last line.

Question 3: For a cantilever with P = 1 kN, L = 1 m, E = 200 GPa and I = 107 mm⁴, what is the tip deflection?

Use consistent units: P = 1000 N, L = 1000 mm, E = 200 000 MPa, I = 107 mm⁴. Then delta = 1000 × 109 / (3 × 200 000 × 107) = 1012 / (6 × 1012) = 0.1667 mm.

Question 4: What is the rotation at the tip of the same cantilever?

Add a moment M at the tip. The moment at a section is M + P x, so the energy gets a cross term. Differentiating with respect to M and setting M = 0 gives theta = P L² / (2 E I). It is a separate result, shown here only to illustrate the rotation rule.

Concept 3: The dummy load trick for points with no load

Castigliano's first theorem needs a load at the point where you want the deflection. But many questions ask for the deflection at a point where no load acts, such as the middle of a beam with only an end load. The fix is simple: add an imaginary load, called a dummy load Q, at that point. Then compute the energy with both P and Q, differentiate with respect to Q, and set Q = 0 at the end. The derivative gives the deflection at the point.

Why does setting Q = 0 work? Because the derivative is evaluated at the real structure, which has Q = 0. The dummy load is only a tool to pick out the deflection at its own point.

Here is the procedure in a list, which is the safe way to tackle any dummy load problem.

  1. Place the dummy load Q at the point where the deflection is wanted, in the direction wanted.
  2. Write the bending moment M(x) in terms of P and Q.
  3. Form U = ∫ M² / (2EI) dx.
  4. Differentiate: ∂U / ∂Q = ∫ M × (∂M / ∂Q) dx / (EI), which uses the rule that the derivative of M² / 2 is M times the derivative of M.
  5. Set Q = 0 everywhere, then integrate. The result is the deflection at the point.

Worked example: uniform load on a cantilever. A cantilever of length L carries a uniform load w per unit length along its whole span. Find the tip deflection. This is a classic case that uses the dummy load method, because it is easy to get the moments right with a dummy load at the tip.

  Uniform load w per unit length along the whole span,
  dummy load Q at the tip (x = 0 at the tip)

     Q
     v
  vvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvvv   <- w on every part
  ============================================  ###
  <---------------- L ---------------------->  ###  fixed wall

  Moment at section x from the tip (x measured from the tip):
  M(x) = w x² / 2 + Q x
  1. Moment: M(x) = w x² / 2 + Q x. The uniform load on the length x gives w x times the arm x / 2, which is w x² / 2. The dummy load gives Q x.
  2. Differentiate with respect to Q: ∂M / ∂Q = x.
  3. So ∂U / ∂Q = (1/EI) ∫ from 0 to L of M × x dx.
  4. Set Q = 0: M = w x² / 2, so ∂U / ∂Q = (1/EI) ∫ from 0 to L of (w x² / 2) × x dx = (w / 2EI) ∫ from 0 to L of x³ dx.
  5. The integral of x³ from 0 to L is L⁴ / 4. So ∂U / ∂Q = (w / 2EI) × L⁴ / 4 = w L⁴ / (8 E I).
  6. So the tip deflection is delta = w L⁴ / (8 E I).
from fractions import Fraction as F
L = 2
integral = F(L**4, 4)              # integral of x^3 from 0 to L is L^4 / 4
deflection = F(1, 2) * integral    # (w / 2EI) times the integral, with w = EI = 1
print("integral of x^3 from 0 to 2 =", integral)
print("deflection with w = EI = 1 is", deflection)

integral of x^3 from 0 to 2 = 4
deflection with w = EI = 1 is 2

TipWrite the moment with the dummy load before doing anything else. The derivative ∂M / ∂Q is often just the distance from the point, so it is quick to find once M is clear.

TrapDifferentiating after setting Q = 0 gives zero, not the answer. The dummy load must stay in the energy until after you differentiate. Only then set Q = 0.

Question 5: Why does the dummy load get set to zero at the end?

Because the real structure has no dummy load. The derivative picks out the deflection at the point, and then Q is put back to zero to represent the real case.

Question 6: What is the tip deflection of a cantilever of length 2 m with w = 1 kN/m, E = 200 GPa and I = 108 mm⁴?

Use w = 1 N/mm, L = 2000 mm, E = 200 000 MPa, I = 108 mm⁴. delta = w L⁴ / (8 E I) = 1 × 1.6 × 1013 / (8 × 200 000 × 108) = 1.6 × 1013 / 1.6 × 1014 = 0.1 mm.

Concept 4: Castigliano for statically indeterminate structures

A structure is statically indeterminate when there are more unknown reactions than equilibrium equations. For example, a beam fixed at one end and supported by a roller at the other has three reaction unknowns: the vertical reaction and the moment at the fixed end, and the vertical reaction at the roller. For vertical loads there are only two useful equilibrium equations, the sum of vertical forces and the sum of moments. The extra unknown is the redundant. Equilibrium cannot find it, so we need a compatibility rule: the support does not move.

Castigliano provides that rule in a neat form. Choose the redundant reaction R as the unknown. Write the energy U in terms of R and the known loads. The support displacement is zero, so the derivative of U with respect to R must be zero:

∂U / ∂R = 0

This is sometimes called Castigliano's second theorem. It is the same idea as the first theorem: the displacement at the redundant point, in the direction of R, is zero, and that displacement equals ∂U / ∂R. Setting it to zero gives an equation for R.

Here is the set-up for a standard indeterminate beam: a beam fixed at the left end A and supported by a roller at the right end B, with a point load P at distance a from A. We take the reaction at B as the redundant R.

  Fixed at A (x = 0)                         Roller at B (x = L)
  ###  ----------------------- P (at x = a) -----------------  roller
  ###  |<-------- a -------->|                                 ^
  ###  |<------------------------- L -----------------------> ^ R (redundant)

  Moment at a section x measured from A, using the load and R on the right side:
  for x < a:   M(x) = R (L - x) - P (a - x)
  for x > a:   M(x) = R (L - x)

Now the energy U depends on R, and the condition ∂U / ∂R = 0 gives:

R × ∫ from 0 to L of (L - x)² dx = P × ∫ from 0 to a of (a - x)(L - x) dx

The first integral is L³ / 3. The second integral needs care, and we evaluate it for a = L / 2, which is the midspan load case. For L = 1 and a = 1/2, the integral of (1/2 − x)(1 − x) from 0 to 1/2 is 5/48. So R × (1/3) = P × (5/48), which gives R = 5P / 16.

The answer says the roller at B takes 5P/16 of the load. The fixed support takes the rest, so the vertical reaction at A is P − 5P/16 = 11P/16. The fixed-end moment at A equals 3PL / 16. These results are the standard values for a propped cantilever with a midspan load, and the Python check below confirms the integral numerically.

L = 1.0
a = 0.5
n = 200000
dx = L / n
s_r = 0.0
s_p = 0.0
for i in range(n):
    x = (i + 0.5) * dx
    s_r += (L - x) ** 2 * dx
    if x < a:
        s_p += (a - x) * (L - x) * dx
print("R_B / P =", round(s_p / s_r, 4))

R_B / P = 0.3125

GATE loves thisIn an indeterminate question, the first move is always to count the unknowns and the equations. The number of extra unknowns is the degree of indeterminacy. Castigliano needs exactly one ∂U / ∂R = 0 equation for each redundant.

TrapUsing ∂U / ∂R = 0 for a determinate structure. If the structure is determinate, there is no redundant, and the reactions come from equilibrium. The method is only for indeterminate structures.

Question 7: Why is ∂U / ∂R set to zero for the redundant support?

Because the support does not move. The displacement at the redundant point, in the direction of R, is zero, and by Castigliano that displacement equals ∂U / ∂R.

Question 8: For the propped cantilever with midspan load P, what is the fixed-end moment?

3PL / 16. Taking moments about A: the midspan load gives P × L/2, and the roller reaction gives 5P/16 × L. The fixed-end moment balances the difference: PL/2 − 5PL/16 = 3PL/16.

Concept 5: A routine for deflection numericals

Most GATE deflection problems follow the same route. Use this checklist every time.

  1. Sketch the beam and mark the loads. Decide the point where the deflection is wanted.
  2. Decide if a dummy load is needed. If the point has a real load, use it directly. If not, add a dummy load there.
  3. Write M(x). Use a clear origin, and include the dummy load if you have one.
  4. Form U and differentiate. Use U = ∫ M² / (2EI) dx, and take the partial derivative with respect to the load of interest.
  5. Set the dummy load to zero and integrate. Keep units consistent, usually N and mm.
  6. State the sign. A positive result means the point moves in the direction of the load.

Worked example. A simply supported beam of span 4 m has a central load P = 10 kN. Take E = 200 GPa and I = 108 mm⁴. Find the midspan deflection. The standard result is δ = P L³ / (48 E I). We check it with the routine.

  1. Use the half-beam from the support to the midspan, with the load at the middle. The reaction is P/2, so M = (P/2) x for 0 ≤ x ≤ L/2.
  2. By symmetry, U = 2 × ∫ from 0 to L/2 of (P x/2)² / (2EI) dx = P² L³ / (96 E I).
  3. Differentiate: ∂U / ∂P = P L³ / (48 E I). This is the midspan deflection.
  4. Substitute: P = 10 000 N, L = 4000 mm, E I = 200 000 × 108 = 2 × 1013 N mm². So δ = 10 000 × 6.4 × 1010 / (48 × 2 × 1013) = 6.4 × 1014 / 9.6 × 1014 = 0.6667 mm.
P = 10000       # N
L = 4000        # mm
E = 200000      # MPa
I = 1e8         # mm^4
delta = P * L**3 / (48 * E * I)
print("midspan deflection =", round(delta, 4), "mm")

midspan deflection = 0.6667 mm

TrapForgetting the factor 2 from symmetry. The energy in the whole beam is twice the energy in one half, because the two halves carry the same moments. Missing this factor doubles the energy and gives the wrong answer.

Question 9: Is the midspan deflection of a simply supported beam with a central load larger or smaller than a cantilever of the same length with a tip load?

Smaller. The simply supported case gives P L³ / (48 E I), while the cantilever gives P L³ / (3 E I), which is 16 times larger for the same load and length.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original GATE-style questions written for practice. They are not past exam questions. Try each one on paper before reading the steps.

Statements about strain energy

Which of the following statements about strain energy in a linear elastic beam are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) Strain energy in bending is proportional to M squared.
(B) Reversing the sign of the bending moment changes the strain energy.
(C) Strain energy increases when EI increases, for the same moment.
(D) Strain energy is always negative.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct. This is an MSQ.
  2. Which idea applies. U = ∫ M² / (2EI) dx, which is positive and depends on M squared.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true, because M appears squared. (B) is false, because M is squared, so the sign does not matter. (C) is false, because EI is in the denominator, so a larger EI gives less energy for the same moment. (D) is false, because energy stored in an elastic body is positive.
  4. Check. Only one statement is true. Stiffer beams store less energy for the same moment, which matches the formula.

Answer: (A) only.

Tip deflection of a cantilever

A cantilever of length 1.5 m carries a tip load of 2 kN. Take E = 200 GPa and I = 8 × 106 mm⁴. Find the tip deflection, in mm, to two decimal places. This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The tip deflection in mm.
  2. Which idea applies. Castigliano with M = P x gives δ = P L³ / (3EI).
  3. Work it out. P = 2000 N, L = 1500 mm, E = 200 000 MPa, I = 8 × 106 mm⁴. L³ = 3.375 × 109. Numerator: 2000 × 3.375 × 109 = 6.75 × 1012. Denominator: 3 × 200 000 × 8 × 106 = 4.8 × 1012. Divide: 6.75 / 4.8 = 1.40625 mm, which is 1.41 mm to two decimal places.
  4. Check. A deflection of about 1.4 mm for a 1.5 m steel cantilever under 2 kN is a sensible size.

Answer: 1.41 mm.

Midspan deflection of a simply supported beam

A simply supported steel beam of span 4 m carries a central load of 10 kN. Take E = 200 GPa and I = 108 mm⁴. Find the midspan deflection, in mm, to four decimal places. This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The midspan deflection in mm.
  2. Which idea applies. δ = P L³ / (48 E I) from Castigliano with symmetry.
  3. Work it out. Numerator: 10 000 × 4000³ = 10 000 × 6.4 × 1010 = 6.4 × 1014. Denominator: 48 × 200 000 × 108 = 9.6 × 1014. Divide: 6.4 / 9.6 = 0.6667 mm.
  4. Check. The same beam as a cantilever would deflect 16 times more, which is about 10.7 mm, showing the simply supported case is much stiffer. This matches the concept.

Answer: 0.6667 mm.

Tip deflection under a uniform load

A cantilever of length 1000 mm carries a uniform load of 2 N/mm along its whole length. Take EI = 1012 N mm². Find the tip deflection, in mm. This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The tip deflection in mm.
  2. Which idea applies. The dummy load result δ = w L⁴ / (8 E I).
  3. Work it out. w = 2 N/mm, L⁴ = 1012, so w L⁴ = 2 × 1012. Denominator: 8 × 1012. Divide: 2 / 8 = 0.25 mm.
  4. Check. The uniform load is 2000 N in total, and a tip load of 2000 N would give a larger deflection of P L³ / (3EI) = 2000 × 109 / (3 × 1012) = 0.667 mm. A uniform load gives a smaller tip deflection than the same total load at the tip, which is expected.

Answer: 0.25 mm.

Which expressions are correct

For a cantilever of length L with a tip load P and constant EI, which of the following are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) The tip deflection is P L³ / (3EI).
(B) The deflection at the midspan is P L³ / (3EI).
(C) The strain energy is P² L³ / (6EI).
(D) The tip deflection is P L³ / (48EI).

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which expressions are correct. This is an MSQ.
  2. Which idea applies. The energy and deflection of a cantilever with tip load.
  3. Work it out. (A) is the standard tip deflection. (B) is wrong, because the midspan deflection is smaller: 5PL³ / (48EI). (C) is the energy we derived: U = P² L³ / (6EI). (D) is the simply supported midspan result, not the cantilever tip.
  4. Check. Differentiate (C) with respect to P: you get P L³ / (3EI), which agrees with (A).

Answer: (A) and (C) only.

Reaction at the roller of a propped cantilever

A beam of span L is fixed at its left end A and rests on a roller at its right end B. A point load P = 16 kN acts at the midspan. Using Castigliano's method with the roller reaction as the redundant, find the reaction at B, in kN. This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The reaction R at B in kN.
  2. Which idea applies. ∂U / ∂R = 0, with M(x) = R (L − x) − P (a − x) for x < a, where a = L/2.
  3. Work it out. The condition gives R × L³/3 = P × ∫ from 0 to a of (a − x)(L − x) dx. For L = 1 and a = 1/2, the integral is 5/48, so R × 1/3 = P × 5/48, and R = 5P/16 = 5 × 16 / 16 = 5 kN.
  4. Check. R is less than half of P, which is reasonable for a beam fixed at one end: the fixed end takes the larger share. The reaction at A is 11 kN, and 5 + 11 = 16 kN, which balances the load.

Answer: 5 kN.

Deflection at a point with no load

A cantilever of length L carries a tip load P and has constant EI. Use a dummy load method to find the deflection at the midspan, which is at distance L/2 from the fixed end. Which of the following is the correct expression? Select one.

(A) P L³ / (3EI)
(B) 5 P L³ / (48 EI)
(C) P L³ / (48 EI)
(D) 5 P L³ / (3 EI)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The midspan deflection of the cantilever.
  2. Which idea applies. A dummy load Q at the midspan, then ∂U / ∂Q at Q = 0.
  3. Work it out. Let x be measured from the tip. For x between 0 and L/2, M = P x. For x between L/2 and L, M = P x + Q (x − L/2). Then ∂M / ∂Q = x − L/2 in the second region, which is zero in the first. So ∂U / ∂Q = (1/EI) ∫ from L/2 to L of P x (x − L/2) dx. The integral is P L³ × 5/48. So the midspan deflection is 5 P L³ / (48 EI), which is option (B).
  4. Check. The midspan point is closer to the wall, so it moves less than the tip, which is P L³ / (3EI). Option (B) is smaller than (A), which fits.

Answer: (B) 5 P L³ / (48 EI).

Statements about Castigliano's theorems

Which of the following statements about Castigliano's theorems are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) Castigliano's first theorem gives the deflection at a point as the partial derivative of strain energy with respect to the load at that point.
(B) For an indeterminate structure, the derivative of strain energy with respect to a redundant reaction is set to zero.
(C) The method applies to non-linear materials without modification.
(D) A dummy load is set to zero only before the integration is carried out.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct. This is an MSQ.
  2. Which idea applies. The statements of the first theorem, the redundant condition, and the dummy load procedure.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true, by the first theorem. (B) is true, because the redundant displacement is zero. (C) is false, because the theorem assumes linear elastic behaviour. (D) is false, because Q must be set to zero only after differentiation, not before; setting it to zero earlier gives zero for the derivative.
  4. Check. Two statements are correct, and each false one breaks a stated condition: linear elasticity, and the order of differentiation and setting to zero.

Answer: (A) and (B) only.

Strain energy of a constant moment

A beam has a constant bending moment M = 3 N mm along its length L = 4 mm. Its EI = 6 N mm². Find the strain energy, in N mm, as a fraction in lowest terms. This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. U as a fraction in N mm.
  2. Which idea applies. U = ∫ M² / (2EI) dx with constant M.
  3. Work it out. M² = 9, so U = M² × L / (2EI) = 9 × 4 / (2 × 6) = 36 / 12 = 3 N mm.
  4. Check. Using the same formula with M = 2 and L = 3 gave 1.5 earlier; here the moment is larger, and the energy is larger, as expected.

Answer: 3 N mm (which is 3/1).

Summary

Strain energy
Energy stored by elastic deformation. Precise definition: U = ∫ M² / (2EI) dx in bending.
Castigliano's first theorem
Deflection equals the derivative of energy with respect to the load. Precise definition: δ = ∂U / ∂P.
Rotation rule
Rotation equals the derivative of energy with respect to the moment. Precise definition: θ = ∂U / ∂M.
Dummy load
A pretend load used to find a deflection at a point with no real load. Precise definition: differentiate with respect to Q, then set Q = 0.
Redundant
An unknown reaction that equilibrium cannot find. Precise definition: the unknown chosen for the compatibility equation.
Statically indeterminate
More unknowns than equilibrium equations. Precise definition: solved using compatibility.
Statically determinate
Solved by equilibrium alone. Precise definition: no redundant reactions.
Compatibility condition
The support does not move. Precise definition: ∂U / ∂R = 0 for a redundant R.
Flexural rigidity
Bending stiffness. Precise definition: EI.
Cantilever tip deflection
Standard result for a tip load. Precise definition: P L³ / (3EI).
Uniform load cantilever
Standard tip deflection. Precise definition: w L⁴ / (8EI).
Simply supported midspan deflection
Standard result for a centre load. Precise definition: P L³ / (48EI).
Propped cantilever reaction
Roller reaction for a midspan load. Precise definition: 5P / 16.
Linear elastic
Stress proportional to strain. Precise definition: the assumption under which Castigliano applies.
Bending moment
Turning effect on a section. Precise definition: M(x) at position x.

Remember these

  1. Strain energy in bending is U = ∫ M² / (2EI) dx, with the factor ½.
  2. Castigliano's first theorem: deflection at a load equals ∂U / ∂P.
  3. Differentiate before setting the dummy load to zero.
  4. For a redundant reaction R, use ∂U / ∂R = 0.
  5. The cantilever tip result is P L³ / (3EI).
  6. The simply supported centre result is P L³ / (48EI).
  7. The method assumes linear elastic material and small deflections.

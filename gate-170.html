Thermal stresses

When a material gets hotter it wants to grow, and if something stops it from growing, a stress builds up inside it. This topic shows how to calculate that thermal stress, including for two different materials bolted together, which is a favourite GATE numerical.

The idea in plain words

Welcome. Start with a simple experience. A metal lid on a hot jar is easier to open after you run it under warm water, because the metal grows slightly. A railway track on a hot summer day can buckle, because the steel wants to grow and has nowhere to go. Both are the same physics. When a material is heated, it tends to get longer. If it is free to grow, it simply grows, and nothing bad happens inside it. If it is held at both ends, it cannot grow, and it pushes back on its supports. That push is a stress.

Here is the everyday picture to hold onto. Imagine a child on a swing who wants to stretch out their legs, but two friends are holding their hands tight. The child cannot stretch, so the friends feel a strong pull. The child is squeezed by the friends' grip. In a constrained bar, the bar is the child, the supports are the friends, and the thermal stress is the pull or push that the supports feel.

Why does this matter for GATE? In Robotics and Automation (RA) papers, robot frames, precision gantries and bearings are all made of materials that heat up in use. A stiff frame that cannot expand may buckle or develop stress. GATE RA questions usually ask you to compute the thermal stress in a rod that is fixed between two walls, or the stress in a bar made of two materials that are joined together. These questions are very direct once you know the steps. Our job on this page is to make those steps automatic.

The plan has three parts. First we define thermal expansion and see what happens when a bar is free. Second we define thermal strain and see what happens when the bar is held. Third we use the result on a composite bar with two different materials. Each part has a picture, a formula, and worked numbers shown line by line.

Key terms

Read this list now. Come back to it when a word feels unfamiliar. Each entry starts with the plain meaning, then gives the precise definition.

Temperature change (delta T)
How much hotter or colder the material becomes. Plain meaning: the number of degrees it warmed up. Precise definition: delta T = T_final minus T_initial, in degrees Celsius or kelvin. A temperature rise is positive.
Thermal expansion
The growth of a material when it is heated. Plain meaning: hot things get bigger. Precise definition: the change in dimension caused by a change in temperature, with no external load.
Coefficient of linear thermal expansion (alpha)
How much a material grows per unit length for each degree of temperature rise. Plain meaning: the growth rate of the material. Precise definition: alpha = (change in length per unit length) / (change in temperature), with units per degree Celsius (per degree C) or per kelvin.
Free expansion
Growth that is not restrained by anything. Plain meaning: the bar is allowed to grow. Precise definition: a bar with no support that resists its change in length, so no stress develops.
Constrained bar
A bar held so that its length cannot change. Plain meaning: a bar held tight at both ends. Precise definition: a bar whose total change in length is prevented by rigid supports.
Thermal strain
The strain a material would have if it were free to expand. Plain meaning: the stretch that heat would give on its own. Precise definition: epsilon_T = alpha × delta T, a dimensionless ratio.
Mechanical strain
The strain caused by stress, not by heat. Plain meaning: the squeeze or stretch that the forces produce. Precise definition: the strain that remains after the free thermal strain is accounted for.
Strain
Change in length divided by original length. Plain meaning: how much a bar has stretched or squeezed, as a fraction. Precise definition: epsilon = delta L / L, which has no units.
Stress
Force divided by area. Plain meaning: how hard the material is pushed inside, per unit area. Precise definition: sigma = F / A, in N/mm² (MPa) or N/m² (Pa).
Young's modulus (E)
A measure of how stiff a material is. Plain meaning: how much force it takes to stretch it a little. Precise definition: the ratio of stress to strain in the linear elastic range, E = sigma / epsilon, in GPa or MPa.
Compressive stress
A stress that squeezes the material. Plain meaning: a push. Precise definition: a stress that shortens the material, taken as negative in a sign convention where tension is positive.
Tensile stress
A stress that pulls the material apart. Plain meaning: a pull. Precise definition: a stress that lengthens the material, taken as positive.
Composite bar
A bar made of two or more different materials joined together. Plain meaning: two materials stuck side by side and moving as one. Precise definition: a set of members, each with its own material properties, that are rigidly connected so they must share the same change in length.
Compatibility
The rule that joined parts must have the same displacement at the joint. Plain meaning: the pieces stay connected. Precise definition: the displacements of connected members at a common point are equal.
Equilibrium
The state where all forces balance. Plain meaning: nothing is pushing the whole thing somewhere. Precise definition: the sum of all external and internal forces on a free body is zero.
Statically indeterminate
A problem where the equilibrium equations alone are not enough to find all the forces. Plain meaning: you need an extra rule about how things move. Precise definition: a structure with more unknown forces than independent equilibrium equations, solved using compatibility.

Concept 1: Free expansion, where nothing goes wrong

Let us start with the simplest case. Take a steel bar of length L, lay it on a table, and heat it so that its temperature rises by delta T. The bar grows. Its new length is larger than L by a small amount. The growth is given by a simple rule:

delta L = alpha × L × delta T

Let us say what each symbol means. delta L is the change in length, in mm. alpha is the coefficient of thermal expansion, which tells us how much the material grows per degree. For structural steel it is about 12 × 10-6 per degree Celsius. L is the original length, in mm. delta T is the temperature rise, in degrees Celsius.

Now a small number example. Take L = 1000 mm, alpha = 12 × 10-6 per degree C, and delta T = 50 degrees C.

  1. Write the rule: delta L = alpha × L × delta T.
  2. Multiply alpha by L: 12 × 10-6 × 1000 = 0.012.
  3. Multiply by delta T: 0.012 × 50 = 0.6.
  4. So delta L = 0.6 mm.

The bar grows by 0.6 mm. Nothing pushes against it, so there is no stress. This is the key point of the whole topic: a free bar grows and carries no stress. The growth is simply a change in shape, like a balloon that gets bigger when you blow into it, with no pressure from a wall.

Key ideaThermal expansion alone does not create stress. Stress appears only when the expansion is prevented. The question is always: is the bar free to grow, or is it held?

Question 1: A free aluminium rod of length 2000 mm is heated by 30 degrees C. Its alpha is 23 × 10-6 per degree C. How much does it grow?

delta L = 23 × 10-6 × 2000 × 30 = 0.046 × 30 = 1.38 mm.

Question 2: Does a free bar carry any stress when it is heated?

No. It grows freely, so no internal force is needed to keep it at its original length. Stress needs a constraint.

Concept 2: The constrained bar and thermal strain

Now hold the bar. Imagine the same steel bar fixed between two rigid walls, with the walls not allowed to move. The bar is heated, but it cannot grow. Here is a text picture of the set-up before and after heating.

  Before heating (temperature T0):

  |<------------- L ------------->|
  |===============================|
  wall                           wall          bar fixed at both ends


  After heating by delta T (walls do not move):

  |<------------- L ------------->|
  |===============================|
  wall      bar wants to grow      wall        but cannot, so it is squeezed
                                               and pushes on both walls

The bar "wants" to grow by delta L = alpha × L × delta T, which is called the free thermal expansion. But the walls keep its length the same. So the total change in length is zero. We can split the bar's behaviour into two parts that add up to zero:

  • The first part is the thermal strain, the strain the heat would produce on its own: epsilonT = alpha × delta T.
  • The second part is the mechanical strain, caused by the stress from the walls. It must cancel the first part exactly, so epsilonmechanical = minus alpha × delta T.

Notice what this means. The total strain is epsilontotal = epsilonT + epsilonmechanical = alpha × delta T minus alpha × delta T = 0. The bar is held at its original length, exactly as required.

The mechanical strain is caused by stress through Hooke's law, sigma = E × epsilon. So the mechanical strain is epsilon = sigma / E. Setting this equal to minus alpha × delta T gives the stress. That is the subject of the next concept.

TipThink of thermal strain as the bar's "wish" to grow, and mechanical strain as the walls' refusal. In a fully constrained bar the refusal exactly cancels the wish, so the total strain is zero.

Question 3: What is the total strain in a bar that is fully fixed between rigid walls and heated?

Zero. The free thermal strain alpha × delta T is cancelled by an equal and opposite mechanical strain, so the length does not change.

Question 4: Is the mechanical strain in a heated, fully constrained bar tensile or compressive?

Compressive. The bar wants to grow, so the walls must push inwards on it to hold it short. A heated, constrained bar is squeezed.

Concept 3: The thermal stress formula sigma = E × alpha × delta T

Now we put the pieces together. The mechanical strain must equal minus alpha × delta T. Hooke's law says the mechanical strain is sigma / E. So:

sigma / E = minus alpha × delta T, so sigma = minus E × alpha × delta T

Let us say what each symbol means. sigma is the thermal stress, in MPa (N/mm²). E is Young's modulus, in MPa. alpha is the thermal expansion coefficient, per degree C. delta T is the temperature rise, in degrees C. The minus sign tells us the stress is compressive when the bar is heated, which matches our reasoning in Concept 2.

For GATE, the magnitude is what matters most. In most questions the answer is quoted as the size of the stress, and you state whether it is compression or tension. So we use:

|sigma| = E × alpha × delta T

Worked example. A steel bar is fixed between rigid walls. Its E = 200 GPa, alpha = 12 × 10-6 per degree C, and the temperature rises by 40 degrees C. Find the thermal stress.

  1. Convert E to MPa: 200 GPa = 200 000 MPa, because 1 GPa = 1000 MPa.
  2. Write the formula: sigma = E × alpha × delta T.
  3. Multiply E by alpha: 200 000 × 12 × 10-6 = 2.4.
  4. Multiply by delta T: 2.4 × 40 = 96.
  5. So the stress is 96 MPa, and because the bar is heated, it is compressive.
E = 200000      # MPa (200 GPa)
alpha = 12e-6   # per degree C
dT = 40         # degrees C
sigma = E * alpha * dT
print("stress =", round(sigma, 2), "MPa")

stress = 96.0 MPa

Now find the force on the walls, which is often asked in GATE. Force equals stress times area. If the bar is 20 mm in diameter, its area is pi × d² / 4 = pi × 400 / 4 = 314.16 mm². The force is F = 96 × 314.16 = 30 159 N, which is about 30.16 kN. Here is the check.

import math
sigma = 96.0    # MPa
d = 20          # mm
A = math.pi * d**2 / 4
F = sigma * A   # N
print("area =", round(A, 2), "mm^2")
print("force =", round(F / 1000, 2), "kN")

area = 314.16 mm^2
force = 30.16 kN

TrapStress does not depend on the length of the bar or on its area. A long bar and a short bar of the same steel, heated by the same delta T, have the same stress. Only the total force depends on area. Many wrong answers come from multiplying by length.

TrapUnits. E is often given in GPa and must be changed to MPa before you multiply, because the stress must come out in MPa. Forgetting the factor of 1000 gives an answer that is 1000 times too small.

Question 5: A copper bar with E = 100 GPa and alpha = 17 × 10-6 per degree C is constrained and heated by 20 degrees C. What is the stress?

E = 100 000 MPa. sigma = 100 000 × 17 × 10-6 × 20 = 1.7 × 20 = 34 MPa, compressive.

Question 6: If the same steel bar is cooled by 40 degrees C instead of heated, what is the stress?

The magnitude is still 96 MPa, but the bar is now in tension, because it wants to shrink and the walls pull it back.

Concept 4: Composite bars of two different materials

Now the more interesting case. Two different materials are joined together, for example a steel bar and a copper bar, side by side, both rigidly attached to a common end plate. Each material has its own alpha and its own E. When the pair is heated, each one wants to grow by a different amount. Because they are joined, they must stay together, and that forces stress into both.

Here is the set-up. The two bars have the same length L. Their left ends are fixed to a wall. Their right ends are both attached to one rigid plate, which is free to move, and no outside force acts on the plate.

  wall |==== STEEL bar (E_s, A_s, alpha_s) ====|
       |                                       |== rigid plate (free to move)
  wall |==== COPPER bar (E_c, A_c, alpha_c) ===|

  Both bars have length L. Both are joined to the same plate.
  The plate is the only thing that connects them, so both bars must
  change length by the same amount: delta_s = delta_c = delta.

We need three rules to solve this. Each rule is a simple idea.

  1. Equilibrium. The plate has no outside force, so the internal forces in the two bars must cancel. If the steel is in tension with force F, the copper is in compression with force F. So Fsteel = F and Fcopper = minus F.
  2. Compatibility. Both bars attach to the same plate, so they both change length by the same amount delta. This is the rule that ties the two bars together.
  3. Force-deformation. Each bar's change in length is the free thermal growth plus the growth caused by its own force: delta = alpha × L × delta T + F × L / (A × E).

Write the change in length for each bar, using the force with its sign:

delta = alphas × L × delta T + F × L / (As × Es)
delta = alphac × L × delta T minus F × L / (Ac × Ec)

The minus sign on the copper line appears because the copper carries the opposite force, minus F. Now set the two expressions equal, which is the compatibility rule. Collect the terms with F on one side and the thermal terms on the other:

F × ( 1 / (As Es) + 1 / (Ac Ec) ) = (alphac minus alphas) × delta T

Note that L cancels out, because it appears in every term. So the force does not depend on the length. Then the stress in each bar is its force divided by its own area.

Why the copper ends up in compression. Copper has the larger alpha. Heating makes it want to grow more than the steel. The steel holds it back, so the copper is squeezed and the steel is stretched. That is why the steel is in tension and the copper is in compression.

Key ideaIn a composite bar, the material with the larger alpha ends up in compression, and the material with the smaller alpha ends up in tension. The stiffer material (larger E × A) carries the larger share of the force.

GATE loves thisA composite bar question usually gives E, A and alpha for each material and asks for the stress in one of them. Write the three rules first, then solve. Do not try to recall a single formula, because the set-up changes from question to question.

Question 7: Why does L cancel out of the force formula for a composite bar with a free plate?

Because L multiplies every term. The thermal terms are alpha × L × delta T, and the force terms are F × L / (A × E). Dividing the whole equation by L leaves a relation with no length in it.

Question 8: In a steel and copper composite, which material is in compression when the pair is heated?

Copper, because it has the larger alpha. It wants to grow more than the steel and is held back by it.

Concept 5: Solving a composite numerical step by step

Let us now solve the composite bar from Concept 4 with numbers. The steel and copper bars each have area A = 1000 mm², and each has length L = 1000 mm. The data are:

  • Steel: Es = 200 000 MPa, alphas = 12 × 10-6 per degree C.
  • Copper: Ec = 100 000 MPa, alphac = 17 × 10-6 per degree C.
  • Temperature rise delta T = 50 degrees C.

Step 1, the stiffness terms. Find 1 / (A E) for each bar.

  • Steel: A × E = 1000 × 200 000 = 2 × 108 N, so 1 / (A E) = 5 × 10-9.
  • Copper: A × E = 1000 × 100 000 = 1 × 108 N, so 1 / (A E) = 1 × 10-8.
  • Sum: 5 × 10-9 + 1 × 10-8 = 1.5 × 10-8.

Step 2, the thermal difference. alphac minus alphas = 17 × 10-6 minus 12 × 10-6 = 5 × 10-6 per degree C. Multiply by delta T = 50, which gives 2.5 × 10-4.

Step 3, the force. F = 2.5 × 10-4 / 1.5 × 10-8 = 16 667 N, about 16.67 kN. The steel is in tension with this force.

Step 4, the stresses. Steel stress = F / A = 16 667 / 1000 = 16.67 MPa, tension. Copper stress = minus F / A = minus 16.67 MPa, compression.

Step 5, the check on the change in length. The steel change in length is alphas × L × delta T + F × L / (A Es) = 0.6 + 0.0833 = 0.6833 mm. The copper change in length is alphac × L × delta T minus F × L / (A Ec) = 0.85 minus 0.1667 = 0.6833 mm. The two agree, so compatibility holds.

E_s, A_s, a_s = 200000, 1000, 12e-6
E_c, A_c, a_c = 100000, 1000, 17e-6
L = 1000
dT = 50
F = (a_c - a_s) * dT / (1 / (A_s * E_s) + 1 / (A_c * E_c))
print("force in steel =", round(F, 2), "N")
print("steel stress =", round(F / A_s, 2), "MPa")
print("copper stress =", round(-F / A_c, 2), "MPa")
d_s = a_s * dT * L + F * L / (A_s * E_s)
d_c = a_c * dT * L - F * L / (A_c * E_c)
print("steel change in length =", round(d_s, 4), "mm")
print("copper change in length =", round(d_c, 4), "mm")

force in steel = 16666.67 N
steel stress = 16.67 MPa
copper stress = -16.67 MPa
steel change in length = 0.6833 mm
copper change in length = 0.6833 mm

TrapForgetting that the copper force is minus F. If you add the two forces instead of cancelling them, equilibrium fails. Always check that the forces in a free plate sum to zero.

Question 9: In the example above, why do the two changes in length come out equal?

Because the force F was found from the compatibility rule, which says the two bars must have the same change in length. The check confirms that the rule was applied correctly.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original GATE-style questions written for practice. They are not past exam questions. Try each one on paper before reading the steps.

Stress in a heated, fixed rod

A steel rod is fixed between two rigid walls. Its E = 200 GPa and alpha = 11 × 10-6 per degree C. The temperature rises by 30 degrees C. What is the magnitude of the thermal stress, in MPa? This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The stress magnitude in MPa for a fully constrained rod.
  2. Which idea applies. sigma = E × alpha × delta T, because the rod is fixed at both ends.
  3. Work it out. E = 200 000 MPa. alpha × delta T = 11 × 10-6 × 30 = 3.3 × 10-4. Multiply: 200 000 × 3.3 × 10-4 = 66.
  4. Check. Compare with the earlier example: alpha × delta T was 4.8 × 10-4 there, giving 96 MPa. A smaller strain gives a smaller stress, so 66 is reasonable.

Answer: 66 MPa (compressive, because the rod is heated).

Growth of a free bar

A free brass bar of length 500 mm is heated by 40 degrees C. Its alpha is 19 × 10-6 per degree C. Which of the following is correct? Select all that apply.

(A) The bar grows by 0.38 mm.
(B) The bar carries a compressive stress.
(C) The bar carries no stress.
(D) The bar grows by 3.8 mm.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct. This is an MSQ.
  2. Which idea applies. Free expansion: delta L = alpha × L × delta T, and no stress.
  3. Work it out. delta L = 19 × 10-6 × 500 × 40 = 19 × 10-6 × 20 000 = 0.38 mm. So (A) is true and (D) is false. The bar is free, so there is no stress, which makes (B) false and (C) true.
  4. Check. Ten times 0.38 is 3.8, so (D) is the value if a factor of 10 is wrongly added. Keep the decimal place in mind.

Answer: (A) and (C) only.

Force on the wall

A copper rod of diameter 16 mm is fixed between two rigid walls. E = 110 GPa and alpha = 17 × 10-6 per degree C. The rod is cooled by 25 degrees C. Find the magnitude of the force that the rod exerts on each wall, in kN. Use pi = 3.1416. This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The force in kN, from a thermal stress.
  2. Which idea applies. Stress first from sigma = E × alpha × delta T, then force from F = sigma × A.
  3. Work it out. Stress: 110 000 × 17 × 10-6 × 25 = 1.87 × 25 = 46.75 MPa. Area: pi × 16² / 4 = 3.1416 × 256 / 4 = 3.1416 × 64 = 201.06 mm². Force: 46.75 × 201.06 = 9400 N, which is 9.40 kN.
  4. Check. Cooling makes the rod want to shrink, so it is in tension, and it pulls on the walls. The magnitude is about 9.4 kN, a plausible size for a 16 mm rod.

Answer: about 9.40 kN (tensile, pulling on each wall).

Which bar is in compression

Two bars with equal areas are joined to one rigid plate that is free to move. Bar P has alpha = 23 × 10-6 per degree C. Bar Q has alpha = 12 × 10-6 per degree C. The assembly is heated. Which statements are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) Bar P is in compression.
(B) Bar Q is in compression.
(C) Bar P is in tension.
(D) Bar Q is in tension.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The sign of the stress in each bar. This is an MSQ.
  2. Which idea applies. In a composite bar, the material with the larger alpha is compressed, and the one with the smaller alpha is stretched.
  3. Work it out. Bar P has the larger alpha (23 against 12). So P is compressed and Q is stretched. That makes (A) true, (B) false, (C) false and (D) true.
  4. Check. Equilibrium: the two forces must be equal and opposite. One compression and one tension, so the signs are opposite, as expected.

Answer: (A) and (D) only.

Stress in a composite bar

A steel bar and an aluminium bar, each of area 800 mm², are attached to one rigid plate that is free to move. Both have length 1000 mm. For steel, E = 200 000 MPa and alpha = 12 × 10-6 per degree C. For aluminium, E = 70 000 MPa and alpha = 23 × 10-6 per degree C. The temperature rises by 20 degrees C. Find the tensile stress in the steel, in MPa, to two decimal places. This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The tensile stress in the steel, in MPa.
  2. Which idea applies. The composite formula: F × (1/(AsEs) + 1/(AcEc)) = (alphac minus alphas) × delta T, where c here means aluminium.
  3. Work it out. A E for steel = 800 × 200 000 = 1.6 × 108, so 1/(AE) = 6.25 × 10-9. A E for aluminium = 800 × 70 000 = 5.6 × 107, so 1/(AE) = 1.7857 × 10-8. Sum = 2.4107 × 10-8. Thermal difference = (23 − 12) × 10-6 × 20 = 2.2 × 10-4. Force F = 2.2 × 10-4 / 2.4107 × 10-8 = 9126 N. Steel stress = 9126 / 800 = 11.41 MPa.
  4. Check. The aluminium is the more flexible, and it wants to grow more, so the steel is stretched. Tension in steel is the expected sign. A Python check is in the block below.

Answer: 11.41 MPa (tension).

Statements about constrained and composite bars

Which of the following statements about thermal stresses are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) A fully constrained bar heated by delta T has a compressive stress of E × alpha × delta T.
(B) In a composite bar with a free plate, the force depends on the length of the bars.
(C) If a bar is free to expand, its stress is zero but its strain is alpha × delta T.
(D) Cooling a fully constrained bar produces tension.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct. This is an MSQ, so test each one.
  2. Which idea applies. Constrained bars give stress E × alpha × delta T. Free bars give strain without stress. Composite forces do not depend on L.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true, using the sign convention where heating gives compression. (B) is false, because L cancels out of the composite force equation. (C) is true: free thermal strain is alpha × delta T, and the stress is zero. (D) is true, because cooling makes the bar want to shrink, so it is pulled into tension.
  4. Check. Each true statement matches a rule from the concepts above. The false statement, (B), contradicts the cancellation of L.

Answer: (A), (C) and (D) only.

Steel and copper with a different plate condition

A steel bar and a copper bar, each of area 1000 mm² and length 1000 mm, are attached to a rigid plate that is free to move. Steel has E = 200 000 MPa and alpha = 12 × 10-6 per degree C. Copper has E = 100 000 MPa and alpha = 17 × 10-6 per degree C. The temperature rises by 50 degrees C. Find the magnitude of the change in length of the plate, in mm, to four decimal places. This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The change in length delta of the plate, which equals the change in length of each bar.
  2. Which idea applies. Compatibility: both bars have the same change in length. Use the steel expression.
  3. Work it out. From the worked numbers, the steel force is F = 16 666.67 N. Steel change in length = alphas × L × delta T + F × L / (A Es) = 12 × 10-6 × 1000 × 50 + 16 666.67 × 1000 / (1000 × 200 000) = 0.6 + 0.08333 = 0.68333 mm.
  4. Check. The copper expression gives 0.85 minus 0.16667 = 0.68333 mm. Both agree, so the answer is 0.6833 mm to four decimal places.

Answer: 0.6833 mm (the plate moves away from the fixed walls by this amount, so both bars elongate by 0.6833 mm).

Cooling a constrained bar

An aluminium bar with E = 70 GPa and alpha = 23 × 10-6 per degree C is fixed between rigid walls. It is cooled by 15 degrees C. What is the magnitude of the thermal stress, in MPa? This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The stress magnitude in MPa.
  2. Which idea applies. sigma = E × alpha × delta T for a fully constrained bar.
  3. Work it out. E = 70 000 MPa. alpha × delta T = 23 × 10-6 × 15 = 3.45 × 10-4. Multiply: 70 000 × 3.45 × 10-4 = 24.15 MPa.
  4. Check. Cooling gives tension, the opposite sign to heating, but the same magnitude rule. 24.15 is a plausible size for aluminium under this change.

Answer: 24.15 MPa (tensile, because the bar is cooled).

E_s, A_s, a_s = 200000, 1000, 12e-6
E_c, A_c, a_c = 100000, 1000, 17e-6
dT = 50
L = 1000
F = (a_c - a_s) * dT / (1 / (A_s * E_s) + 1 / (A_c * E_c))
delta_steel = a_s * dT * L + F * L / (A_s * E_s)
print("plate change in length =", round(delta_steel, 4), "mm")

plate change in length = 0.6833 mm

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Fixed bar heated (NAT, 2 marks)

A steel bar (E = 200 GPa, α = 12 × 10−6/°C) is fixed between rigid walls and heated by 50 °C. Find the thermal stress in MPa.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. σ = EαΔT.
  2. Which idea applies. The free expansion is fully stopped.
  3. Work it out. 200000 · 12e−6 · 50 = 120 MPa (compression).
  4. Check. The stress does not depend on length or area.

Answer: 120 MPa (compression).

Exam-level 2: Partial gap (NAT, 2 marks)

The same bar is 2 m long and has a 0.6 mm gap to the wall before heating by 50 °C. Find the stress.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Stress after the gap closes.
  2. Which idea applies. Free expansion = αLΔT = 1.2 mm. Only the excess 0.6 mm is resisted.
  3. Work it out. ε = 0.6/2000 = 3 × 10−4. σ = 60 MPa.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
free = 12e-6 * 2000 * 50
print("free = %.2f mm, stress = %.1f MPa" % (free, 200e3 * (free - 0.6) / 2000))

free = 1.20 mm, stress = 60.0 MPa

Answer: 60 MPa (compression).

Exam-level 3: Composite bar (NAT, 2 marks)

A steel rod (A = 100 mm², E = 200 GPa, α = 12 × 10−6) and a copper tube (A = 200 mm², E = 100 GPa, α = 18 × 10−6) are joined at the ends and heated by 40 °C. Find the stress in the steel, in MPa.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Steel stress from compatibility.
  2. Which idea applies. Force balance: σsAs = σcAc. Compatibility: αsΔT + σs/Es = αcΔT − σc/Ec.
  3. Work it out. σc = σs/2. σs(1/200000 + 1/200000) = 6e−6 · 40 = 2.4e−4. σs = 24 MPa (tension). Copper 12 MPa (compression).
  4. Check. The code solves it.
dT = 40
ss = (18e-6 - 12e-6) * dT / (1 / 200e3 + (100 / 200) / 100e3)
print("steel %.1f MPa, copper %.1f MPa" % (ss, ss * 100 / 200))

steel 24.0 MPa, copper 12.0 MPa

Answer: 24 MPa (tension).

Summary

Thermal expansion
Growth of a material on heating. Precise definition: delta L = alpha × L × delta T for a free bar.
Free expansion
Growth with no restraint, so no stress. Precise definition: a bar that can change length without any force.
Constrained bar
A bar whose length is held fixed. Precise definition: total strain is zero.
Thermal strain
Strain the heat alone would cause. Precise definition: epsilonT = alpha × delta T.
Mechanical strain
Strain from stress. Precise definition: equal and opposite to the thermal strain in a fully constrained bar.
Thermal stress
Stress in a constrained bar. Precise definition: sigma = E × alpha × delta T in magnitude.
Coefficient of thermal expansion
Growth per unit length per degree. Precise definition: alpha, in per degree C.
Young's modulus
Stiffness of a material. Precise definition: E = sigma / epsilon in the elastic range.
Composite bar
Two or more materials joined so they share one change in length. Precise definition: members with the same displacement at a common joint.
Compatibility
Joined parts have the same displacement. Precise definition: equal change in length at the common plate.
Equilibrium
Forces balance. Precise definition: the internal forces in a free plate sum to zero.
Compressive stress
A squeezing stress, negative in the sign convention. Precise definition: occurs in a heated constrained bar.
Tensile stress
A pulling stress, positive in the sign convention. Precise definition: occurs in a cooled constrained bar.
Statically indeterminate
Equilibrium alone is not enough. Precise definition: needs compatibility as an extra rule.
Stiffness of a bar
How much force per unit change in length a bar gives. Precise definition: A × E / L.
Stress
Force per unit area. Precise definition: sigma = F / A.

Remember these

  1. A free bar grows but carries no stress.
  2. A fully constrained bar has zero total strain, so its mechanical strain cancels the thermal strain.
  3. The thermal stress is sigma = E × alpha × delta T, independent of the length and area of the bar.
  4. Heating a constrained bar gives compression, and cooling gives tension.
  5. Convert GPa to MPa before multiplying.
  6. In a composite bar, the material with the larger alpha is compressed.
  7. In a composite bar with a free plate, the force depends on A, E and alpha, but not on L.
  8. The two bars in a composite must have the same change in length.

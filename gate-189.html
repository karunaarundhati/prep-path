Shafts, rolling & sliding contact bearings

A shaft carries power from one machine part to another, and bearings let it turn with little friction. This page covers how to size a shaft under combined bending and torsion, how long a rolling bearing lasts, and what a sliding journal bearing does with its oil film, with GATE style calculations throughout.

The idea in plain words

Welcome. A shaft is a round bar that turns. It carries torque (a twisting push) from a motor to a gearbox, a pulley or a wheel. Bearings are the supports that hold the shaft in place and let it turn with as little friction as possible. Every rotating machine you know, from a bicycle wheel to a robot arm, has at least one shaft and at least one bearing.

Think of a child on a playground roundabout. The central pole is the shaft. Each hand pushing the roundabout gives a twist, which is torque. The child's weight pushing down on the pole bends it a little, which is bending. The place where the pole meets the ground, with a ring of oil or ball bearings, is the bearing. Both the twist and the bend must be handled by the shaft, and the bearing must last.

Why does this matter for GATE? In the Machine Design and CIM part of the Robotics and Automation paper, two kinds of question appear often. The first asks for the diameter of a shaft under given bending moment and torque. The second asks for the life of a rolling bearing under a given load and speed. The third, more conceptual, asks about the oil film in a journal bearing. Each one is a clear method with a formula and a few steps.

We will go slowly. First we understand the loads, then the formulas, then the bearings.

Key terms

Read this list once, and return to it when a term feels unfamiliar.

Shaft
A rotating round member that transmits power. Precisely: a machine element that carries torque and bending loads.
Torque (T)
A twisting action that turns the shaft. Precisely: force times the perpendicular distance from the axis, in N mm or N m.
Bending moment (M)
A bending action that makes the shaft curve. Precisely: the moment about a cross section caused by transverse loads, in N mm or N m.
Combined loading
Bending and torsion acting at the same time on the same section. Precisely: a stress state with both a normal stress from bending and a shear stress from torsion.
Yield strength (Sy)
The stress at which the shaft material starts to deform permanently. Precisely: the yield point of the material, in MPa.
Factor of safety (n)
The ratio of strength to working stress. Precisely: the number by which the design is safer than the yield limit.
Rolling contact bearing
A bearing where balls or rollers roll between two rings. Precisely: a bearing whose friction is rolling friction, not sliding.
Basic dynamic load rating (C)
The load a bearing can carry for one million revolutions with 90 percent survival. Precisely: the catalogue radial load that gives an L10 life of one million revolutions.
Equivalent load (P)
A single load that represents the real load on the bearing. Precisely: the load used with the rating C to find life, in kN.
L10 life
The life that 90 percent of identical bearings reach or exceed. Precisely: the number of millions of revolutions that 90 percent of a group survive, written L10.
Sliding contact (journal) bearing
A bearing where a shaft slides on a film of oil inside a bushing. Precisely: a plain bearing that works by hydrodynamic lubrication.
Hydrodynamic lubrication
Lubrication in which a moving wedge of oil lifts the shaft off the bearing surface. Precisely: separation of surfaces by a fluid film generated by the motion itself.
Eccentricity (e)
The offset between the shaft centre and the bearing centre under load. Precisely: the distance between the two centres, which shows how thick the oil film is.
Sommerfeld number (S)
A dimensionless number that groups speed, viscosity, load and clearance. Precisely: S = (mu N/P)(r/c)2.
Viscosity (mu)
The thickness or resistance to flow of a fluid. Precisely: the ratio of shear stress to velocity gradient, in Pa s.

Combined bending and torsion in a shaft

Picture a shaft as a long solid rod. When it carries a torque T, its outer fibres are twisted and feel a shear stress. When it carries a bending moment M, its top fibres are stretched and its bottom fibres are squeezed, so they feel a normal stress. Most real shafts have both at the same time, and the largest stress is found by combining the two.

For a solid round shaft of diameter d, the two stresses at the outer surface are given by the following formulas.

Bending stress: sigma = 32 M/(pi d3)
Torsional shear stress: tau = 16 T/(pi d3)

Here sigma and tau are in MPa when M and T are in N mm and d is in mm. Notice that both stresses fall with d cubed. A small increase in diameter gives a big reduction in stress.

How do we combine a normal stress and a shear stress? We use the same idea as the failure theories from the previous topic. The distortion energy (von Mises) theory gives an equivalent stress for a shaft with bending stress sigma and shear tau:

sigma' = square root of (sigma2 + 3 tau2)

Safety requires sigma' to be no more than Sy/n, where n is the factor of safety. Substituting the two stress formulas gives a single equation for the diameter, and that is the origin of the ASME style equation in the next section.

Key ideaA shaft carrying both bending and torque is designed for the equivalent stress, not for either stress alone. Combining them with sigma' = square root of (sigma2 + 3 tau2) is the standard textbook route.

If a shaft's diameter doubles, by how much does the bending stress fall, for the same moment?

The bending stress falls by a factor of 2 cubed = 8, because the stress is proportional to 1/d cubed.

Why do designers use the equivalent stress rather than the bending stress alone?

Because the shaft is loaded in bending and torsion at once. A stress check on bending alone would ignore the shear from torque and be unsafe.

Shaft diameter from the ASME style equation

Now we turn the combined stress idea into a formula for the diameter. The ASME style equation is a standard textbook form for a static solid shaft, based on the distortion energy theory. We can derive it in three short steps.

  1. Start from sigma' = Sy/n. Substitute sigma = 32M/(pi d3) and tau = 16T/(pi d3).
  2. Write sqrt(sigma2 + 3 tau2) = (1/(pi d3)) sqrt((32M)2 + 3(16T)2) = (16/(pi d3)) sqrt(4M2 + 3T2).
  3. Rearrange to get the diameter cubed: d3 = (16 n/(pi Sy)) sqrt(4M2 + 3T2).
d3 = (16 n/(pi Sy)) sqrt(4 M2 + 3 T2)

Here d is the shaft diameter in mm, n is the factor of safety, Sy is the yield strength in MPa, M is the bending moment in N mm, and T is the torque in N mm. In a real design the stress concentration factors Kf and Kfs multiply M and T. We leave them out here, which is the usual case in a GATE numerical that does not give them.

Worked example. A shaft carries a bending moment M = 100 N m and a torque T = 80 N m. The material has Sy = 300 MPa, and we want n = 2. Find the diameter.

  1. Convert to N mm: M = 100000 N mm, T = 80000 N mm.
  2. Inside the square root: 4M2 + 3T2 = 4 times 1010 + 3 times 6.4 times 109 = 4 times 1010 + 1.92 times 1010 = 5.92 times 1010. Its square root is 243311.
  3. Prefactor: 16 n/(pi Sy) = 32/(pi times 300) = 32/942.48 = 0.03395.
  4. Multiply: d3 = 0.03395 times 243311 = 8261 mm3.
  5. Cube root: d = 8261 to the power 1/3 = 20.22 mm.

So the shaft must be at least 20.22 mm in diameter. A designer would then round up to a standard size, which in this case would be a size greater than 20.22 mm.

TrapTake the cube root at the end, not at the beginning. Also convert N m to N mm before you square the moments. A factor of 1000 error in M or T changes the answer by a factor of 1000 to the power 2/3, which is 100.

GATE loves thisCheck the units of the final answer. The formula gives d in mm when M and T are in N mm and Sy is in MPa.

If the factor of safety is doubled, how does the required diameter change?

d3 doubles, so d increases by a factor of 2 to the power 1/3, which is about 1.26.

A shaft carries only torque, with no bending. What happens to the formula?

M = 0, so the square root reduces to 3T2, and the formula becomes d3 = (16 n/(pi Sy)) times square root of 3 times T.

Rolling contact bearings: load rating and L10 life

A rolling contact bearing has an inner ring, an outer ring, and a set of balls or rollers between them. As the shaft turns, the balls roll, and friction is very low. Each ball or roller carries a little load at a time, and that contact is tiny, so the surfaces are under high stress. Fatigue, which we met in the previous topic, eventually causes a small flake to break away from a race or a ball. That is how a rolling bearing wears out.

Because fatigue is random, identical bearings do not all last the same time. Some fail early and some last much longer. Manufacturers therefore quote the L10 life, the life that 90 percent of a large group of identical bearings reach or exceed. The basic dynamic load rating C is the load at which a bearing lasts one million revolutions at this L10 level.

L10 = (C/P)p million revolutions
L10h = (106/(60 n)) (C/P)p hours, with n the speed in rpm

Here C is the basic dynamic load rating in N or kN, P is the equivalent load in the same unit, and p is the life exponent. The exponent is p = 3 for ball bearings and p = 10/3 for roller bearings. The life in hours comes from converting millions of revolutions to hours: at n revolutions per minute, the bearing makes 60 n revolutions per hour.

Worked example, ball bearing. A ball bearing has C = 20 kN and carries an equivalent radial load P = 5 kN at 1500 rpm.

  1. Ratio: C/P = 20/5 = 4.
  2. Life in millions of revolutions: L10 = 43 = 64 million revolutions.
  3. Life in hours: L10h = (106/(60 times 1500)) times 64 = (106/90000) times 64 = 11.11 times 64 = 711.1 hours.

TrapDo not mix up the two forms. The formula with 106 in the denominator gives hours. Without it, you get millions of revolutions. Always check the question's required unit.

Key ideaLife depends on the cube (for ball) of the ratio C/P. Halving the load multiplies life by 8, and doubling the load divides life by 8.

A ball bearing is run at half its load. By how much does its life multiply?

Life multiplies by 2 cubed = 8, because life is proportional to (C/P) cubed and P is halved.

Why is L10 life, not average life, quoted for bearings?

Because bearings wear out by random fatigue, so their lives scatter widely. The L10 value gives a reliable design target: 90 percent of bearings will reach it.

Sliding contact (journal) bearings and the oil film

A journal bearing is simpler in shape. The shaft, called the journal, rotates inside a bushing with a small clearance. There are no balls or rollers. Instead, the shaft slides directly on a thin film of oil. This is useful for heavy loads, shock loads and very high speeds, where rolling bearings would struggle.

How does the oil hold the shaft up? Think of a sled running over snow. At rest, the sled sits on the snow. Once it moves, a thin wedge of air builds up under the front and lifts it a little. In a journal bearing, the rotating shaft drags oil into a narrowing gap, and the oil pressure builds up in the wedge. This pressure lifts the shaft off the bearing surface. The shaft then sits slightly off centre, and the gap is thinnest on one side. This is called hydrodynamic lubrication.

   bushing (fixed)
   ____________________________
  |                            |
  |        ( shaft )           |   shaft moves right
  |___________.________________|   oil is dragged into the gap
       thick          thin        pressure builds in the thin wedge

The picture is a sketch. The key point is that the shaft does not touch the bushing once the oil film is fully formed. During start-up or very slow running, the shaft does touch the bushing, which is called boundary lubrication. That is why starting a journal bearing causes more wear than running it.

TipA useful sentence for exams: "A journal bearing works by a film of oil that the rotating shaft pumps into a converging gap, creating pressure that supports the load."

TrapDo not describe the journal bearing as rolling. Its friction is sliding, and it needs a continuous oil supply. A rolling bearing needs only grease or a light oil bath.

What is the main advantage of a journal bearing over a rolling bearing?

It handles heavy loads, shock loads and very high speeds well. It has a large load-carrying area of oil, and it is quiet and robust.

What is boundary lubrication?

A state where the shaft and bushing are in close contact and the oil film is too thin to separate them. It happens at start-up, at very low speed, or with too little oil.

The Sommerfeld number

Engineers want one number that tells them where a journal bearing is operating. The Sommerfeld number, S, groups the speed, viscosity, load and clearance into one dimensionless value. A dimensionless number has no units, so it can be compared across sizes and oils.

S = (mu N / P) (r/c)2

Here mu is the dynamic viscosity of the oil in Pa s, N is the shaft speed in revolutions per second, P is the bearing pressure in Pa, which is the load divided by the projected area, r is the shaft radius, and c is the radial clearance, which is the gap between the shaft and the bushing. The ratio r/c is usually large, around 1000, because the clearance is very small compared with the shaft radius.

Worked example. A bearing has oil viscosity mu = 0.02 Pa s, speed N = 25 rev/s, bearing pressure P = 1 MPa = 1000000 Pa, and r/c = 1000.

  1. First part: mu N/P = 0.02 times 25/1000000 = 0.5/1000000 = 5 times 10-7.
  2. Square of r/c: 1000 squared = 106.
  3. Multiply: S = 5 times 10-7 times 106 = 0.5.

The Python block below checks this number. The Sommerfeld number is a convenient way to compare bearings. A larger S generally means the shaft sits closer to the centre of the bushing, with a thicker oil film. Use this as a qualitative idea, and check the question for any specific chart it expects.

Key ideaS grows with speed and viscosity, and falls with load. Because of the square, a small change in clearance has a big effect on S.

If the speed doubles and everything else stays the same, what happens to S?

S doubles, because S is proportional to N.

Why is the clearance ratio squared in the formula?

The film thickness and the shear rate both depend on the gap. The gap shows up in the geometry of the oil wedge and in the viscous shear of the oil, which is why the formula uses it twice. For GATE, use the formula as given.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original, written in the GATE style, and they are not copied from any real exam paper. Attempt the "How to think about it" steps yourself before you read the answer.

Life of a ball bearing in millions of revolutions

A ball bearing has a basic dynamic load rating C = 25 kN and carries an equivalent radial load P = 5 kN. What is its L10 life, in millions of revolutions? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. L10 in millions of revolutions.
  2. Which idea applies. For a ball bearing, p = 3, so L10 = (C/P)3.
  3. Work it out. C/P = 25/5 = 5. Then 53 = 125 million revolutions.
  4. Check. A ratio of 5 gives a life of 125 times one million, which is a large number, as a ratio greater than 1 should give.

Answer: 125 million revolutions.

Sommerfeld number of a bearing

A journal bearing has oil viscosity mu = 0.03 Pa s, speed N = 20 rev/s, bearing pressure P = 2 MPa and r/c = 500. What is the Sommerfeld number? Give it to three decimal places. (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The dimensionless number S.
  2. Which idea applies. S = (mu N/P)(r/c)2, with P in Pa.
  3. Work it out. P = 2 MPa = 2000000 Pa. mu N/P = 0.03 times 20/2000000 = 0.6/2000000 = 3 times 10-7. (r/c)2 = 500 squared = 250000. So S = 3 times 10-7 times 250000 = 0.075.
  4. Check. The answer is a small positive number with no units, as a Sommerfeld number should be.

Answer: 0.075.

Statements about bearings

Which of the following statements about bearings are correct? Select all that apply. (MSQ)

  • (A) For a ball bearing, halving the load multiplies the L10 life by 8.
  • (B) A journal bearing relies on a fluid film that separates the shaft from the bushing when running.
  • (C) The L10 life is the life that 50 percent of identical bearings reach.
  • (D) Roller bearings use an exponent p = 3 in the life formula.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are true. More than one may be correct.
  2. Which idea applies. Life goes as (C/P)p, with p = 3 for balls and 10/3 for rollers. The L10 life is the 90 percent survival life. A journal bearing uses a film.
  3. Work it out. (A) True: (2)3 = 8. (B) True: the oil film supports the shaft when the bearing is running at speed. (C) False: L10 is the 90 percent survival life. (D) False: rollers use 10/3.
  4. Check. Statements (C) and (D) both give a value different from the definitions, so both must be wrong.

Answer: (A) and (B) are correct.

Bearing life in hours

A ball bearing has C = 40 kN and carries P = 10 kN at 1200 rpm. What is its L10 life in hours? Give the answer to one decimal place. (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. L10h, the life in hours.
  2. Which idea applies. L10h = (106/(60 n)) (C/P)3.
  3. Work it out. C/P = 4, so (C/P)3 = 64 million revolutions. Hours per million revolutions at 1200 rpm: 106/(60 times 1200) = 106/72000 = 13.89. So L10h = 13.89 times 64 = 888.9 hours.
  4. Check. A life of about 889 hours is about 37 days of continuous running. The number is reasonable for a moderate load ratio.

Answer: 888.9 hours.

Roller bearing life in revolutions

A roller bearing has a dynamic load rating C that is three times the equivalent load P. What is its L10 life, in millions of revolutions, to two decimal places? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. L10 for a roller bearing.
  2. Which idea applies. For a roller bearing, p = 10/3, so L10 = (C/P)10/3.
  3. Work it out. C/P = 3. Then 3 to the power 10/3 = 38.94 million revolutions.
  4. Check. A ball bearing with the same ratio would give 3 cubed = 27 million revolutions. The roller value 38.94 is larger because the exponent 10/3 is larger than 3, and the ratio 3 is greater than 1. Always compare with the correct exponent for each bearing type.

Answer: 38.94 million revolutions.

Shaft diameter under bending and torque

A solid steel shaft carries a bending moment M = 150 N m and a torque T = 100 N m. The yield strength is Sy = 400 MPa and the factor of safety is n = 2.5. Using the ASME style static equation with stress concentration factors taken as 1, find the minimum shaft diameter in mm, to two decimal places. (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The minimum diameter d.
  2. Which idea applies. d3 = (16 n/(pi Sy)) sqrt(4M2 + 3T2), with M and T in N mm.
  3. Work it out. M = 150000 N mm, T = 100000 N mm. Inside the root: 4 times 1500002 + 3 times 1000002 = 9 times 1010 + 3 times 1010 = 1.2 times 1011, whose root is 346410. Prefactor: 16 times 2.5/(pi times 400) = 40/1256.6 = 0.03183. So d3 = 0.03183 times 346410 = 11027 mm3, and d = 22.26 mm.
  4. Check. Compare with the worked example in the unit: a larger moment, a larger torque and a larger n all push the diameter up, so 22.26 mm, larger than 20.22 mm, is consistent.

Answer: 22.26 mm.

Required load rating for a target life

A ball bearing carries an equivalent radial load P = 6 kN and runs at 1500 rpm. It must have an L10 life of at least 500 hours. What is the smallest basic dynamic load rating C, in kN, to two decimal places? (NAT: enter a number.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The required C.
  2. Which idea applies. First convert the target life to millions of revolutions, then invert L10 = (C/P)3.
  3. Work it out. Revolutions in 500 hours at 1500 rpm: 500 times 60 times 1500 = 45000000 = 45 million. Then (C/P)3 = 45, so C/P = 45 to the power 1/3 = 3.557. So C = 6 times 3.557 = 21.34 kN.
  4. Check. A rating of 21.34 kN for a 6 kN load gives a ratio of 3.56, and cubing that gives about 45 million revolutions. The check works.

Answer: 21.34 kN.

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Bearing life (NAT, 2 marks)

A ball bearing has dynamic load rating C = 30 kN and carries P = 5 kN. Find the L10 life in millions of revolutions.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. L = (C/P)³.
  2. Which idea applies. Ball bearings use exponent 3.
  3. Work it out. 6³ = 216 million revolutions.
  4. Check. A roller bearing would use exponent 10/3.

Answer: 216.

Exam-level 2: Life in hours (NAT, 2 marks)

The same bearing turns at 1200 rpm. Find the life in hours.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Lh = L · 10⁶/(60N).
  2. Which idea applies. Convert revolutions to hours.
  3. Work it out. 216e6/72000 = 3000 h.
  4. Check. Halving the load gives 8 times the life.

Answer: 3000 h.

Exam-level 3: Petroff (NAT, 2 marks)

A journal bearing has diameter 50 mm, length 50 mm, radial clearance 0.05 mm, oil viscosity 0.02 Pa·s and speed 1500 rpm. Find the friction torque by Petroff's law in N·m, correct to 4 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The friction torque T = 2πηωr³L/c.
  2. Which idea applies. Shear stress τ = ηU/c on the area 2πrL, with arm r.
  3. Work it out. r = 0.025, ω = 157.08. T = 2π · 1.5625e−5 · 0.05 · 0.02 · 157.08/5e−5 = 0.3084 N·m.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
import math
r, L, c, eta, w = 0.025, 0.05, 5e-5, 0.02, 1500 * 2 * math.pi / 60
print("%.4f N m" % (2 * math.pi * r ** 3 * L * eta * w / c))

0.3084 N m

Answer: about 0.3084 N·m.

Remember these

  2. The ASME style equation has 16 n over pi Sy and a square root of 4M squared plus 3T squared.
  3. Life is (C/P) to the power p, with p = 3 for balls and 10/3 for rollers.

Marginal, conditional & joint probability

When two random things are studied together, you need three related ideas: the joint probability of both, the marginal probability of one on its own, and the conditional probability of one given the other. These appear in almost every GATE DA question that gives a table of counts or a joint distribution, so they are worth learning slowly and carefully.

The idea in plain words

Imagine a survey of 20 students. Each student has two answers: whether they own a bike (yes or no) and whether they walk to college (yes or no). You can put the answers in a grid, with rows for bike and columns for walking. Each box counts the students with that pair of answers. That grid is a joint table. It holds everything about the two questions at once.

From the grid you can answer three kinds of question. The first is about a pair: how many students both own a bike and walk? That is a joint probability. The second is about one question alone: how many students own a bike, whatever the walking answer? That is a marginal probability. The name comes from the margins of the table, where the row and column totals are written. The third is about a condition: among students who walk, what share own a bike? That is a conditional probability.

Why does this matter for GATE DA? Many questions give a table, a joint probability mass function (PMF) or a joint probability density function (PDF), and ask for a marginal, a conditional, or a check of independence. The method is always the same: read the right box or add the right boxes, then divide by the right total.

Key ideaThe joint probability says "both". The marginal says "one, regardless of the other", and you get it by adding across the other variable. The conditional says "one, given the other", and you get it by dividing by the given part.

Key terms

Joint probability P(A, B)
The probability that A and B both happen. Written P(A ∩ B) or P(A, B) when A and B are two random quantities.
Joint PMF p(x, y)
For discrete random variables X and Y, the probability P(X = x, Y = y) for each pair. The values add to 1 over all pairs.
Joint PDF f(x, y)
For continuous variables, a density whose double integral over the whole plane is 1. Probabilities come from integrating it over a region.
Marginal probability
The probability of one variable alone, found by adding (discrete) or integrating (continuous) the joint over the other variable. Plain meaning: sum out the other variable.
Conditional probability P(B | A)
The probability of B given that A has happened. Defined as P(A, B)/P(A) when P(A) > 0.
Chain rule
P(A, B) = P(A) × P(B | A). It rebuilds a joint probability from a marginal and a conditional.
Independence (for variables)
X and Y are independent when P(X = x, Y = y) = P(X = x) × P(Y = y) for every pair.
Random variable
A number that depends on the outcome of a random experiment, such as the count of heads in three tosses.

Step 1: Joint probability and joint tables

Building the table

Take 20 students. Each student is classified by two variables. Let X be 0 if the student does not own a bike and 1 if they do. Let Y be 0, 1 or 2 for the number of days per week they walk to college, grouped into "none", "some" and "most". The table of counts is:

Y = 0Y = 1Y = 2Row total
X = 02428
X = 135412
Column total59620

The grand total 20 is the number of students. To turn counts into probabilities, divide every entry by 20. The result is the joint PMF. For example, P(X = 0, Y = 1) = 4/20 = 1/5.

Worked example: reading a joint probability

Find P(X = 1, Y = 2), the probability that a student owns a bike and walks most days.

  1. Locate the box. Row X = 1, column Y = 2. The entry is 4.
  2. Divide by the total. 4 out of 20 students.
  3. Simplify. P(X = 1, Y = 2) = 4/20 = 1/5.

TrapDo not divide a box by its row total when you want a joint probability. The joint needs the grand total, 20. Dividing by 8 or 12 gives a conditional probability, which is a different quantity.

Question: What is P(X = 0, Y = 0) for the table above?

The box is 2, and the grand total is 20, so P = 2/20 = 1/10.

Question: Why must all the joint probabilities add to 1?

Every student falls into exactly one box. The boxes cover all possible pairs, so the probabilities of all pairs sum to 1, which is the normalisation axiom.

Step 2: Marginal probability by summing out

Summing out the other variable

Suppose you want the probability that a student owns a bike, ignoring how often they walk. You want all the students in row X = 1, whatever their Y value. So you add the row: 3 + 5 + 4 = 12 students. This is the marginal probability:

P(X = 1) = Σy P(X = 1, Y = y) = 3/20 + 5/20 + 4/20 = 12/20 = 3/5

The symbol Σy means "add over all values of y". Summing out Y is the discrete version of "sum the joint over the other variable". For the other margin, add down the column instead.

Worked example: marginal of Y

Find P(Y = 1), the probability that a student walks "some" days, regardless of bike ownership.

  1. Choose the column. Y = 1 has entries 4 (for X = 0) and 5 (for X = 1).
  2. Add. P(Y = 1) = P(X = 0, Y = 1) + P(X = 1, Y = 1) = 4/20 + 5/20 = 9/20.
  3. Check against the column total. The column total is 9, which gives 9/20. The two methods agree.

Continuous case: integrate out

For a joint PDF, the same idea uses an integral. The marginal density of X is fX(x) = ∫ f(x, y) dy over all values of y. You will see a worked version in Step 4.

TipThe marginal of X always sums to 1 over the values of X. Check this at the end. In the example, 3/5 + 2/5 = 1.

TrapA marginal is not the same as a single entry in the margin. The row total is the sum of the row's joint entries. Writing the total as the first entry alone is a common slip.

Question: What is P(X = 0) for the table above?

Add row X = 0: 2 + 4 + 2 = 8, out of 20. So P(X = 0) = 8/20 = 2/5. Check: 2/5 + 3/5 = 1.

Question: What is P(Y = 2)?

Add column Y = 2: 2 + 4 = 6, out of 20. So P(Y = 2) = 6/20 = 3/10.

Step 3: Conditional probability and the chain rule

The meaning of "given"

A conditional probability restricts your attention to one part of the world. P(Y = 1 | X = 0) asks: among students who do not own a bike, what share walk "some" days? You are no longer looking at all 20 students. You are looking only at the 8 students in row X = 0, and asking what share of those are in column Y = 1.

P(B | A) = P(A, B) / P(A)

Here P(A, B) is the joint probability and P(A) is the marginal probability of the condition. Dividing by P(A) rescales the joint so that the condition becomes the whole world, with total 1.

Worked example: every step

Find P(Y = 1 | X = 0).

  1. Joint. P(X = 0, Y = 1) = 4/20 = 1/5.
  2. Marginal of the condition. P(X = 0) = 8/20 = 2/5.
  3. Divide. P(Y = 1 | X = 0) = (1/5)/(2/5) = (1/5) × (5/2) = 1/2.
  4. Check with counts. Among the 8 students in row X = 0, 4 are in column Y = 1. So 4/8 = 1/2. The two methods agree.

The chain rule

Rearranging the conditional gives the chain rule:

P(A, B) = P(A) × P(B | A)

This is useful when a problem describes events in sequence, such as "first draw a bag, then draw a ball". The first step gives the marginal and the second step gives the conditional, and the product gives the joint. The same rule works with variables: P(X = x, Y = y) = P(X = x) P(Y = y | X = x).

Independence in terms of conditionals

X and Y are independent exactly when conditioning changes nothing. That means P(Y = y | X = x) = P(Y = y) for every x and y. In the table, P(Y = 1 | X = 0) = 1/2, while P(Y = 1) = 9/20. These differ, so X and Y are not independent. Equivalently, P(X = 0, Y = 1) should equal P(X = 0) × P(Y = 1) = 2/5 × 9/20 = 18/100 = 9/50. But the table gives 1/5 = 10/50. The mismatch confirms dependence.

GATE loves thisTo test independence from a table, compute one joint entry and compare it with the product of its two marginals. One mismatch proves dependence. All cells must match to prove independence.

TrapP(A | B) and P(B | A) are different. In the table, P(Y = 1 | X = 0) = 1/2, but P(X = 0 | Y = 1) = 4/9. Always write the condition after the bar and divide by its probability.

Question: Using the table, what is P(X = 1 | Y = 2)?

The column Y = 2 has total 6, and its X = 1 entry is 4. So P(X = 1 | Y = 2) = 4/6 = 2/3.

Question: If P(A) = 0.4 and P(B | A) = 0.5, what is P(A, B)?

By the chain rule, P(A, B) = 0.4 × 0.5 = 0.2.

Step 4: Joint PMF and PDF, and marginals from a density

Normalising a joint PMF

Sometimes a joint PMF is given as a formula, with an unknown constant. The total probability must be 1, and that fixes the constant. For example, let p(x, y) = k(x + y) for x and y in {0, 1}. The four values are p(0,0) = 0, p(0,1) = k, p(1,0) = k and p(1,1) = 2k. Their sum is 4k, and it must equal 1. So k = 1/4.

Worked example: marginal of a discrete formula

With k = 1/4, find P(X = 1).

  1. Values with X = 1. p(1, 0) = 1/4 × 1 = 1/4 and p(1, 1) = 1/4 × 2 = 1/2.
  2. Add. P(X = 1) = 1/4 + 1/2 = 3/4.
  3. Check. P(X = 0) = p(0,0) + p(0,1) = 0 + 1/4 = 1/4. Then 1/4 + 3/4 = 1.

Continuous case: marginal from a joint density

Let f(x, y) = x + y on the unit square 0 ≤ x ≤ 1 and 0 ≤ y ≤ 1. Check that it is a valid density first. The double integral is ∫01 ∫01 (x + y) dy dx = ∫01 (x + 1/2) dx = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1. Good.

  1. Marginal of X. Integrate out y: fX(x) = ∫01 (x + y) dy = [xy + y2/2] from 0 to 1 = x + 1/2.
  2. Check it integrates to 1. ∫01 (x + 1/2) dx = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1.
  3. Probability. P(X ≤ 1/2) = ∫01/2 (x + 1/2) dx = [x2/2 + x/2] from 0 to 1/2 = 1/8 + 1/4 = 3/8.

The Python check below repeats the density checks numerically with a grid of midpoints.

n = 500
h = 1 / n
total = 0.0
inside = 0.0
for i in range(n):
    x = (i + 0.5) * h
    for j in range(n):
        y = (j + 0.5) * h
        total += (x + y) * h * h
        if x <= 0.5:
            inside += (x + y) * h * h
print("total probability =", round(total, 4))
print("P(X <= 1/2) =", round(inside, 4))
total probability = 1.0
P(X <= 1/2) = 0.375

Key ideaFor any joint distribution, the marginal of X is found by summing or integrating over all values of the other variable. It always gives a valid distribution for X on its own.

TrapWhen integrating out y in a density, keep the limits of y as given by the region. If the region is a triangle, the limits depend on x, and forgetting this gives a wrong marginal.

Question: For f(x, y) = x + y on the unit square, what is the marginal of Y?

By symmetry, fY(y) = y + 1/2. Check: ∫01 (y + 1/2) dy = 1.

Question: Is the density f(x, y) = x + y on the unit square a product of its marginals?

No. The product (x + 1/2)(y + 1/2) = xy + x/2 + y/2 + 1/4 is not equal to x + y, for example at x = y = 0, where the product is 1/4 but f = 0. So X and Y are not independent.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original GATE-style questions written for practice. They are not past exam questions. Use the 20-student table from Step 1 unless a problem gives its own data.

Reading a marginal

Using the 20-student table, find P(Y = 0) (NAT style, as a decimal).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The marginal of Y at the value 0.
  2. Which idea applies. Add the column Y = 0 and divide by the grand total.
  3. Work it out. Column Y = 0 has 2 + 3 = 5 students. So P(Y = 0) = 5/20 = 0.25.
  4. Check. The column totals are 5, 9 and 6. They add to 20, so the marginals sum to 1.

Answer: 0.25.

Which statements about the table are true?

Using the 20-student table, which of the following are true? Select all that apply.

(A) P(X = 1) = 3/5. (B) P(X = 0, Y = 2) = 1/10. (C) P(Y = 1 | X = 1) = 5/12. (D) X and Y are independent.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Check each statement against the table.
  2. Which idea applies. Marginals, joints and conditionals.
  3. Work it out. (A): row X = 1 totals 12, so 12/20 = 3/5. True. (B): the box is 2, so 2/20 = 1/10. True. (C): row X = 1 totals 12, and the Y = 1 box is 5, so 5/12. True. (D): P(X = 0, Y = 0) = 2/20 = 1/10, but P(X = 0) P(Y = 0) = 2/5 × 1/4 = 1/10. That matches. Check another cell: P(X = 0, Y = 1) = 1/5 but P(X = 0) P(Y = 1) = 2/5 × 9/20 = 9/50. These differ, so (D) is false.
  4. Check. The independence test fails at one cell, which is enough.

Answer: (A), (B) and (C).

Conditional from a column

Using the 20-student table, find P(X = 1 | Y = 1) as a fraction.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The probability of X = 1 given that Y = 1.
  2. Which idea applies. Restrict to column Y = 1, then take the share in row X = 1.
  3. Work it out. Column Y = 1 has total 9, and the box X = 1 in that column is 5. So P(X = 1 | Y = 1) = 5/9. Using the formula: P(X = 1, Y = 1)/P(Y = 1) = (5/20)/(9/20) = 5/9.
  4. Check. The other conditional in the same column is 4/9, and 5/9 + 4/9 = 1, as the conditional must sum to 1 over X.

Answer: 5/9.

Finding a constant

A joint PMF is p(x, y) = k(x + y) for x, y in {0, 1}, and p = 0 elsewhere. Find k (NAT style, as a decimal).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The constant that makes the PMF valid.
  2. Which idea applies. The PMF must add to 1 over all cells.
  3. Work it out. The four values are k(0+0) = 0, k(0+1) = k, k(1+0) = k and k(1+1) = 2k. Their sum is 4k = 1, so k = 0.25.
  4. Check. With k = 0.25 the values are 0, 0.25, 0.25 and 0.5. They are non-negative and add to 1.

Answer: 0.25.

Marginal of the formula

Use the PMF from the previous problem with k = 0.25. Find P(X = 1) (NAT style, as a decimal).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The marginal of X at 1.
  2. Which idea applies. Sum the joint over y with x = 1.
  3. Work it out. P(X = 1) = p(1, 0) + p(1, 1) = 0.25 + 0.5 = 0.75.
  4. Check. P(X = 0) = p(0, 0) + p(0, 1) = 0 + 0.25 = 0.25. The two marginals add to 1.

Answer: 0.75.

Probability in a triangle

Let (X, Y) have joint PDF f(x, y) = x + y on the unit square 0 ≤ x ≤ 1, 0 ≤ y ≤ 1. Find P(X + Y ≤ 1) as a fraction.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The probability of the triangle below the line x + y = 1.
  2. Which idea applies. Integrate the density over the region, with limits from the triangle.
  3. Work it out. For x from 0 to 1, y runs from 0 to 1 - x. The inner integral is ∫01-x (x + y) dy = x(1 - x) + (1 - x)2/2. The outer integral is ∫01 [x - x2 + (1 - x)2/2] dx = 1/2 - 1/3 + 1/6 = 1/3.
  4. Check. The triangle has area 1/2, and the density is larger on the far corner (1,1), where it equals 2. Near the origin the density is small, so the probability in the lower triangle is less than the average density times area. The density averages 1 over the square, and the lower triangle is where x + y is small, so its probability must be below one half. The answer 1/3 is plausible.

Answer: 1/3.

Conditional on a union of columns

Using the 20-student table, find P(X = 0 | Y ≥ 1) as a fraction. Then state whether P(X = 0 | Y ≥ 1) equals P(X = 0).

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A conditional on the event Y ≥ 1, which is the union of columns Y = 1 and Y = 2.
  2. Which idea applies. Use P(A | B) = P(A, B)/P(B), with the event B spanning two columns.
  3. Work it out. P(Y ≥ 1) = (9 + 6)/20 = 15/20. The part with X = 0 and Y ≥ 1 is 4 + 2 = 6 students, so P(X = 0, Y ≥ 1) = 6/20. Then P(X = 0 | Y ≥ 1) = (6/20)/(15/20) = 6/15 = 2/5.
  4. Check. P(X = 0) = 2/5 as well. So the conditional equals the marginal, which means the event Y ≥ 1 does not change the chance of X = 0 in this table. So the events X = 0 and Y ≥ 1 are independent, even though the cell-by-cell test for X and Y failed earlier.

Answer: 2/5. Yes, it equals P(X = 0) = 2/5.

Exam-level 1: conditional probability from a joint table (NAT, 2 marks)

The joint probability table of two random variables X and Y is: P(X=0, Y=0) = 0.1, P(X=0, Y=1) = 0.2, P(X=0, Y=2) = 0.1, P(X=1, Y=0) = 0.2, P(X=1, Y=1) = 0.3, and P(X=1, Y=2) = 0.1. Find the conditional probability P(X = 1 given that Y is 1 or 2). Use the formula P(A|B) = P(A and B) / P(B), adding the relevant entries of the table. Give the answer as a fraction in lowest terms.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The probability that X is 1, once we know Y is 1 or 2.
  2. Which idea applies. The conditional probability formula, with the table entries added up for the event B = {Y is 1 or 2}.
  3. Work it out. P(B) = P(Y=1) + P(Y=2) = (0.2 + 0.3) + (0.1 + 0.1) = 0.5 + 0.2 = 0.7. The event A and B means X = 1 and Y is 1 or 2, so P(A and B) = 0.3 + 0.1 = 0.4. Then P(A|B) = 0.4 / 0.7 = 4/7, which is about 0.571.
  4. Check. The Python check adds the table entries with exact fractions. The total of all six entries should be 1.
from fractions import Fraction as F

joint = {(0, 0): F(1, 10), (0, 1): F(2, 10), (0, 2): F(1, 10),
         (1, 0): F(2, 10), (1, 1): F(3, 10), (1, 2): F(1, 10)}
print("total probability:", sum(joint.values()))
numer = sum(p for (x, y), p in joint.items() if x == 1 and y != 0)
denom = sum(p for (x, y), p in joint.items() if y != 0)
print("P(X=1 given Y is 1 or 2):", numer / denom)
total probability: 1
P(X=1 given Y is 1 or 2): 4/7

Answer: 4/7, which is about 0.571.

Exam-level 2: reading the same joint table (MSQ, 2 marks)

Use the joint table from the previous problem: P(X=0, Y=0) = 0.1, P(X=0, Y=1) = 0.2, P(X=0, Y=2) = 0.1, P(X=1, Y=0) = 0.2, P(X=1, Y=1) = 0.3 and P(X=1, Y=2) = 0.1. Which of the following statements are true? Select all that apply.

(A) P(X = 0) = 0.4. (B) P(Y = 1) = 0.5. (C) X and Y are independent. (D) P(Y = 2 given X = 1) = 1/6.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which of the four statements are true for this table.
  2. Which idea applies. Marginal probabilities add a row or a column. Independence means every joint entry equals the product of its marginals.
  3. Work it out. P(X = 0) = 0.1 + 0.2 + 0.1 = 0.4, so (A) is true. P(Y = 1) = 0.2 + 0.3 = 0.5, so (B) is true. For (C), P(X=0) x P(Y=0) = 0.4 x 0.3 = 0.12, but P(X=0, Y=0) = 0.1, so they are not independent and (C) is false. For (D), P(X = 1) = 0.6 and P(Y = 2 and X = 1) = 0.1, so P(Y = 2 given X = 1) = 0.1 / 0.6 = 1/6, and (D) is true.
  4. Check. The Python check computes the marginals and the conditional probability with exact fractions.
from fractions import Fraction as F

joint = {(0, 0): F(1, 10), (0, 1): F(2, 10), (0, 2): F(1, 10),
         (1, 0): F(2, 10), (1, 1): F(3, 10), (1, 2): F(1, 10)}
pX0 = sum(v for (x, y), v in joint.items() if x == 0)
pX1 = sum(v for (x, y), v in joint.items() if x == 1)
pY0 = sum(v for (x, y), v in joint.items() if y == 0)
pY1 = sum(v for (x, y), v in joint.items() if y == 1)
print("P(X=0):", pX0)
print("P(Y=1):", pY1)
print("P(X=0, Y=0):", joint[(0, 0)], "vs product:", pX0 * pY0)
print("P(Y=2 given X=1):", joint[(1, 2)] / pX1)
P(X=0): 2/5
P(Y=1): 1/2
P(X=0, Y=0): 1/10 vs product: 3/25
P(Y=2 given X=1): 1/6

Answer: (A), (B) and (D). (C) is wrong, because P(X=0, Y=0) = 0.1 but the product of the marginals is 0.12.

Exam-level 3: screening test with Bayes rule (NAT, 2 marks)

A screening test is used on a population in which 2 per cent of people have a disease. The test is positive for 90 per cent of people who have the disease (this is its sensitivity), and positive for 5 per cent of people who do not have it (the false positive rate). Use the law of total probability to find P(positive), then Bayes' rule to find the probability that a randomly chosen person with a positive result actually has the disease. Give the answer as a fraction, and as a decimal to three places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. P(disease given positive), which is the positive predictive value of the test.
  2. Which idea applies. The law of total probability for P(positive), then Bayes' rule: P(D|+) = P(+|D) P(D) / P(+).
  3. Work it out. P(D and +) = 0.02 x 0.9 = 0.018. P(not D and +) = 0.98 x 0.05 = 0.049. So P(+) = 0.018 + 0.049 = 0.067. Then P(D|+) = 0.018 / 0.067 = 18/67, which is about 0.269.
  4. Check. The Python check uses exact fractions for each step. The answer is well below 0.9, which is a reasonable outcome when the disease is rare.
from fractions import Fraction as F

prev, sens, fp = F(2, 100), F(90, 100), F(5, 100)
pos = prev * sens + (1 - prev) * fp
print("P(positive):", pos)
print("P(disease given positive):", prev * sens / pos, "=", round(float(prev * sens / pos), 3))
P(positive): 67/1000
P(disease given positive): 18/67 = 0.269

Answer: 18/67, which is about 0.269.

Exam-level 4: reversing the order of draws (NAT, 2 marks)

A bag contains 3 red balls and 2 blue balls. Two balls are drawn one after another without replacement, and every ordered pair of draws is equally likely. Find the probability that the first ball is blue and the second is red. Then find the probability that the second ball is red. Finally, find the probability that the first ball was blue, given that the second ball is red. Give the last answer as a fraction in lowest terms.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A conditional probability that runs backwards in time: the first draw given the second.
  2. Which idea applies. The multiplication rule for the joint event, the law of total probability for the second draw, then the conditional formula.
  3. Work it out. P(first blue and second red) = (2/5) x (3/4) = 6/20 = 3/10. P(second red) = P(first red) x P(second red | first red) + P(first blue) x P(second red | first blue) = (3/5)(2/4) + (2/5)(3/4) = 3/10 + 3/10 = 3/5. So P(first blue | second red) = (3/10) / (3/5) = 1/2.
  4. Check. The Python check uses exact fractions. The second draw's probability should match 3/5, the same as the first draw's, which is a useful symmetry check.
from fractions import Fraction as F

p_b1_r2 = F(2, 5) * F(3, 4)
p_r2 = F(3, 5) * F(2, 4) + F(2, 5) * F(3, 4)
print("P(first blue and second red):", p_b1_r2)
print("P(second red):", p_r2)
print("P(first blue given second red):", p_b1_r2 / p_r2)
P(first blue and second red): 3/10
P(second red): 3/5
P(first blue given second red): 1/2

Answer: 1/2.

Summary

Joint probability
P(A, B), the probability that both happen. A single cell of a table divided by the grand total.
Joint PMF
The probabilities P(X = x, Y = y) for all pairs, adding to 1.
Joint PDF
A density f(x, y) whose double integral over the plane is 1.
Marginal probability
The probability of one variable alone, obtained by summing or integrating out the other.
Conditional probability
P(B | A) = P(A, B)/P(A). The share of A's outcomes that are also in B.
Chain rule
P(A, B) = P(A) P(B | A).
Independence for variables
P(X, Y) = P(X) P(Y) for every pair of values.
Normalisation
The sum of a PMF, or the integral of a PDF, over all values equals 1.
Row or column total
The sum of the joint entries in one row or column, which is a marginal count.
Restricting to a condition
Conditioning keeps only the cells in the condition and divides by their total.
Reversing a condition
P(A | B) and P(B | A) are different numbers. The joint probability links them: both equal P(A, B) divided by the relevant marginal.

Remember these

  • Joint divides by the grand total. Conditional divides by the row or column total of the condition.
  • Marginal means add across the other variable.
  • Check the marginals add to 1 before you go on.
  • Test independence cell by cell: one mismatch proves dependence.
  • P(A | B) and P(B | A) are not the same number.
  • For a density, the marginal integrates out the other variable, and the limits come from the region.

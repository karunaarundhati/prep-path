t-distribution & Chi-squared distribution

The t-distribution and the chi-squared distribution are the two bell-like and skewed shapes you use when you estimate a mean or a variance from a small sample. Knowing which one to use, and how to read its table, is a steady source of GATE DA marks.

The idea in plain words

In the last topic you built a confidence interval for a mean using the normal distribution, with z = 1.96. That worked because you knew the population standard deviation sigma. In real life you usually do not know sigma. You estimate it with the sample standard deviation s. Now the number you divide by is itself uncertain, and the normal distribution is no longer quite right.

The fix is the t-distribution. It looks like the normal, but with heavier tails, which means extreme values are a little more likely. The extra tail width pays for the extra uncertainty in s. The t-distribution gets closer and closer to the normal as the sample grows.

The chi-squared distribution is a different shape. It describes sums of squares, and it is used for variances and for checking whether observed counts fit an expected pattern. It is skewed to the right, and it can never be negative.

Both distributions depend on a number called the degrees of freedom. GATE DA questions often hide the degrees of freedom inside the sample size, so this page shows you how to find them.

Key terms

Each entry gives the plain meaning first and the precise definition second.

Standard normal
The normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 1, written Z.
Degrees of freedom (df)
The number of independent pieces of information left after estimating parameters. For one sample mean, df = n - 1.
t-distribution
The distribution of (x-bar - mu) / (s / square root of n) for normal data. It has df degrees of freedom.
Heavy tails
Tails that die away more slowly than the normal, so extreme values are more likely.
Chi-squared distribution
The distribution of a sum of k squared independent standard normal values. It has k degrees of freedom.
Sample variance
s squared, equal to the sum of squared deviations divided by n - 1.
Critical value
The cut-off in a table that leaves a chosen probability in the tail.
Test statistic
A number computed from the sample and used to judge a hypothesis.
Goodness-of-fit
Checking whether observed counts match an expected pattern.

Why we need new distributions

The problem with an unknown sigma

Suppose you take a sample of 5 measurements and compute the t-like ratio:

T = (x-bar - mu) / (s / square root of n)

Symbols: x-bar is the sample mean, mu is the population mean, s is the sample SD, and n is the sample size. If the population is normal and sigma were known, the ratio using sigma would be exactly normal. Using s instead adds noise, because s changes from sample to sample. Small samples give a very unreliable s, so the spread of the ratio is larger than the normal predicts.

Look at the 95 per cent cut-off. The normal says 1.96. For 5 observations, the correct cut-off is 2.776, which is much larger. Ignoring the extra uncertainty would make your intervals and tests too confident.

The problem with variances

A test about a variance needs the distribution of the sample variance. The sample variance is built from squared deviations, so its distribution is skewed and cannot go below zero. The normal is symmetric and can go negative, so it cannot be used here. This is where the chi-squared distribution comes in.

Key ideaReplacing sigma by s makes the ratio spread out more for small samples. The t-distribution corrects for this, and it converges to the normal as the sample grows.

Self-check: why is a sample variance never negative?

It is a sum of squared deviations divided by a positive number, and squares cannot be negative. So the chi-squared distribution, which starts at zero, is the natural model.

Self-check: when should you use the normal instead of t?

When sigma is truly known, or when the sample is large enough that t and z give nearly the same answer.

The t-distribution: heavier tails and degrees of freedom

Shape

The t-distribution is symmetric around 0, bell-shaped, and has heavier tails than the normal. Its exact shape depends on one number, the degrees of freedom. With few degrees of freedom the tails are very heavy. With many degrees of freedom, the shape is almost identical to the normal.

Where degrees of freedom come from

Think of degrees of freedom as the number of values that are free to vary once you have estimated something. Suppose you have 5 numbers and you know their mean. Four of the numbers can be anything you like. The fifth is then forced by the mean. So you have 4 degrees of freedom. For one sample mean, the rule is df = n - 1.

For the sample 4, 6, 5, 7, 8, the mean is 6, and n - 1 = 4. You would use the t-distribution with 4 degrees of freedom.

Critical values

Table values give the cut-off t* such that P(T greater than t*) equals the tail probability. Two-sided 95 per cent intervals use the 97.5 per cent point, because 2.5 per cent sits in each tail. Some standard values for the two-sided 95 per cent point are:

Worked example: a t statistic

Take the sample 4, 6, 5, 7, 8. Test whether mu = 5.

Step 1: the mean is (4 + 6 + 5 + 7 + 8) / 5 = 30 / 5 = 6.

Step 2: the deviations are -2, 0, -1, 1, 2. Their squares are 4, 0, 1, 1, 4, which add to 10.

Step 3: the sample variance is s squared = 10 / (5 - 1) = 2.5, so s = 1.5811.

Step 4: the standard error is s / square root of n = 1.5811 / 2.2361 = 0.7071.

Step 5: the t statistic is (6 - 5) / 0.7071 = 1.4142, with df = 4.

Step 6: the two-sided 5 per cent critical value for df = 4 is 2.776. Since 1.4142 is smaller, we do not reject mu = 5 at the 5 per cent level.

TrapDo not use df = n instead of n - 1 for a single mean. Using the wrong df changes the critical value and gives a wrong conclusion.

TipAs df grows, the t critical value falls toward 1.96. For df above about 30, the difference from the normal is small.

Self-check: n = 10. What df do you use for a t interval for one mean?

df = 10 - 1 = 9.

Self-check: why does the t distribution have heavier tails than the normal?

Because s is an estimate, and sometimes s is small by chance, which makes the t ratio large. This extra variability appears as heavier tails.

The chi-squared distribution: sums of squares

The definition

Take k independent standard normal values Z1, Z2, ..., Zk. Square each one and add them:

chi-squared with k df = Z1 squared + Z2 squared + ... + Zk squared

The result is chi-squared with k degrees of freedom. Its average is k, and its variance is 2k. Because it is built from squares, it is never negative, and its shape is skewed to the right. The larger k is, the more symmetric the shape becomes.

Why it matters for variances

If the population is normal with variance sigma squared, then the quantity (n - 1) s squared / sigma squared follows a chi-squared distribution with n - 1 degrees of freedom. This is the key formula for testing a variance:

chi-squared statistic = (n - 1) times s squared / sigma squared, with df = n - 1

Worked example

Using the same sample 4, 6, 5, 7, 8, we have n - 1 = 4 and s squared = 2.5. Suppose we want to test whether sigma squared = 4.

Step 1: the statistic is 4 times 2.5 / 4 = 2.5.

Step 2: the chi-squared distribution has df = 4. From a table, the upper 5 per cent point for df = 4 is 9.488.

Step 3: since 2.5 is smaller than 9.488, the statistic is not in the upper 5 per cent tail, so we do not reject sigma squared = 4 at the 5 per cent level in that direction.

Key ideaThe chi-squared statistic is always zero or positive. Large values signal that the sample variance is large compared with the hypothesised variance.

TrapChi-squared tables often give upper-tail areas. A small chi-squared value falls in the lower tail. Read the table heading carefully before you compare.

Self-check: what are the mean and variance of a chi-squared with 6 df?

The mean is 6 and the variance is 12.

Self-check: is it possible for a chi-squared statistic to be negative?

No. It is a sum of squares, so it cannot be negative.

Reading tables and choosing between z, t and chi-squared

A decision guide

  • Mean, sigma known: use z.
  • Mean, sigma unknown: use t with n - 1 degrees of freedom.
  • Variance or standard deviation of one sample: use chi-squared with n - 1 degrees of freedom.
  • Counts compared with expected counts (goodness-of-fit): use chi-squared with the number of categories minus the number of estimated parameters minus 1.

How to read the tables

Three things to check before you read a number. First, is the table one-sided or two-sided? Second, which degrees of freedom apply? Third, is the question asking for the cut-off or the probability? For a two-sided 5 per cent test, look up the 2.5 per cent point in each tail, or the 0.975 point in a table of cumulative probabilities.

GATE loves thisA common question gives a sample size and asks which distribution to use. Remember: small sample plus unknown sigma means t. A variance question means chi-squared.

Self-check: a test about a population variance with 20 observations. Which distribution and df?

Chi-squared with 19 degrees of freedom.

Self-check: n = 4 and sigma unknown. Which distribution and df for the mean?

t with 3 degrees of freedom.

A Python check

This program computes the sample mean, the sample variance, the t statistic and the chi-squared statistic for the worked example. Each line prints one of the values used above.

import statistics as st
import math

data = [4, 6, 5, 7, 8]
n = len(data)
xbar = st.mean(data)
s2 = st.variance(data)
t_stat = (xbar - 5) / (math.sqrt(s2) / math.sqrt(n))
print(f"mean = {xbar} | sample variance = {s2}")
print(f"t statistic = {round(t_stat, 4)} | df = {n - 1}")
chi = (n - 1) * s2 / 4
print(f"chi-squared statistic = {chi} | df = {n - 1}")

mean = 6 | sample variance = 2.5
t statistic = 1.4142 | df = 4
chi-squared statistic = 2.5 | df = 4

The output matches the hand calculations: the t statistic is square root of 2, and the chi-squared statistic is 2.5.

Degrees of freedom

A sample of 12 values is used to estimate one mean with the t-distribution. What are the degrees of freedom?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The df for a t statistic.
  2. Which idea applies. For one sample mean, df = n - 1.
  3. Work it out. df = 12 - 1 = 11.
  4. Check. df is always smaller than the sample size for one mean.

Answer: 11

Shape of the chi-squared distribution

Which of the following is true of a chi-squared distribution?

  • (A) It is symmetric around zero.
  • (B) It can take negative values.
  • (C) It is skewed to the right and never negative.
  • (D) Its mean is always 1.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The correct description of the shape.
  2. Which idea applies. Chi-squared is a sum of squares.
  3. Work it out. Squares cannot be negative, so (B) is false. The distribution is skewed right, so (A) is false. Its mean is k, so (D) is false.
  4. Check. Only (C) matches the definition.

Answer: (C)

t statistic for a small sample

A sample of n = 9 has mean 52 and sample SD 6. Test H0: mu = 50. Find the t statistic. (Give a decimal to 3 places.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The t statistic.
  2. Which idea applies. T = (x-bar - mu) / (s / square root of n).
  3. Work it out. s / square root of 9 = 6 / 3 = 2. T = (52 - 50) / 2 = 1.000.
  4. Check. A t of 1 means the sample mean is one standard error above the hypothesised value.

Answer: 1.000

Select all that apply. Which of the following situations call for the t-distribution?

  • (B) A test of a variance from a sample of 20.
  • (C) A confidence interval for a mean with sigma known to be 3.

  1. What is asked. Which cases use t.
  2. Which idea applies. t is for a mean when sigma is unknown. Chi-squared is for variances. z is for a mean when sigma is known.

Chi-squared statistic for a variance

A sample of n = 11 has sample variance s squared = 16. Test whether sigma squared = 10. Find the chi-squared statistic and its degrees of freedom. (Give the statistic as a decimal.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The chi-squared statistic and df.
  2. Which idea applies. chi-squared = (n - 1) s squared / sigma squared, with df = n - 1.
  3. Work it out. df = 10. Statistic = 10 times 16 / 10 = 16.0.
  4. Check. The sample variance is larger than the hypothesised value, so the statistic is above its mean of 10, as expected.

Answer: 16.0, with 10 degrees of freedom

Mean and variance of a sum of squares

Let W = Z1 squared + Z2 squared + Z3 squared + Z4 squared + Z5 squared + Z6 squared, where each Z is independent and standard normal. Find the mean and the variance of W. (Give both as whole numbers.)

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The mean and variance of a chi-squared with 6 df.
  2. Which idea applies. Chi-squared with k df has mean k and variance 2k.
  3. Work it out. Mean = 6. Variance = 2 times 6 = 12.
  4. Check. For one squared normal, the mean is 1 and the variance is 2, which is the k = 1 case of the rule.

Answer: mean 6, variance 12

Exam-level 1: Lower limit for sigma (NAT, 2 marks)

A lab measures 10 samples of a chemical and finds a sample variance of 16 (units squared), so n - 1 = 9. The chi-squared table gives the value 19.023 (2.5 percent of the distribution lies above it) and the value 2.700 (2.5 percent lies below it), both for 9 degrees of freedom. Assume the measurements are normal. Find the lower limit of the 95 percent confidence interval for the population standard deviation sigma, to 3 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The lower limit for sigma, which is the square root of the lower limit for sigma squared.
  2. Which idea applies. The chi-squared interval for a variance: [(n - 1) s^2 / upper value, (n - 1) s^2 / lower value].
  3. Work it out. (n - 1) s^2 = 9 times 16 = 144. The lower limit for sigma squared is 144 / 19.023 = 7.570. The upper limit is 144 / 2.700 = 53.333. Taking the square root of the lower limit gives sigma at least 2.751.
  4. Check. The interval for the variance, 7.570 to 53.333, is skewed to the right around s^2 = 16, as chi-squared intervals usually are, because the lower end is 8.43 below 16 and the upper end is 37.3 above it.

Answer: 2.751

import math
n, s2 = 10, 16
chi_upper, chi_lower = 19.023, 2.700
lower_var = (n - 1) * s2 / chi_upper
upper_var = (n - 1) * s2 / chi_lower
print(f"CI for variance: {lower_var:.3f} to {upper_var:.3f}")
print(f"lower limit for sigma = {math.sqrt(lower_var):.3f}")
CI for variance: 7.570 to 53.333
lower limit for sigma = 2.751

Exam-level 2: t statistic for a loaf claim (NAT, 2 marks)

A bakery claims its loaves weigh 20 grams on average. A random sample of 12 loaves has mean weight 21.5 grams and sample standard deviation 3.2 grams. Assume the weights are roughly normal, and that the population standard deviation is unknown. Compute the t statistic for testing the claim against a two-sided alternative, to 2 decimal places. The two-sided 5 percent critical value for 11 degrees of freedom is 2.201.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The t statistic, then a decision at the 5 percent level.
  2. Which idea applies. One-sample t-test: t = (sample mean - claimed mean) / (s / square root of n), with n - 1 degrees of freedom.
  3. Work it out. The standard error is 3.2 / square root of 12 = 0.9238. Then t = (21.5 - 20) / 0.9238 = 1.62, with 11 degrees of freedom.
  4. Check. The statistic must be larger than 2.201 in size to reject. Since 1.62 is smaller, the claim is not rejected at the 5 percent level.

Answer: t = 1.62 (do not reject the claim at the 5 percent level)

import math
n, xbar, s, mu0 = 12, 21.5, 3.2, 20
t = (xbar - mu0) / (s / math.sqrt(n))
print(f"df = {n - 1}")
print(f"t = {round(t, 2)}")
print(f"reject at 5% two-sided? {abs(t) > 2.201}")
df = 11
t = 1.62
reject at 5% two-sided? False

Exam-level 3: Which t and chi-squared facts hold (MSQ, 2 marks)

Select all that apply. These statements compare the t distribution, the standard normal distribution and the chi-squared distribution.

  • (A) The t distribution has heavier tails than the standard normal, and it gets closer to the normal as the degrees of freedom grow.
  • (B) A chi-squared random variable is never negative.
  • (C) With 1 degree of freedom, the t distribution is the same as the standard normal.
  • (D) A chi-squared distribution with k degrees of freedom has mean k and variance 2k.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which facts about these three distributions are correct.
  2. Which idea applies. The shape of the t distribution depends on its degrees of freedom. A chi-squared variable is a sum of squares of standard normals.
  3. Work it out. (A) True: heavy tails shrink towards the normal as df increases. (B) True: it is a sum of squares, so it cannot be negative. (C) False: with 1 degree of freedom the t distribution has very heavy tails, and its 97.5 percent point is 12.706, against 1.96 for the normal. (D) True: the mean of a chi-squared with k degrees of freedom is k and its variance is 2k.
  4. Check. The Python block compares the table values with 1.96 and shows how the gap closes as the degrees of freedom grow.

Answer: (A), (B) and (D). Select all that apply.

t_crit = {1: 12.706, 5: 2.571, 30: 2.042}
z = 1.960
for df, t in t_crit.items():
    print(f"df = {df}: t = {t}, excess over z = {round(t - z, 3)}")
df = 1: t = 12.706, excess over z = 10.746
df = 5: t = 2.571, excess over z = 0.611
df = 30: t = 2.042, excess over z = 0.082

Exam-level 4: Chi-squared fit of a die (NAT, 2 marks)

A die is rolled 60 times and the counts for faces 1 to 6 are 5, 8, 9, 8, 10 and 20. If the die is fair, each face is expected to appear 10 times. Compute the chi-squared goodness-of-fit statistic, which is the sum of (observed minus expected) squared divided by expected, to 1 decimal place. The 5 percent critical value for 5 degrees of freedom is 11.070. State whether the fair-die model is rejected.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The chi-squared statistic, then a decision at the 5 percent level.
  2. Which idea applies. The goodness-of-fit statistic sums (O - E) squared / E over all categories, with 6 - 1 = 5 degrees of freedom.
  3. Work it out. Each expected count is 10. The contributions are (5 - 10)^2 / 10 = 2.5, (8 - 10)^2 / 10 = 0.4, (9 - 10)^2 / 10 = 0.1, (8 - 10)^2 / 10 = 0.4, (10 - 10)^2 / 10 = 0 and (20 - 10)^2 / 10 = 10. They add to 13.4. Since 13.4 is greater than 11.070, the fair-die model is rejected.
  4. Check. Most of the statistic comes from face 6, which appeared 20 times, twice its expected count, so the result is driven by one face.

Answer: chi-squared = 13.4, which is greater than 11.070, so the fair-die model is rejected

observed = [5, 8, 9, 8, 10, 20]
expected = sum(observed) / len(observed)
chi2 = sum((o - expected) ** 2 / expected for o in observed)
print(f"expected count per face = {expected}")
print(f"chi-squared = {round(chi2, 1)}")
print(f"df = {len(observed) - 1}")
print(f"reject at 5%? {chi2 > 11.070}")
expected count per face = 10.0
chi-squared = 13.4
df = 5
reject at 5%? True

Testing of materials: UTM, hardness & impact strength

Before a designer trusts a material, the material is tested. A tensile test pulls a sample until it breaks, hardness tests press a hard point into the surface, and impact tests strike a notched bar to see how much energy it absorbs. Knowing which test measures which property is a common GATE conceptual topic in Robotics and Automation.

The idea in plain words

Welcome. Imagine you are choosing a material for a robot gripper finger. You need to know how much force it can take before it bends, how far it stretches before it snaps, how hard its surface is, and whether it cracks suddenly when hit. You cannot answer all of these by looking at the material. So engineers test it in a machine, under controlled conditions, and measure numbers.

Here is an everyday picture. Think about a biscuit, a rubber band and a glass. Each one behaves differently when you press or pull it. The biscuit snaps quickly, the rubber band stretches a lot before it breaks, and the glass shatters without warning. Material tests put numbers on these behaviours. The tensile test is like pulling the rubber band. The hardness test is like pressing a pin into the surface. The impact test is like hitting the glass with a hammer and seeing how much energy it takes to break it.

Why does this matter for GATE? In Robotics and Automation (RA) papers, you are expected to know what each test measures, how to read a stress-strain curve, and how to convert a measurement into a number like a yield strength or a hardness value. Many GATE questions are conceptual. They ask which test to use for a given property, or which property a graph shows. Others are numerical, such as finding a percent elongation or a Brinell hardness number from a formula. This page covers both.

The plan: we first meet the universal testing machine and the tensile test. Next we read the stress-strain curve, which is the main output. Then we look at hardness and impact tests. Finally we compare the tests, and solve numericals.

Key terms

Read this list now, and come back to it whenever a word feels unfamiliar. Each entry gives the plain meaning first, then the precise definition.

Specimen
A small sample of the material prepared for a test. Plain meaning: the piece you test. Precise definition: a standard-shaped piece of the material with known dimensions, used in a test.
Gauge length (L0)
The length of the central part of a tensile specimen over which the elongation is measured. Plain meaning: the marked part you stretch. Precise definition: the original length between two reference marks on the specimen.
Universal testing machine (UTM)
A machine that pulls or squeezes a specimen and records the force and the change in length. Plain meaning: the machine that does the pulling. Precise definition: a testing machine that applies controlled tensile or compressive load and measures force and displacement.
Engineering stress (sigma)
Force divided by the original area. Plain meaning: the force per unit of original area. Precise definition: sigma = F / A0, in MPa (N/mm²).
Engineering strain (epsilon)
Change in length divided by the original length. Plain meaning: the stretch as a fraction of the original length. Precise definition: epsilon = (L − L0) / L0, a number with no unit.
Proportional limit
The highest stress up to which stress and strain are proportional. Plain meaning: the point where the straight-line behaviour ends. Precise definition: the stress at which the stress-strain curve stops being a straight line.
Elastic region
The part of the curve where the material returns to its original length on unloading. Plain meaning: the stretch that springs back. Precise definition: the region below the yield point where strain is fully recovered.
Young's modulus (E)
The slope of the straight part of the stress-strain curve. Plain meaning: the stiffness of the material. Precise definition: E = sigma / epsilon in the elastic range.
Yield strength (sigmay)
The stress at which the material starts to deform permanently. Plain meaning: the point where the material gives in. Precise definition: the stress at the upper yield point, or for materials without a clear yield, the stress at 0.2 percent offset strain.
0.2 percent offset yield
A yield definition for materials with no clear yield point. Plain meaning: draw a parallel line from a small strain and see where it cuts the curve. Precise definition: the stress where a line parallel to the elastic slope, starting at 0.002 strain, meets the stress-strain curve.
Ultimate tensile strength (UTS)
The highest engineering stress reached during the test. Plain meaning: the strongest point on the curve. Precise definition: sigmaUTS = Fmax / A0.
Necking
The local narrowing of the specimen just before fracture. Plain meaning: the specimen gets thin in one spot. Precise definition: localised reduction in cross-section that begins after the UTS in ductile materials.
Percent elongation
The percentage increase in gauge length at fracture. Plain meaning: how much the specimen stretched before it broke, as a percentage. Precise definition: (Lf − L0) / L0 × 100.
Reduction of area
The percentage decrease in the cross-sectional area at the fracture. Plain meaning: how thin the neck became. Precise definition: (A0 − Af) / A0 × 100.
Ductile material
A material that stretches a lot before breaking. Plain meaning: it bends and stretches without snapping. Precise definition: a material with large elongation and reduction of area at fracture.
Brittle material
A material that breaks with little or no permanent stretch. Plain meaning: it snaps. Precise definition: a material with small elongation at fracture.
Hardness
The resistance of a surface to indentation or scratching. Plain meaning: how hard it is to press a dent into the surface. Precise definition: a measure obtained from the size or depth of an indent made by a hard indenter under a known load.
Indenter
The hard tip pressed into the surface in a hardness test. Plain meaning: the pointed or round tool that makes the dent. Precise definition: a ball, cone or pyramid of a harder material than the specimen.
Brinell hardness (HB)
A hardness number from a steel ball indenter. Plain meaning: a number from the size of a round dent. Precise definition: HB = 2F / (π D (D − √(D² − d²))), with F in kgf.
Rockwell hardness (HR)
A hardness number from the depth of penetration. Plain meaning: a number read directly from a dial. Precise definition: a hardness scale based on the extra depth of penetration of a cone or ball under a major and minor load.
Vickers hardness (HV)
A hardness number from a diamond square pyramid. Plain meaning: a number from the size of a square dent. Precise definition: HV = 1.8544 F / d², with F in kgf and d the mean diagonal in mm.
Impact strength
The energy absorbed by a notched specimen when it is broken by a sudden blow. Plain meaning: how much energy the material takes in before it snaps. Precise definition: the energy absorbed in fracture under impact, in joules.
Charpy test
An impact test with a simply supported notched bar struck at the back of the notch. Plain meaning: the bar lies on two supports and the hammer hits the middle. Precise definition: a pendulum impact test on a notched specimen supported at both ends.
Izod test
An impact test with a notched bar clamped as a cantilever. Plain meaning: the bar is clamped at one end and hit at the notch side. Precise definition: a pendulum impact test on a notched specimen held vertically at one end.
Notch
A small V-shaped cut in the specimen. Plain meaning: a sharp groove that makes cracks start easily. Precise definition: a stress raiser that concentrates stress and tests the material's sensitivity to cracking.
Toughness
The ability to absorb energy before fracture. Plain meaning: how much energy the material takes in. Precise definition: the area under the stress-strain curve up to fracture, or the energy absorbed in an impact test.

Concept 1: The universal testing machine and the tensile test

The universal testing machine is the workhorse of materials testing. Its name says "universal" because the same machine can pull (tension), squeeze (compression) and bend specimens, with the right grips and fixtures. For a tensile test, the specimen is held at both ends by grips, and the machine pulls it at a slow, steady rate. A load cell measures the force, and an extensometer or the crosshead displacement measures the change in length.

Here is a text picture of the set-up.

        Crosshead (moves up slowly)
           |
        [ grip ]          load cell measures force F
           |
      ____|____
     |         |
     |  test   |   gauge length L0 (the marked middle part)
     | specimen|
     |_________|
           |
        [ grip ]
           |
        Base (fixed)

  The machine pulls: F rises, the gauge length grows, and the
  load cell and extensometer record F and the change in length.

The tensile test records force against extension. To compare different specimens fairly, we convert these readings into stress and strain. The conversion uses the original size of the specimen:

sigma = F / A0 and epsilon = (L − L0) / L0

Let us say what each symbol means. F is the force, in N. A0 is the original cross-sectional area, in mm². L0 is the original gauge length, in mm. L is the current gauge length, in mm. Stress is in MPa (N/mm²), and strain is a pure number. These are called engineering stress and strain, because they use the original size.

Worked example. A round specimen has a diameter of 10 mm and an original gauge length of 50 mm. At the peak, the force is 30 kN. Find the engineering stress at the peak.

  1. Find the area: A0 = π × 10² / 4 = π × 25 = 78.54 mm².
  2. Convert the force to newtons: 30 kN = 30 000 N.
  3. Divide: sigma = 30 000 / 78.54 = 382.0 MPa.
  4. So the ultimate tensile strength in this example is 382 MPa.
import math
L0 = 50.0
Lf = 58.5
d0 = 10.0
df = 6.0
Fmax = 30000
A0 = math.pi * d0**2 / 4
Af = math.pi * df**2 / 4
print("A0 =", round(A0, 2), "mm^2")
print("UTS =", round(Fmax / A0, 1), "MPa")
print("elongation =", round((Lf - L0) / L0 * 100, 1), "percent")
print("reduction of area =", round((A0 - Af) / A0 * 100, 1), "percent")

A0 = 78.54 mm^2
UTS = 382.0 MPa
elongation = 17.0 percent
reduction of area = 64.0 percent

TrapUsing the current area or the current length in the formula. The standard engineering stress and strain use the original area and original gauge length. The true stress, which uses the actual area, is a different quantity and is not usually asked in the basic GATE question.

Question 1: Why do we convert force and extension into stress and strain?

So that specimens of different sizes can be compared fairly. Stress and strain describe the material, while force and extension depend on the size of the specimen.

Question 2: Why is the test pulled at a slow, steady rate?

So that the measurements reflect the material's behaviour under steady loading, rather than the effects of sudden loading or heating caused by fast deformation.

Concept 2: Reading the stress-strain curve

The main output of the tensile test is the stress-strain curve. For a ductile metal such as mild steel, the curve has several distinct regions. Here is a text picture with the key points labelled. The horizontal axis is strain, and the vertical axis is stress.

  stress
    ^
    |                     UTS (highest point)
    |                    _.-*-._
    |                  .'       '.   necking
    |        yield   .'           '*  fracture
    |          *---*'
    |         /
    |        /   elastic slope = E (straight part)
    |       /
    |      /
    +------------------------------------------> strain

  Elastic region: straight line, the stretch springs back.
  Yield: the curve starts to flatten, permanent stretch begins.
  Plastic region: the stress keeps rising slowly, then the curve peaks at UTS.
  Necking: the specimen narrows locally, the force falls, and it breaks.

Here is what each part means.

  • The elastic region is the straight part at the start. Inside it, stress is proportional to strain, and the slope is Young's modulus E. If you unload here, the specimen returns to its original length.
  • The yield point is where the curve bends and permanent deformation starts. For some steels it is a sharp drop. For many metals, there is no clear point, so we use the 0.2 percent offset method: draw a line parallel to the elastic slope starting at a strain of 0.002, and take the stress where it cuts the curve.
  • The ultimate tensile strength (UTS) is the highest stress on the curve. Beyond this point the specimen starts to neck, and the force drops even though the strain keeps rising.
  • Fracture is the final break. The percent elongation and the reduction of area are measured after the specimen is put back together.

The two post-fracture numbers are defined as:

Percent elongation = (Lf − L0) / L0 × 100, and reduction of area = (A0 − Af) / A0 × 100

Here Lf is the gauge length after fracture, and Af is the area at the neck after fracture. A large elongation and reduction of area mean a ductile material. Small values mean a brittle material.

Worked example. A specimen with gauge length L0 = 50 mm measures Lf = 58.5 mm after fracture. Find its percent elongation.

  1. Find the extension: 58.5 − 50 = 8.5 mm.
  2. Divide by the original length: 8.5 / 50 = 0.17.
  3. Multiply by 100: 17 percent.

Key ideaThe stress-strain curve tells you four things: the stiffness (E), the yield strength (where permanent deformation starts), the UTS (the strongest point), and the ductility (how far it stretches before fracture).

TrapAssuming that UTS is the yield strength. UTS is the highest point on the curve, and it comes after yield. A design that uses UTS as the safe limit may permanently deform the part long before failure.

Question 3: What is the slope of the straight part of the stress-strain curve?

Young's modulus E. The stress and strain are proportional in this region.

Question 4: A material with 2 percent elongation at fracture is called what?

A brittle material, or at least a relatively brittle one. Ductile metals usually stretch much more than this before they break.

Concept 3: Hardness tests: Brinell, Rockwell and Vickers

A hardness test is quick and needs only a small indent. A hard indenter is pressed into the surface with a known load, and the size or depth of the dent is measured. The harder the material, the smaller the dent for the same load. The hardness number is a convenient index of the material's resistance to indentation. It is also a useful guide to strength, but it is not a strength itself.

There are three common methods. Each one measures a different feature of the dent.

  BRINELL: a steel ball is pressed in.  Measure the diameter d of the round dent.

      F (load)
      |
    ( ball D )          HB = 2F / [ pi D ( D - sqrt(D^2 - d^2) ) ]
      \_______/         F in kgf, D and d in mm
       d (dent)

  VICKERS: a diamond square pyramid is pressed in.  Measure the two diagonals.
      F
      |
     /\        HV = 1.8544 F / d^2   (F in kgf, d = mean diagonal in mm)
    /  \
    \  /
     \/

  ROCKWELL: a cone or ball is pressed in, then the extra depth is read on a dial.
      Depth of penetration gives the HR number directly. Fast and simple.

Let us check the Brinell formula with a small example. A 10 mm steel ball is pressed with F = 3000 kgf, and the dent diameter is d = 4.0 mm. Find HB.

  1. Find D² − d²: 100 − 16 = 84.
  2. Find the square root: √84 = 9.165.
  3. Find D − √(D² − d²): 10 − 9.165 = 0.835.
  4. Find π D: 3.1416 × 10 = 31.416.
  5. Multiply: 31.416 × 0.835 = 26.23.
  6. Divide: HB = 2 × 3000 / 26.23 = 6000 / 26.23 = 228.8.
import math
F = 3000
D = 10.0
d = 4.0
HB = 2 * F / (math.pi * D * (D - math.sqrt(D**2 - d**2)))
print("HB =", round(HB, 1))
F2 = 10
dv = 0.2
HV = 1.8544 * F2 / dv**2
print("HV =", round(HV, 1))

HB = 228.8
HV = 463.6

The Vickers formula uses a different geometry. The pyramid has a face angle of 136 degrees, and the constant 1.8544 comes from that angle. For Vickers, the diagonals are measured, and the mean of the two diagonals gives d. The Vickers test works for very thin samples and for hard materials, because the pyramid gives a sharp, well-defined dent at any load.

Rockwell is the fastest. The machine reads the hardness directly from a dial or a digital display. Rockwell C (HRC) uses a diamond cone and a heavy load, and is used for hard steels. Rockwell B (HRB) uses a steel ball and is used for softer metals. Because the scales are different, you must always quote the scale letter with the number.

GATE loves thisFor a hardness question, the key words are the indenter and the reading. Brinell uses a ball and measures the diameter. Vickers uses a diamond pyramid and measures diagonals. Rockwell reads depth of penetration directly. If a question asks which test measures depth, the answer is Rockwell.

TrapComparing HB and HRC numbers directly. They come from different scales and cannot be compared without a conversion table. Always compare hardness numbers that use the same scale.

Question 5: Which hardness test uses a diamond square pyramid?

The Vickers test. The indenter is a square-based pyramid with a face angle of 136 degrees.

Question 6: In the Brinell formula, what does d represent?

The diameter of the round dent left in the surface. D is the diameter of the steel ball.

Concept 4: Impact tests: Charpy and Izod

A tensile test is slow. Many parts, however, fail suddenly, when they are struck or when a crack runs through them. Impact tests measure how much energy a notched specimen absorbs when it is broken by a single fast blow. The result is the impact strength, in joules. A high value means the material is tough, and a low value means it is brittle.

The notch is a small V-shaped cut. It concentrates stress at its tip, just as a scratch on a glass makes it crack easily. So the impact test shows how sensitive a material is to cracks, which the tensile test does not.

There are two standard forms. They differ in how the specimen is held.

  CHARPY: simply supported, struck at the back of the notch

      pendulum hammer swings down
          \
           \
            O  hits here (opposite the notch)
            |
  ==========+=========        notched bar lying on two supports
  ^                   ^
  support        support

  IZOD: cantilever, clamped, struck at the notch side

      clamp
  ||=====================   notched bar held vertically at one end
  ||
  ||         hammer strikes the notch side
  ||

The energy absorbed is the energy the hammer loses. The pendulum starts from a raised height and swings through the specimen. After the break, it rises less. The energy absorbed is the drop in potential energy of the hammer.

Let the hammer weigh W, let its arm be R, let the release angle from the downward vertical be alpha, and let the angle reached after breaking be beta, with beta smaller than alpha. The energy absorbed is:

E = W × R × (cos β − cos α)

Each symbol has a plain meaning. W is the weight of the hammer, in N. R is the length of the pendulum arm, in m. alpha is the starting angle, and beta is the angle after breaking, both measured from the downward vertical. The cosine terms give the height difference, so E comes out in N m, which is joules.

Worked example. A pendulum hammer has W = 200 N and arm length R = 0.8 m. It is released from alpha = 150 degrees and rises to beta = 100 degrees after breaking the specimen. Find the energy absorbed.

  1. cos 100° = −0.17365.
  2. cos 150° = −0.86603.
  3. Difference: cos β − cos α = −0.17365 − (−0.86603) = 0.69238.
  4. Multiply: E = 200 × 0.8 × 0.69238 = 110.78 J.
import math
W = 200
R = 0.8
alpha = math.radians(150)
beta = math.radians(100)
E = W * R * (math.cos(beta) - math.cos(alpha))
print("energy =", round(E, 2), "J")

energy = 110.78 J

The impact test also shows the ductile-to-brittle transition. Many steels absorb a lot of energy at room temperature, but much less at low temperature. At that point they become brittle, and the value drops sharply over a narrow range of temperature. Designers in cold climates or cold-storage robots must use materials that stay tough at the lowest service temperature.

TrapConfusing the Charpy and Izod set-ups. Charpy: simply supported, struck at the back of the notch. Izod: clamped as a cantilever, struck at the notch side. The energy formula is the same for both.

Question 7: What does the notch do in an impact test?

It concentrates stress at its tip, so the test shows how easily a crack starts and spreads in the material.

Question 8: Does a larger drop in the hammer's height mean more or less energy absorbed?

More. The energy absorbed equals the height the hammer loses, times its weight. A bigger drop means the specimen took more energy.

Concept 5: Choosing the right test

Each test answers a different question. A simple way to remember them is to ask what you want to know.

Question to answerBest testWhat it gives
How stiff is it, and where does it yield?Tensile test (UTM)E, yield strength, UTS, elongation
How hard is its surface, quickly?Rockwell, Brinell or VickersHardness number
Can it take a sudden blow without cracking?Charpy or Izod impactEnergy absorbed (toughness)
Is it ductile or brittle?Tensile (elongation, reduction of area) or impactDuctility and toughness
Is the surface very thin or small?VickersHardness from a small dent

Notice that a tensile test gives the most information about strength and ductility, but it is slow and destructive. A hardness test is quick and can be done on a finished part. An impact test shows toughness and crack sensitivity, which neither of the other two shows.

TipWrite the question as "what property does this test measure?" before you read the options. For example, the impact test measures energy absorbed in fracture, not strength.

Question 9: A designer needs to know whether a steel part will crack suddenly in a cold room. Which test is most useful?

An impact test, such as the Charpy test at the service temperature. It shows the energy absorbed and the transition to brittle behaviour.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original GATE-style questions written for practice. They are not past exam questions. Try each one on paper first.

Which test measures which property

Which of the following statements about materials tests are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) The Brinell test uses a steel ball and reads the diameter of the dent.
(B) The Charpy test is a cantilever test with the notch on the side facing the hammer.
(C) The Rockwell test reads the depth of penetration.
(D) The Vickers test uses a diamond square pyramid.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct. This is an MSQ.
  2. Which idea applies. The indenter and reading of each hardness test, and the set-up of the impact tests.
  3. Work it out. (A) is true. (B) describes the Izod test, not the Charpy test, so it is false. (C) is true, because Rockwell reads depth. (D) is true, by definition.
  4. Check. Three statements are true and one is false. The false one mixes up Charpy and Izod, which is a common trap.

Answer: (A), (C) and (D) only.

Percent elongation

A tensile specimen has an original gauge length of 60 mm. After fracture, the two pieces are put together and the gauge length is 72 mm. What is the percent elongation? This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Percent elongation, a number with no units.
  2. Which idea applies. (Lf − L0) / L0 × 100.
  3. Work it out. The extension is 72 − 60 = 12 mm. Divide by 60: 12 / 60 = 0.2. Multiply by 100: 20 percent.
  4. Check. An extension of 12 mm on a 60 mm length is one fifth of the length, which is 20 percent.

Answer: 20 percent.

Brinell hardness of a dent

A 10 mm diameter steel ball is pressed into a steel surface with a load of 3000 kgf. The diameter of the round dent is 4.0 mm. Find the Brinell hardness number HB. Give the answer to one decimal place. This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. HB to one decimal place.
  2. Which idea applies. HB = 2F / (π D (D − √(D² − d²))).
  3. Work it out. D² − d² = 84, √84 = 9.165, D − 9.165 = 0.835. π D × 0.835 = 26.23. HB = 6000 / 26.23 = 228.8.
  4. Check. The Python check below prints 228.8, which agrees with the hand calculation.

Answer: 228.8.

Energy absorbed in a Charpy test

A Charpy pendulum weighs 300 N and has an arm of 0.6 m. It is released from alpha = 140 degrees and rises to beta = 100 degrees after breaking the specimen. Using E = W R (cos β − cos α), find the energy absorbed in joules to one decimal place. This is a NAT question.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The energy E in J.
  2. Which idea applies. E = W R (cos β − cos α).
  3. Work it out. cos 100° = −0.17365. cos 140° = −0.76604. Difference: −0.17365 + 0.76604 = 0.59239. Multiply: 300 × 0.6 × 0.59239 = 180 × 0.59239 = 106.63 J.
  4. Check. The energy is positive, and it is smaller than the initial potential energy, which is W R (1 − cos 140°) = 300 × 0.6 × 1.76604 = 317.9 J. So the answer lies in the right range.

Answer: 106.6 J (about 106.6 J when rounded to one decimal place).

Which property shows toughness

A designer wants to compare two steels for a part that may be hit suddenly in service. Which of the following quantities best describes the toughness of each steel? Select all that apply.

(A) The energy absorbed in a Charpy impact test.
(B) The Brinell hardness number.
(C) The area under the tensile stress-strain curve up to fracture.
(D) The Young's modulus.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which quantities describe toughness. This is an MSQ.
  2. Which idea applies. Toughness is the ability to absorb energy before fracture.
  3. Work it out. (A) is energy absorbed in fracture, so it is a toughness measure. (B) is a surface indentation number, not toughness. (C) is the area under the curve, which is the energy per unit volume up to fracture, so it measures toughness. (D) is stiffness, not toughness.
  4. Check. Only the energy-based measures, (A) and (C), describe toughness.

Answer: (A) and (C) only.

Yield strength by the 0.2 percent offset

A tensile specimen has elastic modulus E = 200 000 MPa. Its stress-strain curve has no clear yield point. A line drawn parallel to the elastic slope, starting at a strain of 0.002, cuts the curve at a stress of 300 MPa. Find the yield strength of the material, using the 0.2 percent offset definition, in MPa. Then find the strain in the elastic part at that stress and state whether the elastic strain is larger or smaller than 0.002. This is a NAT question for the yield value.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The yield strength in MPa, using the offset definition.
  2. Which idea applies. The 0.2 percent offset yield is the stress where the offset line meets the curve.
  3. Work it out. The stress where the offset line meets the curve is given as 300 MPa, so the yield strength is 300 MPa. The elastic strain at that stress, from the slope, is 300 / 200 000 = 0.0015. This is smaller than 0.002, which is the offset strain.
  4. Check. The offset strain is greater than the elastic strain at yield, because the offset includes the permanent set. The numbers are consistent with that picture.

Answer: 300 MPa. The elastic strain at yield is 0.0015, which is smaller than the offset strain of 0.002, as it must be.

Comparing two specimens

Two round specimens of the same material are tested. Specimen 1 has an original diameter of 10 mm and a gauge length of 50 mm. Specimen 2 has an original diameter of 20 mm and a gauge length of 100 mm. Both fracture at the same engineering stress, which is the UTS. Which statements are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) The forces at fracture are in the ratio 1 to 4.
(B) The percent elongation of both specimens is the same, if they stretch by the same strain.
(C) The UTS of specimen 2 is four times that of specimen 1.
(D) The engineering stress at fracture is the same for both.

Select all that apply.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statements are correct. This is an MSQ.
  2. Which idea applies. Stress is force over original area, and strain is extension over original length, so both are size-independent.
  3. Work it out. Since the stress at fracture is the same, F = sigma × A. The area is proportional to D², so the forces are in the ratio 100 : 400, which is 1 to 4. So (A) is true. Percent elongation depends on strain, so with the same strain it is the same: (B) is true. The UTS is a stress, which is the same for both, so (C) is false and (D) is true.
  4. Check. Force scales with area, which is the square of diameter. A doubling in diameter gives four times the force, which fits (A).

Answer: (A), (B) and (D) only.

Hardness scale reading

A technician reads a hardness value of 60 on the Rockwell C scale for a hardened steel. Which statement is correct? Select one.

(A) The value 60 can be compared directly with a Brinell number of 60.
(B) The value 60 is a Rockwell C hardness and must be quoted with its scale letter.
(C) The value 60 is a Vickers hardness in HV.
(D) The value 60 is the energy absorbed in joules.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which statement about the reading is correct.
  2. Which idea applies. Each hardness scale has its own letter, and numbers from different scales cannot be compared directly.
  3. Work it out. (A) is wrong, because scales differ. (B) is right: the value is an HRC reading and needs the letter. (C) is wrong, because it is not a Vickers reading. (D) is wrong, because it is a hardness reading, not an energy.
  4. Check. Only (B) matches the rule about scale letters.

Answer: (B).

import math
alpha = math.radians(140)
beta = math.radians(100)
E = 300 * 0.6 * (math.cos(beta) - math.cos(alpha))
print("energy =", round(E, 1), "J")

energy = 106.6 J

Exam-level 1: Brinell hardness (NAT, 2 marks)

A 3000 kgf load with a 10 mm ball gives an indentation of 4 mm diameter. Find the BHN, correct to 1 decimal place.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. BHN = 2P/(πD(D − √(D² − d²))).
  2. Which idea applies. P in kgf, D and d in mm.
  3. Work it out. √(100 − 16) = 9.165. D − 9.165 = 0.835. 6000/(π · 10 · 0.835) = 228.8.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
import math
P, D, d = 3000, 10, 4
print("%.1f" % (2 * P / (math.pi * D * (D - math.sqrt(D * D - d * d)))))

228.8

Answer: about 228.8.

Exam-level 2: Tensile test (NAT, 2 marks)

A specimen of gauge length 50 mm and diameter 10 mm breaks at 62 mm with a neck diameter of 7 mm. Find the percentage elongation and the reduction in area.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Two ductility measures.
  2. Which idea applies. Elongation = ΔL/L. Reduction in area = (A0 − Af)/A0.
  3. Work it out. 12/50 = 24%. 1 − 0.49 = 51%.
  4. Check. Both indicate a ductile material.

Answer: 24% and 51%.

Exam-level 3: Charpy energy (NAT, 2 marks)

A Charpy pendulum of mass 20 kg falls from 1.5 m and rises to 0.6 m after breaking the specimen. Take g = 9.81. Find the absorbed energy in J.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. mg(h1 − h2).
  2. Which idea applies. Friction is ignored.
  3. Work it out. 20 · 9.81 · 0.9 = 176.6 J.
  4. Check. A brittle sample would let the pendulum rise higher.

Answer: about 176.6 J.

Summary

Universal testing machine
Machine that applies and measures load. Precise definition: pulls or squeezes a specimen while recording force and extension.
Engineering stress
Force over original area. Precise definition: sigma = F / A0.
Engineering strain
Extension over original length. Precise definition: epsilon = (L − L0) / L0.
Young's modulus
Slope of the elastic part. Precise definition: E = sigma / epsilon.
Yield strength
Stress where permanent deformation starts. Precise definition: upper yield or 0.2 percent offset.
Ultimate tensile strength
Highest engineering stress. Precise definition: Fmax / A0.
Percent elongation
Ductility measure. Precise definition: (Lf − L0) / L0 × 100.
Reduction of area
Ductility measure at the neck. Precise definition: (A0 − Af) / A0 × 100.
Ductile
Stretches a lot before breaking. Precise definition: large elongation and reduction of area.
Brittle
Breaks with little stretch. Precise definition: small elongation at fracture.
Brinell hardness
Ball indenter, dent diameter. Precise definition: HB = 2F / (π D (D − √(D² − d²))).
Rockwell hardness
Depth of penetration read directly. Precise definition: HR with a scale letter.
Vickers hardness
Diamond pyramid, diagonal. Precise definition: HV = 1.8544 F / d².
Charpy test
Simply supported notched bar, struck at the back. Precise definition: pendulum impact test.
Izod test
Cantilever notched bar, struck at the notch. Precise definition: pendulum impact test.
Toughness
Energy absorbed before fracture. Precise definition: area under the curve or impact energy.

Remember these

  1. Engineering stress and strain use the original area and original gauge length.
  2. The elastic slope is Young's modulus E.
  3. UTS is the highest point, after yield, not the yield strength.
  4. Percent elongation and reduction of area measure ductility.
  5. Brinell uses a ball and dent diameter, Vickers uses a diamond pyramid, Rockwell reads depth.
  6. Impact energy is E = W R (cos β − cos α) for a pendulum test.
  7. Charpy is simply supported and struck at the back of the notch; Izod is a cantilever struck at the notch.
  8. Do not compare hardness numbers from different scales.

Hypothesis testing basics, PDF & CDF (recap)

Hypothesis testing is the formal way to decide whether data support a claim, and the PDF and CDF are the two functions that describe how a continuous random variable spreads its probability. This recap covers the test setup, the p-value and its errors, and how the PDF and CDF are linked.

The idea in plain words

Suppose a factory claims its bulbs last 50 hours on average. You test 25 bulbs and find an average of 52 hours. Is that evidence the claim is wrong, or is it just normal variation? Hypothesis testing gives a fair way to decide.

The method is like a court trial. The starting assumption is "innocent until proven guilty". Here the starting assumption is the claim, and we only reject it if the evidence is strong enough. A result that is merely different is not enough. The evidence must be unlikely under the claim.

The PDF and CDF are the tools that describe the probabilities for a continuous variable such as a bulb's life. The PDF gives the shape, and the CDF gives the running total of probability. You need both to find p-values, which are the core of most GATE statistics questions in RA.

Key terms

Null hypothesis (H0)
Plain meaning: the claim we assume is true until the data say otherwise. Precise definition: the statement, such as μ = 50, that is tested.
Alternative hypothesis (H1)
Plain meaning: what we accept if the claim looks wrong. Precise definition: the statement that is true if H0 is false, such as μ ≠ 50 or μ > 50.
Test statistic
Plain meaning: one number that measures how far the data are from H0. Precise definition: a function of the data whose distribution is known when H0 is true, such as Z.
Significance level (α)
Plain meaning: how rare a result must be before we reject H0. Precise definition: the largest acceptable probability of rejecting H0 when it is true.
p-value
Plain meaning: how surprising the data would be if H0 were true. Precise definition: the probability, assuming H0, of a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed.
Type I error
Plain meaning: a false alarm, rejecting a true H0. Precise definition: the event of rejecting H0 when H0 is true. Its probability is α.
Type II error
Plain meaning: a miss, failing to reject a false H0. Precise definition: the event of not rejecting H0 when H1 is true. Its probability is β.
Probability density function (PDF)
Plain meaning: the height of the probability curve for a continuous variable. Precise definition: f(x) with f(x) ≥ 0 and total area 1. Probabilities are areas under f.
Cumulative distribution function (CDF)
Plain meaning: the probability of getting a value up to x. Precise definition: F(x) = P(X ≤ x).

Part 1: Hypotheses and the test

Every hypothesis test has the same steps. First, state the claim you are testing as H0. Second, state H1, the alternative. Third, choose α before looking at the data. Fourth, compute the test statistic. Fifth, decide.

The alternative can be two-sided or one-sided:

  • Two-sided: H1: μ ≠ 50. We care about a change in either direction.
  • One-sided: H1: μ > 50. We only care about an increase.

For a sample mean with known σ, the Z test statistic is:

Z = (x̄ − μ0) / (σ/√n)
  • x̄ is the sample mean.
  • μ0 is the value of the mean claimed by H0.
  • σ/√n is the standard error, the spread of the sample mean.

A worked example, every step shown

A claim says the mean life of a bulb is μ0 = 50 hours. A sample of n = 25 bulbs has mean x̄ = 52. The population standard deviation is σ = 10. Test H0: μ = 50 against the two-sided H1: μ ≠ 50 at α = 0.05.

Step 1: Standard error. σ/√n = 10/√25 = 10/5 = 2.

Step 2: Statistic. Z = (52 − 50)/2 = 1.

Step 3: p-value. For a two-sided test, p = 2 × P(Z ≥ 1) = 2 × (1 − Φ(1)) = 2 × (1 − 0.8413) = 0.3174 with the rounded table value. With the more precise Φ(1) = 0.84134, the answer is 0.3173.

Step 4: Decide. 0.317 is much larger than 0.05, so we do not reject H0. The data are consistent with a mean of 50.

import math

def Phi(z):
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2)))

xbar, mu0, sigma, n = 52, 50, 10, 25
se = sigma / math.sqrt(n)
z = (xbar - mu0) / se
p_two = 2 * (1 - Phi(abs(z)))
print("Standard error =", se)
print("Z =", z)
print("Two-sided p-value =", round(p_two, 4))
Standard error = 2.0
Z = 1.0
Two-sided p-value = 0.3173

TrapDo not use σ in the test if the question gives you only the sample standard deviation, unless the question says to. Read the wording carefully before you pick a formula.

What is the standard error when σ = 6 and n = 36?

σ/√n = 6/6 = 1.

Why is the alternative stated before looking at the data?

Because choosing a one-sided or two-sided alternative after seeing the data lets you pick the direction that makes the result look significant. That is cheating, so the choice must be fixed in advance.

Part 2: p-values, significance and errors

The p-value is the chance, if H0 were true, of getting a result at least as far from the claim as the one we saw. Small p-values mean the data are surprising under H0.

Reject H0 if p-value ≤ α

For a one-sided test, the p-value uses one tail only. For Z = 1.645, the upper-tail probability is 1 − Φ(1.645) ≈ 0.05, so a one-sided test at α = 0.05 is at the boundary.

Two kinds of error

Any decision can be wrong in two ways:

H0 is trueH0 is false
Reject H0Type I error, probability αCorrect, probability 1 − β (the power)
Do not reject H0CorrectType II error, probability β

Lowering α makes Type I errors rarer, but it makes Type II errors more likely. Increasing the sample size makes both kinds easier to avoid. Everyday picture: a smoke alarm set very sensitive will go off for toast (a false alarm, Type I), while one set very loose may miss a real fire (Type II).

Key ideaA p-value is not the probability that H0 is true. It is a probability computed assuming H0 is true.

Trap"Not rejected" does not mean "accepted". It only means the data were not surprising enough.

A test at α = 0.05 is run on 1000 situations where H0 is really true. About how many false rejections do you expect?

About 0.05 × 1000 = 50 false rejections, since each test has probability α of a Type I error.

What is the power of a test with β = 0.2?

Power = 1 − β = 0.8.

Part 3: The PDF of a continuous variable

A continuous variable can take any value in a range, such as a bulb's life in hours. For such a variable, the chance of exactly one value is zero. We can only talk about the chance of an interval.

The probability density function f(x) describes the shape. Probabilities are areas under the curve:

P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫ from a to b of f(x) dx

Two rules must hold for any valid PDF: f(x) ≥ 0 everywhere, and the total area is 1, so ∫ over all x of f(x) dx = 1. The second rule is the one to check first when a question gives a density.

A worked example, every step shown

Let X have density f(x) = 1/2 for 0 ≤ x ≤ 2, and f(x) = 0 otherwise. This is the uniform distribution on [0, 2].

Step 1: Check the total area. The shape is a rectangle with height 1/2 and width 2, so its area is 1/2 × 2 = 1. Valid.

Step 2: Find P(0.5 ≤ X ≤ 1.5). The area is ∫ from 0.5 to 1.5 of (1/2) dx = (1/2) × (1.5 − 0.5) = (1/2) × 1 = 0.5.

Step 3: Check. The interval has length 1 and the whole range has length 2, so half the area sits in it. 0.5 is right.

TrapThe PDF value f(x) is not a probability. It can be bigger than 1. For the uniform on [0, 0.5], the height is 2, which is perfectly valid.

Find k so that f(x) = kx on [0, 2] is a valid PDF.

The area is ∫ from 0 to 2 of kx dx = k × [x²/2] from 0 to 2 = k × 2 = 2k. Setting this to 1 gives k = 1/2.

Can a PDF have f(x) = 3 for some x?

Yes. A PDF can exceed 1 at some points, provided the total area is still 1 and it is never negative.

Part 4: The CDF and its link to the PDF

The CDF is the running total. It gives the probability that X is at most x.

F(x) = P(X ≤ x) = ∫ from −∞ to x of f(t) dt
  • F(x) is between 0 and 1.
  • F never decreases as x increases.
  • F(−∞) = 0 and F(∞) = 1.
  • Going back, f(x) = F′(x), the slope of the CDF, wherever the CDF is smooth.

Using the CDF, any interval probability becomes a subtraction: P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = F(b) − F(a). This is much quicker than integrating every time.

A worked example, every step shown

Using the uniform on [0, 2] again, the CDF is F(x) = 0 for x < 0, F(x) = x/2 for 0 ≤ x ≤ 2, and F(x) = 1 for x > 2. Check that F rises from 0 to 1 over [0, 2]: the slope is 1/2, which matches the PDF.

Step 1: F(1.5). 1.5 lies in [0, 2], so F(1.5) = 1.5/2 = 0.75.

Step 2: F(0.5). F(0.5) = 0.5/2 = 0.25.

Step 3: Interval. P(0.5 ≤ X ≤ 1.5) = F(1.5) − F(0.5) = 0.75 − 0.25 = 0.5, the same answer as the integral.

The discrete case

For a discrete variable, the CDF is a staircase. It jumps at each value by the probability of that value. For a fair die, F(3) = P(X = 1) + P(X = 2) + P(X = 3) = 3/6 = 1/2. Between whole numbers, F stays flat.

def F(x):
    # CDF of the uniform distribution on [0, 2]
    if x < 0:
        return 0.0
    if x > 2:
        return 1.0
    return x / 2

print("F(1.5) =", F(1.5))
print("F(0.5) =", F(0.5))
print("P(0.5 <= X <= 1.5) =", F(1.5) - F(0.5))

# check the CDF by a Riemann sum of the PDF up to x = 1.5
steps = 100000
dx = 1.5 / steps
area = sum(0.5 * dx for _ in range(steps))
print("Area under PDF from 0 to 1.5 =", round(area, 4))
F(1.5) = 0.75
F(0.5) = 0.25
P(0.5 <= X <= 1.5) = 0.5
Area under PDF from 0 to 1.5 = 0.75

Key ideaThe PDF is the slope of the CDF, and the CDF is the area under the PDF. Going between them is the same as differentiating or integrating.

GATE loves thisWhen a question gives F(x) and asks for a PDF, differentiate. When it gives a PDF and asks for a probability, integrate or use the CDF.

If F(x) = x² on [0, 1], what is f(x)?

f(x) = F′(x) = 2x for 0 ≤ x ≤ 1.

Why must the CDF never decrease?

Because F(x) is the probability of being at most x. A larger x covers more outcomes, so the probability cannot shrink.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original, written in GATE style, and are not past exam questions. Try each one before reading the answer.

Errors at a fixed level

A test is run at α = 0.05 on 1000 cases where the null hypothesis is actually true. About how many cases are expected to be rejected by mistake?

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The expected number of Type I errors.
  2. Which idea applies. A Type I error has probability α when H0 is true.
  3. Work it out. 0.05 × 1000 = 50.
  4. Check. A smaller α would give fewer false alarms, so the answer should be a small fraction of 1000. 50 is 5 percent, which matches α.

Answer: about 50.

Finding the scale constant

Let f(x) = kx for 0 ≤ x ≤ 2 and f(x) = 0 otherwise. Find k so that f is a valid PDF.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The constant k that makes the total area 1.
  2. Which idea applies. ∫ f(x) dx over all x must equal 1.
  3. Work it out. ∫ from 0 to 2 of kx dx = k × [x²/2] from 0 to 2 = k × 2. Setting 2k = 1 gives k = 1/2.
  4. Check. With k = 1/2 the density is never negative on [0, 2], as required.

Answer: k = 1/2.

Which statements about p-values are correct?

A test returns p = 0.03. Select all statements that are correct.

(A) The probability that H0 is true is 0.03.
(B) H0 is rejected at α = 0.05.
(C) H0 is not rejected at α = 0.01.
(D) The probability of a Type I error is 0.03.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Which claims about p = 0.03 are true.
  2. Which idea applies. Decision rule: reject when p ≤ α. A p-value is not the probability of H0. Type I error probability is the fixed α.
  3. Work it out. A: false, p is not P(H0). B: 0.03 is at most 0.05, so reject, true. C: 0.03 is more than 0.01, so not rejected, true. D: the Type I error probability is α, not the p-value, so false.
  4. Check. Both B and C come from comparing 0.03 with the two levels.

Answer: (B) and (C).

A NAT question on a two-sided test

A sample of n = 36 has mean 52. The population σ is 6. Test H0: μ = 50 against H1: μ ≠ 50. Find the two-sided p-value, correct to 4 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A two-sided p-value to 4 decimal places.
  2. Which idea applies. Z = (x̄ − μ0)/(σ/√n), then p = 2(1 − Φ(|Z|)).
  3. Work it out. σ/√n = 6/6 = 1. Z = (52 − 50)/1 = 2. p = 2(1 − Φ(2)). With Φ(2) = 0.97725 (more precise than the common table value 0.9772), 1 − Φ(2) = 0.02275 and p = 0.0455.
  4. Check. A Z of 2 sits near the usual 5 percent boundary for two-sided tests, so a p-value near 0.05 is expected.

Answer: 0.0455.

Probability on an interval

X is uniform on [2, 5], with PDF f(x) = 1/3 on that interval. Find P(3 ≤ X ≤ 4) as a fraction.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The probability of an interval inside the support.
  2. Which idea applies. The probability is the area under the PDF over [3, 4].
  3. Work it out. Area = (1/3) × (4 − 3) = 1/3.
  4. Check. The interval length is 1 out of a total length 3, and each unit of length carries probability 1/3, which gives 1/3.

Answer: 1/3.

From a PDF to a probability

X has density f(x) = 3x² for 0 ≤ x ≤ 1, and zero otherwise. Find P(X > 0.5) as a fraction.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The upper-tail probability above 0.5.
  2. Which idea applies. Use the CDF: P(X > 0.5) = 1 − F(0.5).
  3. Work it out. F(x) = ∫ from 0 to x of 3t² dt = x³ for 0 ≤ x ≤ 1. So F(0.5) = (1/2)³ = 1/8. Then P(X > 0.5) = 1 − 1/8 = 7/8.
  4. Check. F(1) = 1, which confirms the density is valid. Most of the mass sits near 1 because the density grows as x² grows, so 7/8 is plausible.

Answer: 7/8.

A one-sided test

A sample of n = 25 has mean 53. The population σ is 10. Test H0: μ = 50 against H1: μ > 50 at α = 0.05. Find the one-sided p-value to 4 decimal places and state the decision.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. A one-sided p-value and a decision.
  2. Which idea applies. Z = (x̄ − μ0)/(σ/√n), then p = 1 − Φ(Z) because H1 only looks upward.
  3. Work it out. σ/√n = 10/5 = 2. Z = (53 − 50)/2 = 1.5. p = 1 − Φ(1.5) = 1 − 0.9332 = 0.0668. Since 0.0668 is greater than 0.05, do not reject.
  4. Check. A two-sided p-value would be 0.1336, double this. The one-sided test is less strict in the direction it tests, so 0.0668 is the right number here.

Answer: p = 0.0668, so H0 is not rejected at α = 0.05.

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: From PDF to a probability (NAT, 2 marks)

X has PDF f(x) = cx² on 0 ≤ x ≤ 3 and 0 elsewhere. Find c and then P(X > 2), correct to 3 decimal places.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The normalising constant and a tail probability.
  2. Which idea applies. The PDF integrates to 1. Then P(X > 2) = 1 − F(2).
  3. Work it out. ∫03 cx² dx = 9c = 1, so c = 1/9. F(x) = x³/27. P(X > 2) = 1 − 8/27 = 19/27 ≈ 0.704.
  4. Check. The code integrates numerically.
c = 1 / 9
n = 100000
h = 1 / n
print("%.3f" % sum(c * (2 + (k + 0.5) * h) ** 2 * h for k in range(n)))

0.704

Answer: c = 1/9 and P(X > 2) = 19/27 ≈ 0.704.

Exam-level 2: Type I and type II errors (MSQ, 1 mark)

Which are TRUE? (A) The significance level α is P(reject H0 | H0 true). (B) Power = 1 − P(type II error). (C) Lowering α with a fixed sample size usually raises the type II error rate. (D) A p value is the probability that H0 is true.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Definitions used in hypothesis testing.
  2. Which idea applies. Error rates are conditional on which hypothesis is true.
  3. Work it out. (A) True. (B) True. (C) A stricter rejection region rejects less often in both cases. True. (D) The p value assumes H0 and measures how extreme the data are. False.
  4. Check. (D) is the most common misreading of a p value.

Answer: (A), (B) and (C).

Exam-level 3: z test decision (NAT, 2 marks)

A machine should fill 500 g. The fill weight has σ = 4 g. A sample of 16 bags has mean 502.5 g. Compute the z statistic for H0: μ = 500.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. z = (x̄ − μ0)/(σ/√n).
  2. Which idea applies. The standard error of the mean is σ/√n.
  3. Work it out. σ/√n = 4/4 = 1. z = 2.5. At α = 0.05 two sided (critical 1.96), we reject H0.
  4. Check. 2.5 is larger than 1.96.

Answer: z = 2.5 (reject at the 5% level).

Summary

Null hypothesis
The claim assumed true until the data say otherwise.
Alternative hypothesis
What is accepted if the claim looks wrong, either two-sided or one-sided.
Z statistic
(x̄ − μ0)/(σ/√n) for a mean with known σ.
p-value
The probability, assuming H0, of a result at least as extreme as the one seen.
Significance level
The chosen α, the largest acceptable Type I error probability.
Decision rule
Reject H0 when p ≤ α.
Type I error
Rejecting a true H0, with probability α.
Type II error
Failing to reject a false H0, with probability β.
Power
1 − β, the chance of correctly rejecting a false H0.
PDF
A non-negative function f(x) whose total area is 1. Probabilities are areas.
CDF
F(x) = P(X ≤ x), non-decreasing from 0 to 1.
Link between PDF and CDF
F(x) is the integral of f, and f(x) is the derivative of F.
Interval probability
P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = F(b) − F(a) for a continuous variable.
Standard error
σ/√n, the spread of the sample mean.

Remember these

  1. A p-value is computed assuming H0 is true. It is not the probability that H0 is true.
  2. Choose one-sided or two-sided before seeing the data.
  3. Reject when p is at most α. Otherwise, do not reject.
  4. Type I error probability is α. Lowering α raises the chance of a Type II error.
  5. A valid PDF is never negative and has total area 1.
  6. For a continuous variable, P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = F(b) − F(a).

Stress and strain, elastic constants, Poisson's ratio

Stress is the force inside a material, strain is how much it stretches or shrinks, and the elastic constants (Young's modulus, shear modulus, bulk modulus and Poisson's ratio) link the two. These ideas are the foundation of every strength question in GATE Robotics and Automation, so this page builds them slowly from zero.

The idea in plain words

Welcome. This page is about what happens inside a solid when you load it. Pull on a rubber band and it stretches. Pull harder and it stretches more. Let go, and it returns. A steel bar behaves in a similar way, but it stretches much less, and it needs a far larger pull. Engineers need a way to describe this behaviour with numbers, so that they can design a bridge, a crane or a robot arm that will not stretch too far or break. The numbers are the stress, the strain and the elastic constants.

Here is a simple picture. Imagine a spring made of steel, hung from a ceiling, with a bucket at the bottom. Put in one stone, and the spring stretches a little. Put in two stones, and it stretches twice as much. Take the stones out, and the spring goes back to its old length. This is the idea of elasticity: the material returns to its shape when the load is removed. Stress is the load per unit area inside the material, and strain is the stretch per unit length. The ratio between them is the stiffness of the material, and this stiffness is an elastic constant.

Why does this matter for GATE? Almost every mechanics question starts with a stress or a strain and then uses an elastic constant. A question might ask for the stretch of a wire, the change in diameter of a rod, or the value of the shear modulus from two given numbers. In GATE Robotics and Automation (RA), these ideas appear in the mechanics of materials section, and they are also needed for the later topics of bending, torsion and deflection. Learn the definitions carefully, because the numbers in these questions are often small and simple once the definitions are clear.

We will go slowly. Each section defines one idea, uses an everyday picture, and then works a small example with every step shown.

Key terms

Read this list now and come back to it when a word feels new.

Force
A push or a pull, measured in newtons (N). Plain meaning: how hard something is pushed or pulled.
Stress (sigma, tau)
Force divided by the area it acts on. Plain meaning: how hard the material is loaded inside, per square millimetre. Precise definition: sigma = F/A for a normal stress, tau = F/A for a shear stress, in pascals (Pa) or megapascals (MPa).
Normal stress (sigma)
Stress that acts straight across the area. Tension is positive and compression is negative.
Shear stress (tau)
Stress that acts along the area, trying to slide one part past another.
Strain (epsilon)
The change in length divided by the original length. Plain meaning: the fraction by which the length changed. It has no unit, because it is a ratio.
Normal strain (epsilon)
Strain along the direction of the force: epsilon = delta L / L.
Shear strain (gamma)
The change in a right angle, in radians. Plain meaning: how much a square leans over.
Lateral strain
Strain at right angles to the force. For a stretched bar, it is the strain across the width.
Elastic behaviour
The material returns to its original shape after the load is removed.
Hooke's law
Within the elastic range, stress is proportional to strain. Plain meaning: double the load, double the stretch.
Young's modulus (E)
The ratio of normal stress to normal strain in the elastic range. Plain meaning: how stiff the material is when stretched.
Shear modulus (G)
The ratio of shear stress to shear strain in the elastic range. Plain meaning: how stiff the material is when sheared.
Bulk modulus (K)
The ratio of hydrostatic pressure to volumetric strain. Plain meaning: how hard the material is to squeeze on all sides.
Poisson's ratio (nu)
The ratio of lateral strain to axial strain, with a minus sign. Plain meaning: how much a material gets thinner when it is stretched.
Proportional limit
The largest stress for which stress and strain stay in a straight line.
Elastic limit
The largest stress after which the material is left with a permanent change when unloaded.
Yield point (yield stress)
The stress at which the material starts to deform permanently, with only a small increase in load.
Ultimate stress (ultimate tensile strength)
The largest stress on the stress-strain diagram.
Permanent set
The strain left in the material after the load is removed.

Normal stress and normal strain

Stress: force shared over an area

Why do we divide the force by the area? Think of standing on snow. If you stand on one foot with a small boot, you sink. If you stand on snowshoes, you do not sink, even though you weigh the same. The force is the same, but the area is larger, so the pressure is smaller. Stress works in the same way. A rope that holds a load on a thick cable is less stressed than a thin thread holding the same load.

sigma = F / A

Here sigma is the normal stress, F is the force in newtons, and A is the cross-sectional area in square millimetres. The unit is newtons per square millimetre, which is written N/mm2. This is the same as megapascals (MPa), so a stress of 150 N/mm2 is 150 MPa.

Worked example. A bar has a square section of 10 mm by 10 mm, so the area is A = 10 x 10 = 100 mm2. It is pulled with a force of F = 2000 N. The stress is sigma = 2000/100 = 20 N/mm2, which is 20 MPa. It is tension, because the bar is being pulled, so the normal stress is positive.

Strain: stretch divided by the original length

Strain tells us how much the material has changed, as a fraction of its original size. Suppose a rubber band of length 10 cm stretches to 11 cm. It has stretched by 1 cm. The strain is the stretch divided by the original length, which is 1/10 = 0.1. Strain is a pure ratio, so it has no unit. Engineers often write very small strains as micro-strain, which is one millionth, so 0.0005 is 500 micro-strain.

epsilon = delta L / L

Here epsilon is the normal strain, delta L (read "delta L") is the change in length, and L is the original length. The change delta L is positive for stretching and negative for shortening.

Worked example. A steel wire is 500 mm long. Under load it stretches by 0.25 mm. The strain is epsilon = 0.25/500 = 0.0005. This is 500 micro-strain. Notice that the strain has no unit, because mm divided by mm cancels.

TrapDo not use the stretched length as the denominator. The strain uses the original length. A common mistake is to divide by 500.25 instead of 500.

A bar of area 50 mm2 carries a tensile force of 4000 N. What is its normal stress?

sigma = F/A = 4000/50 = 80 N/mm2, which is 80 MPa, positive because it is tension.

A bar of original length 1000 mm is shortened by 0.5 mm under compression. What is its normal strain?

The change in length is -0.5 mm, so epsilon = -0.5/1000 = -0.0005. The strain is negative because the bar is shortened.

Shear stress and shear strain

Sliding instead of pulling

Think of a pack of cards. If you push the top card sideways while holding the bottom card still, the cards slide. The force is sideways, not straight through the pack. This is shear. Scissors cut paper by shear, and a bolt in a joint can fail by shear.

tau = F / A

Here tau is the shear stress, F is the force that acts parallel to the area, and A is the area that the force slides across. The formula looks the same as the normal stress, but the direction is different. The force runs along the face, not across it.

Shear strain gamma (gamma) is the change in a right angle. If a square of material is pushed into a parallelogram, the sides stay the same length, but the right angles change. The angle through which a right angle changes is the shear strain, in radians. For small angles, gamma is approximately equal to the sideways movement divided by the height of the block.

Before:              After shear:

  +-----------+          /----------/
  |           |         /          /
  |           |  ->    /          /
  |           |       /          /
  +-----------+        /----------/

  The sides stay the same length.
  The right angles change by gamma.

Worked example. A block of area 100 mm2 has a shear force of 10000 N. The shear stress is tau = 10000/100 = 100 MPa. If the block is made of a material with shear modulus G = 80000 MPa, the shear strain is gamma = tau/G = 100/80000 = 0.00125 radians. This small value is a tiny change of angle, but it matters a great deal in a machine.

TipShear stress and shear strain are linked by the shear modulus, just as normal stress and normal strain are linked by Young's modulus. Learn the pairs together.

What is the shear stress in a pin of area 200 mm2 that carries a shear force of 16000 N?

tau = 16000/200 = 80 MPa.

Is shear strain a length ratio or an angle?

It is a change in angle, measured in radians. It is a small angle in most engineering problems.

Hooke's law and Young's modulus

The straight line of elasticity

Robert Hooke noticed that a spring stretches by an amount proportional to the load it carries, as long as the spring is not overloaded. Engineers use the same rule for most solid materials, within a range of small strains. This is called Hooke's law. In words: stress is proportional to strain.

sigma = E x epsilon

Here sigma is the normal stress, epsilon is the normal strain, and E is Young's modulus. E is the constant of proportionality. A large E means the material is stiff, because a large stress is needed to produce a small strain. Young's modulus is written in the same units as stress, usually GPa (gigapascals). One GPa equals 1000 MPa.

Rearranging gives the strain for a given stress: epsilon = sigma/E. Also, from the definitions, the stretch of a bar is delta L = sigma L/E = F L/(A E). This is the formula you will use in most elongation questions.

delta L = F L / (A E)

Worked example. A steel bar of length 2000 mm and area 314 mm2 carries a tensile force of 50000 N. Take E = 200 GPa = 200000 MPa. The stress is 50000/314 = 159.2 MPa. The strain is 159.2/200000 = 0.000796. The elongation is 0.000796 x 2000 = 1.59 mm. The bar gets 1.59 mm longer.

Key ideaHooke's law is valid only in the elastic range, up to the proportional limit. Beyond that, the straight line is lost, and the answer from E is no longer correct.

TrapUnits must match. If the force is in newtons and the area in mm2, the stress is in MPa, so E must be in MPa too. A value of 200 GPa must be written as 200000 MPa before you divide.

A wire has E = 100 GPa and stress 50 MPa. What is its strain?

epsilon = sigma/E = 50/100000 = 0.0005. (Both values are converted to MPa first: 100 GPa = 100000 MPa.)

Does a material with a larger Young's modulus stretch more or less under the same stress?

Less. A larger E means a smaller strain for the same stress, because the material is stiffer.

Poisson's ratio and lateral strain

Getting thinner when stretched

Stretch a rubber band and it becomes thinner. Stretch a bar of steel and it also becomes thinner, though much less. This change across the width is the lateral strain. It happens because the material is pulled along one direction and squeezed at right angles, like a pulled plasticine sausage.

Simeon Poisson gave the ratio of these two strains a name. Poisson's ratio (nu, the Greek letter) is the lateral strain divided by the axial strain, with a minus sign so that it is positive for most materials:

nu = - (lateral strain) / (axial strain)

The minus sign is needed because the two strains have opposite signs. When the bar is stretched, the axial strain is positive and the lateral strain is negative. Dividing one by the other gives a negative number, and the minus sign turns it positive. For most engineering materials, nu lies between 0 and 0.5. Steel is about 0.3, and rubber is close to 0.5.

Worked example. A bar of diameter 20 mm is stretched with an axial strain of 0.0008. Take nu = 0.3. The lateral strain is -nu x axial strain = -0.3 x 0.0008 = -0.00024. The change in diameter is -0.00024 x 20 = -0.0048 mm. The bar becomes slightly thinner. The Python check below gives the same answer with more care.

import math
F = 50000.0
d = 20.0
L = 2000.0
E = 200000.0
nu = 0.3
A = math.pi * d ** 2 / 4
sigma = F / A
eps = sigma / E
print("area =", round(A, 2), "mm^2")
print("stress =", round(sigma, 2), "MPa")
print("elongation =", round(eps * L, 3), "mm")
print("diameter change =", round(-nu * eps * d, 5), "mm")
area = 314.16 mm^2
stress = 159.15 MPa
elongation = 1.592 mm
diameter change = -0.00477 mm

GATE loves thisPoisson's ratio is almost always given with the axial strain. Remember the minus sign. If a question gives a lateral strain of -0.0003 and an axial strain of 0.001, then nu = -(-0.0003)/0.001 = 0.3.

A rod is stretched, so its axial strain is +0.002. Poisson's ratio is 0.25. What is the lateral strain?

Lateral strain = -nu x axial strain = -0.25 x 0.002 = -0.0005. The rod is thinner by that fraction.

Can Poisson's ratio be negative for a normal material?

For most engineering materials, no. Typical values are between 0 and 0.5. (A few special materials called auxetics have negative values, but they are outside the usual GATE scope.)

Shear modulus, bulk modulus and the relations between them

Shear modulus

Shear modulus G is the shear version of Young's modulus. It is the ratio of shear stress to shear strain, in the elastic range:

G = tau / gamma

A large G means the material resists sliding. Steel has a shear modulus of about 80 GPa, which is less than its Young's modulus of about 200 GPa. This is not a coincidence. For a normal, isotropic solid, the three elastic constants are linked by formulas that you must know for GATE.

Bulk modulus

Bulk modulus K describes how hard it is to squeeze a material on all sides at once, like a deep-sea diver pressed by the water. It is the ratio of the hydrostatic pressure p to the volumetric strain, which is the fractional change in volume:

K = p / (volumetric strain)

The volumetric strain is delta V / V, the change in volume divided by the original volume. Because a squeeze reduces the volume, the volumetric strain is negative, and the formula is often written with a minus sign so that K is positive. In GATE questions, the sign is usually clear from the context.

The relations between the four constants

For an isotropic, linear elastic material, the four constants E, G, K and nu are linked by these relations:

E = 2G(1 + nu) = 3K(1 - 2nu)

Here E is Young's modulus, G is the shear modulus, K is the bulk modulus and nu is Poisson's ratio. Because of these links, if you know any two of the constants, you can find the other two. This is a very common GATE pattern: give two constants and ask for a third.

Worked example. Let E = 200 GPa and nu = 0.3. From E = 2G(1 + nu), G = E/(2(1 + nu)) = 200/2.6 = 76.92 GPa. From E = 3K(1 - 2nu), K = E/(3(1 - 2nu)) = 200/1.2 = 166.67 GPa. The Python check below confirms that both relations give back E = 200.

E = 200.0
nu = 0.3
G = E / (2 * (1 + nu))
K = E / (3 * (1 - 2 * nu))
print("G =", round(G, 2), "GPa")
print("K =", round(K, 2), "GPa")
print("check 2G(1+nu) =", round(2 * G * (1 + nu), 2))
print("check 3K(1-2nu) =", round(3 * K * (1 - 2 * nu), 2))
G = 76.92 GPa
K = 166.67 GPa
check 2G(1+nu) = 200.0
check 3K(1-2nu) = 200.0

TrapThe relation E = 3K(1 - 2nu) needs nu less than 0.5. When nu is exactly 0.5, the bulk modulus is infinite, which means the material cannot change its volume at all, like an ideal rubber.

TipWhen a question gives E and nu, and asks for G, use G = E/(2(1 + nu)) directly. Do not guess a formula from memory of a different text: the relation above is the one to use.

Given E = 100 GPa and nu = 0.25, what is G?

G = E/(2(1 + nu)) = 100/(2 x 1.25) = 100/2.5 = 40 GPa.

Why is G smaller than E for an ordinary solid?

Because G = E/(2(1 + nu)), and the factor 2(1 + nu) is larger than 2 when nu is positive. For nu = 0.3, the factor is 2.6, so G is about 0.38 times E.

The stress-strain diagram

Drawing the test

To learn how a material behaves, engineers pull a standard test piece in a testing machine. The machine records the force and the stretch. Dividing the force by the original area gives the stress, and dividing the stretch by the original length gives the strain. Plotting stress on the vertical axis and strain on the horizontal axis gives the stress-strain diagram. Its shape tells us almost everything about the material.

Stress
  ^
  |                     ultimate stress
  |                   .---.
  |                 /       \  necking
  |   yield point  o         \   fracture x
  |              /  .--------/
  |            /  /
  |  elastic  /  /
  |  limit   /  /   straight line
  |  o------/--/   (Hooke's law region)
  |        /
  +--------------------------------------> Strain

The diagram has four important features, in order of increasing load:

  1. The straight line. From the origin up to the proportional limit, stress and strain are proportional. The slope of this line is Young's modulus E.
  2. The elastic limit. Up to this stress, the material returns to its original shape when unloaded. Beyond it, some permanent change remains.
  3. The yield point. For ductile metals such as mild steel, the stress reaches a level where the strain grows quickly with very little extra load. This is the start of permanent deformation.
  4. The ultimate stress. The largest stress on the diagram. After this point, the material forms a local neck and the stress falls until fracture.

Permanent set

Suppose you load a bar past its elastic limit and then unload it. The unloading line is parallel to the original straight line, and the bar does not return to zero strain. The strain left behind is the permanent set. This is why a paper clip bent too far stays bent.

Key ideaWithin the elastic range, every formula in this page works and the material returns to its shape. Beyond the yield point, the material is permanently changed, and the simple formulas no longer describe it.

GATE loves thisA typical question asks which points are on the stress-strain diagram and what they mean. Remember: Hooke's law holds only up to the proportional limit, the elastic limit is where permanent set starts, and the ultimate stress is the largest stress on the curve.

What is the slope of the straight part of the stress-strain diagram?

Young's modulus E, because E = stress/strain in the straight, elastic region.

Is the yield point the same as the ultimate stress?

No. The yield point is where permanent deformation starts. The ultimate stress is the largest stress on the whole curve, and it is reached later.

Solved GATE-style problems

These problems are original, written in the style of GATE. They are not real past questions. Work each one on paper before you read the solution.

Normal stress in a rod

A round rod of area 100 mm2 carries a tensile force of 2000 N. Find the normal stress in MPa. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The normal stress in MPa.
  2. Which idea applies. sigma = F/A.
  3. Work it out. sigma = 2000/100 = 20 N/mm2, which is 20 MPa.
  4. Check. 20 MPa times 100 mm2 gives 2000 N, which is the force. The check works.

Answer: 20 MPa.

Strain of a stretched wire

A wire of original length 500 mm stretches by 0.25 mm under load. Find the normal strain. Give a single number as a decimal.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The strain, which is a ratio.
  2. Which idea applies. epsilon = delta L / L, using the original length.
  3. Work it out. epsilon = 0.25/500 = 0.0005.
  4. Check. Multiply back: 0.0005 times 500 = 0.25 mm. The check works, and the unit cancels.

Answer: 0.0005.

Shear modulus from two constants (NAT)

A material has Young's modulus E = 200 GPa and Poisson's ratio nu = 0.25. Find the shear modulus G in GPa. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. G, given E and nu.
  2. Which idea applies. E = 2G(1 + nu), so G = E/(2(1 + nu)).
  3. Work it out. G = 200/(2 x 1.25) = 200/2.5 = 80 GPa.
  4. Check. Put it back: 2 x 80 x 1.25 = 200. The check works.

Answer: 80 GPa.

Which statements are correct? (MSQ)

Which of the following statements about elastic constants of an isotropic material are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) Poisson's ratio is the negative of the ratio of lateral strain to axial strain
(B) The shear modulus is always larger than Young's modulus
(C) The bulk modulus can be found as K = E/(3(1 - 2nu))
(D) The bulk modulus is zero when nu = 0.5

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Every correct statement.
  2. Which idea applies. The definitions and the relations E = 2G(1 + nu) = 3K(1 - 2nu).
  3. Work it out. (A) is the definition of Poisson's ratio, so it is correct. (B) is wrong, since G = E/(2(1 + nu)) is smaller than E/2. (C) is the rearranged relation, so it is correct. (D) is wrong: at nu = 0.5 the denominator 1 - 2nu is zero, so K is infinite, not zero.
  4. Check. Two statements are correct and the two wrong ones each break a formula above.

Answer: (A) and (C).

Stretch of a bar (NAT)

A bar of length 1000 mm and area 200 mm2 carries a tensile force of 10000 N. Take E = 100 GPa. Find the elongation of the bar in mm. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. delta L in mm.
  2. Which idea applies. delta L = F L/(A E), with E in MPa.
  3. Work it out. E = 100 GPa = 100000 MPa. sigma = 10000/200 = 50 MPa. epsilon = 50/100000 = 0.0005. delta L = 0.0005 x 1000 = 0.5 mm.
  4. Check. F L/(A E) = 10000 x 1000/(200 x 100000) = 10000000/20000000 = 0.5 mm. Both routes agree.

Answer: 0.5 mm.

Change in diameter (NAT)

A round bar of diameter 20 mm carries a tensile stress of 159.15 MPa. Take E = 200 GPa and nu = 0.3. Find the magnitude of the change in diameter, in mm, to five decimal places. Give a single number.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The size of the diameter change in mm.
  2. Which idea applies. Axial strain = sigma/E. Lateral strain = -nu x axial strain. Change in diameter = lateral strain x original diameter.
  3. Work it out. Axial strain = 159.15/200000 = 0.00079575. Lateral strain = -0.3 x 0.00079575 = -0.00023873. Change in diameter = -0.00023873 x 20 = -0.0047746 mm. The magnitude is 0.00477 mm to five decimal places. The Python check in the chapter gives the same value.
  4. Check. The bar gets thinner, so the sign is negative, and the size is small compared with the diameter, as expected for a strain of about 0.0008.
sigma = 159.15
E = 200000.0
nu = 0.3
d = 20.0
axial = sigma / E
lateral = -nu * axial
print("axial strain:", round(axial, 8))
print("change in diameter:", round(lateral * d, 5), "mm")
axial strain: 0.00079575
change in diameter: -0.00477 mm

Answer: 0.00477 mm (the diameter shrinks, so the change is -0.00477 mm).

Points on the stress-strain diagram (MSQ)

For a ductile metal tested in tension, which of the following statements are correct? Select all that apply.

(A) The elastic limit is the largest stress that leaves no permanent set after unloading
(B) For mild steel, the yield point shows a sharp drop and then a nearly flat region
(C) The ultimate stress is the largest stress on the stress-strain curve
(D) Hooke's law holds beyond the proportional limit

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Every correct statement about the diagram.
  2. Which idea applies. The meaning of each special point, and the range where Hooke's law holds.
  3. Work it out. (A) is the definition of the elastic limit, so it is correct. (B) describes the yield behaviour of mild steel, so it is correct. (C) is the definition of the ultimate stress, so it is correct. (D) is wrong, because Hooke's law holds only up to the proportional limit.
  4. Check. Three correct statements, and the one wrong statement is the one that goes beyond the straight line.

Answer: (A), (B) and (C).

Exam-level practice

Exam-level 1: Bulk modulus from E and ν (NAT, 2 marks)

A material has E = 210 GPa and ν = 0.3. Find G and K in GPa.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. The other two elastic constants.
  2. Which idea applies. G = E/(2(1 + ν)), K = E/(3(1 − 2ν)).
  3. Work it out. G = 210/2.6 = 80.77 GPa. K = 210/1.2 = 175 GPa.
  4. Check. The code computes both.
E, v = 210, 0.3
print("G = %.2f, K = %.2f" % (E / (2 * (1 + v)), E / (3 * (1 - 2 * v))))

G = 80.77, K = 175.00

Answer: G ≈ 80.77 GPa and K = 175 GPa.

Exam-level 2: Elongation of a stepped bar (NAT, 2 marks)

A steel bar (E = 200 GPa) has two segments: 1 m of area 500 mm² and 0.5 m of area 250 mm². It carries 50 kN tension. Find the total elongation in mm.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. Sum of PL/(AE).
  2. Which idea applies. Each segment carries the full load.
  3. Work it out. 50000 · 1/(500e−6 · 200e9) = 0.5 mm. 50000 · 0.5/(250e−6 · 200e9) = 0.5 mm. Total 1 mm.
  4. Check. The code computes it.
P, E = 50e3, 200e9
print("%.3f mm" % (1000 * (P * 1 / (500e-6 * E) + P * 0.5 / (250e-6 * E))))

1.000 mm

Answer: 1 mm.

Exam-level 3: Volume change (NAT, 2 marks)

A bar under uniaxial strain ε = 0.001 has ν = 0.25. Find the volumetric strain.

How to think about it

  1. What is asked. εv = ε(1 − 2ν).
  2. Which idea applies. Lateral strains are −νε each.
  3. Work it out. 0.001 · 0.5 = 0.0005.
  4. Check. For ν = 0.5 the material is incompressible.

Answer: 0.0005.

Remember these

  1. Stress is force over area. Strain is change over original length.
  2. Hooke's law holds only up to the proportional limit.
  3. Use the original length and the original area in every formula.
  4. Convert GPa to MPa (multiply by 1000) before you divide by a stress.
  5. Lateral strain is minus nu times axial strain.
  6. E = 2G(1 + nu) = 3K(1 - 2nu). Two constants give the others.
